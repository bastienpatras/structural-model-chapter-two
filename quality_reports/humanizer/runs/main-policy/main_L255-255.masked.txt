A uniform national subsidy is therefore poorly targeted because the cost-sharing requirement varies across markets, although {{M001}} is an incentive-compatibility object and not a welfare optimum (Remark{{M025}}{{M026}}). A flat per-m{{M029}} grant set at the cross-market mean would over-pay markets where the private incentive already suffices and under-pay those where the gap is largest, the first through fiscal waste and the second through forgone renovations. The dispersion is systematic: the cost-sharing ratio is low where {{M004}} is high, and {{M005}} is high where local prices are high and the energy-efficiency premium {{M006}} is large. The premium reflects the capitalised value of verified quality to sensitive buyers, not a freestanding measure of how many buyers are sensitive (Appendix{{M030}}{{M031}} shows the two are not identified apart in price data alone). An efficient programme would differentiate the subsidy along these axes and co-target audit-infrastructure investment on markets where the threshold is large but verification is scarce, since {{M007}} does not change the threshold but scales the population it applies to (Section{{M032}}{{M033}}).
