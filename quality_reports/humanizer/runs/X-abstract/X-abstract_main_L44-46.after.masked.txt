Standard unraveling theory predicts that mandatory disclosure separates high-quality from low-quality products: silence signals bad news and non-disclosers should trade at-or-below the worst disclosed category. We document the opposite. Following France's 2021 enforcement of Energy Performance Certificate disclosure, properties listed without a certified score trade at a premium of 6.9 percent above disclosed low-efficiency properties for apartments and 3.4 percent for houses. The puzzle resolves because non-disclosure pools informed sellers strategically concealing unfavorable scores with uninformed sellers who genuinely lack a certified audit. Unable to distinguish between them, buyers treat silence as ambiguous rather than damning. We build and structurally estimate a model of the disclosure game, bringing it to the data through a variable unobservable from the buyer's side: private listing data from SeLoger, France's leading residential platform, matched to the ADEME registry of certified energy audits, give us direct observation of each seller's information status at listing time. We derive a closed-form expression for the non-disclosure premium that, in the partial-pooling equilibrium, collapses in theory to the structural concealment cost, invariant to market composition; we test this restriction directly and find it holds for houses but is rejected for apartments, a divergence we trace to a measurable over-identifying wedge, reported directly rather than resolved by construction. An extension in which buyers attend less to energy on listings that show no rating reconciles the two segments with one attention parameter per segment, rejecting full attention for apartments but not for houses. Raising attention to silence changes who hides but leaves the minimum renovation subsidy unchanged. {{M001}}
 We extend the same framework to endogenous renovation, deriving a local minimum incentive-compatible subsidy and showing that renovation subsidies, enforcement against hiding, and audit-infrastructure investment act on distinct margins. We conclude by showing that the required cost-share varies widely across French housing markets, so that a uniform national programme overpays some markets and underpays others.
