When Silence Pays: Mandatory Disclosure and Strategic Concealment
in Housing Markets
Bastien Patras, bastien.patras@sciencespo.fr†
†
    PhD Student, Sciences Po Department of Economics, Paris, France


      Abstract
      Standard unraveling theory predicts that mandatory disclosure separates high-quality from low-quality prod-
      ucts: silence signals bad news and non-disclosers should trade at-or-below the worst disclosed category. We
      document the opposite. Following France’s 2021 enforcement of Energy Performance Certificate disclosure,
      properties listed without a certified score trade at a premium of 6.9 percent above disclosed low-efficiency
      properties for apartments and 3.4 percent for houses. The puzzle resolves because non-disclosure pools
      informed sellers strategically concealing unfavorable scores with uninformed sellers who genuinely lack a
      certified audit. Unable to distinguish between them, buyers treat silence as ambiguous rather than damn-
      ing. We build and structurally estimate a model of the disclosure game, bringing it to the data through
      a variable unobservable from the buyer’s side: private listing data from SeLoger, France’s leading resi-
      dential platform, matched to the ADEME registry of certified energy audits, give us direct observation of
      each seller’s information status at listing time. We derive a closed-form expression for the non-disclosure
      premium that, in the partial-pooling equilibrium, collapses in theory to the structural concealment cost,
      invariant to market composition; we test this restriction directly and find it holds for houses but is rejected
      for apartments, a divergence we trace to a measurable over-identifying wedge, reported directly rather than
      resolved by construction. An extension in which buyers attend less to energy on listings that show no
      rating reconciles the two segments with one attention parameter per segment, rejecting full attention for
      apartments but not for houses. Attention changes who hides, not who renovates: raising attention to
      silence leaves the minimum renovation subsidy unchanged. We extend the same framework to endogenous
      renovation, deriving a local minimum incentive-compatible subsidy and showing that renovation subsidies,
      enforcement against hiding, and audit-infrastructure investment act on distinct margins. We conclude by
      showing that the required cost-share varies widely across French housing markets, so that a uniform national
      programme overpays some markets and underpays others.

1 Introduction
Mandatory disclosure is the standard regulatory response to asymmetric information about product quality. Yet
the case for mandating disclosure is contested, because theory suggests markets may not need it. When quality
is verifiable, the unraveling result of Grossman (1981) and Milgrom (1981) holds that sellers disclose voluntarily,
since buyers rationally read silence as the worst possible news. All but the very worst sellers therefore disclose, and
disclosure becomes effectively universal without a mandate. Whether this result describes real markets remains
unsettled. It rests on strong assumptions, and it is difficult to test, because in most settings where quality is
verifiable disclosure has long been compulsory.
To examine whether unraveling describes a market where quality is verifiable but silence is common, we study the
French residential housing market around the July 2021 enforcement of Energy Performance Certificate (EPC)
disclosure. Under a 2018 reform that took effect on July 1, 2021, a property’s diagnostic de performance
énergétique (DPE) became legally opposable.1 A seller who advertises an energy score is now bound by it, and

invoice-based calculation with the current physical-characteristics-based method) was set by the Arrêté du 31 mars 2021. The loi

buyers acquired legal recourse against misstatement. The reform changed what a disclosed score means without
changing the underlying housing stock or the legal consequences of remaining silent. It therefore isolates the
demand-side value of the signal, and it does so in a market where the certificate is issued and archived by a
national agency rather than by the seller or the listing platform.
Applied to this setting, unraveling makes a sharp prediction. Once the DPE is credible, sellers of energy-efficient
properties should publish their score to separate themselves; sellers of mediocre properties should then publish to
avoid being pooled with worse ones; and the process should continue until listings without a score are confined
to the very bottom of the quality distribution, priced at or below the worst disclosed grade. French housing is a
suitable testbed for this prediction because the disclosed attribute is a single certified index with a clear mapping
into a running cost buyers face directly, because the certificate is verifiable against an independent public registry,
and because a large stock of properties entered the post-reform market without one.
The unraveling prediction rests on four assumptions: (a) buyers know sellers possess private information about
quality, (b) disclosure is costless, (c) disclosure is credible, and (d) buyers are homogeneous in how much they
value the disclosed attribute. Three of the four are either directly satisfied in this setting or can be dispensed
with. Assumption (c) holds by construction after July 2021, since the DPE is issued by a certified third-party
diagnostician, archived in the registry of the Agence de la Transition Écologique (ADEME), and legally binding on
the seller. Assumption (b) is close to satisfied, because a DPE is a one-off outlay of roughly 100–250 e according
to ADEME’s own estimates, negligible relative to residential transaction values, and it is the seller’s obligation
rather than the buyer’s. Assumption (a), which the theoretical literature treats as a maintained hypothesis, we do
not need to assume at all. By matching listings to the ADEME registry we observe directly which sellers hold a
certificate at the moment of listing, and among the apartment listings we can match at the address level, 48.8%
hold none. Silence in this market is therefore not always a choice, a possibility first identified by Dye (1985) and
formalised by Jung and Kwon (1988), and here it is measured rather than inferred.
The descriptiveness of unraveling in this market accordingly hinges on assumption (d). Unlike disclosure costs and
disclosure credibility, which a large literature has examined, the homogeneity of buyer valuations has received little
attention, even though it sets the size of the disclosure incentive. A seller’s penalty for withholding is proportional
to the share of buyers who price the withheld attribute. Housing makes the assumption particularly implausible.
A buyer of a rarely-occupied second home in a mild climate may rationally ignore an energy score that a buyer of a
primary residence in a cold region prices directly, as may an investor facing the impending rental prohibition on the
worst-rated properties. Evidence across many regulated markets shows that disclosure interventions fail precisely
when recipients are inattentive or heterogeneous in their response (Loewenstein et al., 2014; Ben-Shahar and
Schneider, 2011), and Fishman and Hagerty (2003) show formally that mandated disclosure can be ineffective, or
welfare-reducing, when buyers differ in their capacity to use the disclosed information. We therefore let the share
of buyers who price the EPC vary across local markets and estimate it.
Testing a theory of strategic concealment requires observing both sides of the information asymmetry at once, the
seller’s true information, the seller’s disclosure choice and the market’s response. Most empirical work observes
only one or two. Studies of information asymmetry in housing infer agent advantages from equilibrium price
deviations (Levitt and Syverson, 2008); analyses of certification intermediaries characterise incentives without
observing individual information endowments (Lizzeri, 1999); and work on the energy-efficiency gap proxies for
seller awareness through indirect variation in energy prices (Myers, 2020). We observe all three. Listing records
from SeLoger, a major French residential platform, give the listing price, property characteristics, the seller’s
disclosure decision, and, when a score is displayed, the letter itself. The registry of the Agence de la Transition
Écologique (ADEME) is the official national database of certified DPE audits, reported by accredited third-party
assessors and compiled independently of the listing market. We match listings to the registry on address, property
type, area, and floor, which identifies whether a certified audit existed at the time of listing, the one thing buyers
can never observe. Because the match requires a geocoded address, the matched sample is the subset of listings
that can be located at the address level rather than the full listing universe. Within that matched sample, every
listing falls into one of four groups, namely truthful disclosers, strategic hiders who hold a certificate and omit
it, genuinely uninformed sellers for whom no certificate exists, and a small residual displaying a score without
a verified audit. The information structure of the disclosure game is therefore observed rather than inferred, a
vantage point that to our knowledge is unique in this literature. Transaction records enter separately and are not
matched to individual listings. We use them to estimate the mapping from listing to realised sale price, which

supports treating the listing price as the relevant outcome, since the negotiation margin in French residential sales
is small and most properties transact at or near list price (Merlo and Ortalo-Magné, 2004; Genesove and Mayer,
2001; Friggit, 2013).
The data yield four descriptive findings, drawn from two distinct samples that we keep separate throughout. First,
silence commands a premium rather than a penalty. Estimated on the panel of market-level hedonic price indices,
and relative to the pre-reform baseline, listings without a score trade approximately 6.9% above disclosed energy-
intensive properties for apartments and 3.4% for houses, the opposite of the sign unraveling predicts. Second, the
premium widened abruptly at the reform rather than emerging from trend. The composition-adjusted difference-
in-differences estimates evolve in parallel before July 2021, averaging +0.002 for apartments and +0.008 for
houses with no discernible drift, and then shift sharply upward, stabilising around +0.044 and +0.037 by mid-
2024. For apartments the level ordering already favoured non-disclosers beforehand, so what the reform changes
is the magnitude of the premium rather than its sign. Third, turning to the address-matched listing sample, non-
disclosure is overwhelmingly involuntary. Among apartment listings 79.8% disclose truthfully, 16.7% are genuinely
uninformed, and only 3.5% hold a certificate and withhold it, with the corresponding house shares 90.4%, 8.5%
and 1.1%. Fourth, the non-disclosure rate varies widely across local markets, ranging among apartments from
6.5% to 40.9% across départements, around a median of 16.5%. Together these facts are inconsistent with
unraveling as a description of this market, while pointing to the mechanism that defeats it. The pool of silent
sellers is dominated by the uninformed, which blunts the inference buyers can draw from silence and shelters the
few who conceal deliberately.
To recover the forces behind these patterns, we build and estimate a model of the disclosure game that allows
for, but does not impose, strategic concealment. Sellers differ in whether they hold a certified audit and in the
quality it would reveal; buyers differ in whether they price energy performance. Informed sellers of poor properties
choose between publishing a bad score and accepting the price buyers assign to silence, and that choice depends
on how contaminated the silent pool is by sellers who simply have no certificate. Because the composition of the
silent pool is itself the outcome of those choices, the model is closed by a fixed point in buyer beliefs, and it nests
the classical benchmark. When audit coverage and buyer sensitivity are both high, the silent pool empties and
the Grossman–Milgrom outcome is recovered. For intermediate values, which is where the French data sit, the
model predicts partial pooling in which some informed sellers conceal and silence carries a premium.
The estimates deliver two structural magnitudes and one specification test. Identifying buyer energy-sensitivity
separately from the concealment cost requires a source of variation external to prices, since the two are shown to
be identified only as their product by price moments alone (Appendix B); we construct one from the capitalised
energy-cost saving a sensitive buyer attaches to verified high- versus low-quality certification, built from ADEME-
certified consumption bands rather than from the price data itself. The resulting share of buyers who price
energy performance averages 49.5% across apartment markets and 45.8% across house markets, and the cost of
withholding a certificate settles at 2.4% of property value for apartments and 3.5% for houses, on the order of a
third of the notary bill a French buyer separately pays on the same transaction. Both are economically substantial
magnitudes, and of the same order in two segments that trade very differently. The model predicts, in the partial-
pooling equilibrium that describes the bulk of French housing markets, that the non-disclosure premium should
collapse to this concealment cost exactly, invariant to market composition; we test this prediction directly rather
than impose it. It holds for houses: the gap between the observed premium and the identified concealment cost
is small and statistically indistinguishable from zero. It does not hold for apartments: the premium exceeds the
concealment cost by a wide and precisely estimated margin, a specification tension the baseline cannot absorb,
because no belief about silence can price a silent listing above a verified efficient one. Section 4.6 shows that one
change on the buyer side can account for it: if buyers attend to energy when a rating is displayed and much less
when it is not, the model fits houses as before, rejects full attention in apartments, and leaves the estimated buyer
sensitivity unchanged. We maintain, rather than test, the model’s prediction that informed sellers of high-quality
properties never conceal; Appendix A relaxes it by reading the withheld grade from the registry and finds the
structural estimates essentially unchanged. Under the limited-attention extension the prediction survives whenever
not every informed low-quality seller hides (Corollary 4.3).
Our paper contributes to the literature on disclosure by testing the unraveling result in a market where the informed
and uninformed can be told apart. Prior work has examined the consequences of uncertainty about information
endowment (Dye, 1985; Jung and Kwon, 1988), of disclosure costs, and of disclosure credibility, but has generally


                                                          3
had to treat sellers’ information as latent. By observing it directly we can decompose silence into inability and
choice, and show that the first dominates the second by an order of magnitude while the second is what sustains
the price premium. The finding that silence is rewarded rather than punished is not a failure of the theory so
much as a demonstration of how sensitive its central prediction is to an assumption that is rarely examined.
Our paper also provides an information-based account of the residential energy-efficiency gap (Allcott and Green-
stone, 2012; Gerarden et al., 2017). The standard diagnosis appeals to split incentives, credit constraints, and
inattention to future energy costs (Allcott, 2011; Sallee, 2014; Allcott and Taubinsky, 2015). We identify a com-
plementary channel internal to the disclosure game. A seller holding a poor rating can renovate and publish a good
one, earning the quality premium, or stay silent and earn the non-disclosure premium instead, so the premium
to opacity looks like an opportunity cost of renovation that raises the private hurdle above engineering cost. In
equilibrium it does not, except in the corner where every informed low-quality seller hides. This has an unexpected
policy implication. Because the equilibrium absorbs enforcement through the fraction of sellers who conceal, the
renovation threshold depends only on the price gap between disclosed quality levels and is independent of en-
forcement intensity. Renovation subsidies, enforcement against concealment, and investment in audit coverage
act on three distinct margins and do not substitute for one another, contrary to the conventional treatment in
Gillingham and Palmer (2014). The result holds outside the corner in which every informed low-quality seller
hides, and with one concealment cost per market. diagnosis inside the disclosure game: buyers who attend less
to energy when no rating is shown let silence earn a premium that no belief about quality can produce. We do
not claim to have estimated the one true model of housing disclosure; we propose one way of reading these data
through a workhorse framework, and we are explicit throughout about which parameters are point-identified and
which are bounded.
The model also speaks to renovation policy. Extending the framework to an endogenous upgrade decision, we
show the minimum subsidy needed to make renovation privately worthwhile reduces, in the empirically dominant
partial-pooling regime, to the price gap between verified high and low quality alone, invariant to enforcement
against hiding and to how many sellers are informed, which act on separate margins instead. Forcing silent
listings to be read as carefully as rated ones would change who hides but not who renovates, and only a higher
weight on displayed ratings could raise the renovation threshold, within a bound that the data do not pin down. A
uniform national subsidy is accordingly poorly targeted. The required cost-share the state must cover varies widely
across markets, averaging 53 percent in apartment markets that need support at all at the light benchmark, and
aggregate programme costs among the sellers who can renovate run to e0.44–1.72 billion a year in our listing
sample at plausible renovation-cost benchmarks, for scale between 9 and 43 percent of MaPrimeRénov’s current
annual budget. Efficient targeting requires conditioning subsidy design on local market composition rather than
applying one national rate. Enforcement against strategic hiding and investment in audit infrastructure act on
separate margins: the first changes who hides, the second who is eligible to renovate, and neither lowers the
subsidy a given seller needs.
Our paper draws on three strands of work. It belongs to the literature that estimates disclosure models structurally
in order to assess the descriptiveness of classical disclosure theory and to recover primitives such as information
endowment, disclosure costs, and beliefs; closest to us is Bourveau et al. (2025), who structurally examine whether
the unraveling result describes an unregulated nineteenth-century market and find that full disclosure is reached
only gradually. Where that literature and the experimental work on disclosure ask how far receivers infer the
worst from silence (Jin et al., 2021; Hagenbach and Saucet, 2025), our extension asks whether they attend to
silence at all, drawing on evidence that limited attention changes how disclosed and undisclosed information is
priced (Hirshleifer and Teoh, 2003; DellaVigna and Pollet, 2009). It is closely related to the extensive evidence
on the capitalisation of energy labels in housing, which documents robust premia for efficient properties wherever
labels have been studied (Brounen and Kok, 2011; Hyland et al., 2013; Fuerst et al., 2016; Frondel et al., 2018;
Myers et al., 2022); that literature establishes that labels are priced, whereas our question is why their absence is
priced favourably. And it builds on work on information asymmetry in real-estate markets, where the informational
advantage of one side of the transaction has been inferred from prices and time-on-market (Levitt and Syverson,
2008; Lizzeri, 1999).
The features that make this setting informative also bound it. French EPC disclosure is a single mandate, in one
country, covering one verifiable attribute, and the low audit coverage that lets us separate inability from choice is
itself a feature of a young disclosure regime. The mechanism, however, is not specific to energy labels. Wherever


                                                         4
a disclosure mandate is introduced before the infrastructure that makes disclosure feasible is universal, a pool
of genuinely uninformed non-disclosers will exist, and that pool will shelter strategic concealment and dull the
incentive to disclose. That configuration characterises a broad class of emerging disclosure regimes, from climate-
risk and emissions reporting to supply-chain and sustainability disclosure, in which mandates routinely precede
measurement capacity, including the French carbon-disclosure mandate studied by Bourveau et al. (2024), where
the same gap between obligation and measurement infrastructure arises.


2 Unraveling with Possibly Uninformed Sellers
2.1 The Unraveling Benchmark
Asymmetric information about product quality can prevent trade from happening at the right prices. If buyers
cannot verify quality before purchasing, they bid no more than the value of what they expect to receive on average.
Sellers of genuinely good products are then underpaid, and may withdraw from the market altogether, leaving
buyers facing a pool of worse quality than they had hoped for (Akerlof, 1970). The outcome is bad for both sides.
Buyers pay for uncertainty they would rather not bear, and the best sellers cannot capture the value of what they
own.

Disclosure appears to resolve this problem without any regulatory intervention. Suppose quality is verifiable, so
that a seller who makes a claim about it can be held to that claim. A seller of the highest-quality product will
disclose, because staying silent means being valued at the average of the pool rather than at what the product
is actually worth. Buyers, anticipating this, understand that any seller who remains silent is not of the highest
quality, and revise downward what they will pay for silence. That revision gives the second-highest-quality seller
the same incentive, and disclosing becomes worthwhile for them too. The argument repeats. Each round of
disclosure worsens the expected quality of the remaining silent pool, which strengthens the incentive for the best
of those who remain to separate themselves. The process continues until all sellers but possibly the very worst
have disclosed. This is the unraveling result of Grossman (1981) and Milgrom (1981). Even sellers with bad news
disclose, because they anticipate that silence will be read as worse news still. If it holds, market forces alone
deliver near-complete disclosure and a mandate is redundant.

The argument is powerful but it is not free. It rests on four assumptions. First, (a) buyers know that sellers
possess private information about quality; if a seller may simply not know, silence no longer implies unwillingness
to reveal. Second, (b) disclosure is costless, or at least cheap relative to the price consequences of silence; a
large literature examines how disclosure costs truncate the unraveling process and leave a residual pool of non-
disclosers (Verrecchia, 1983; Jovanovic, 1982). Third, (c) disclosure is credible, so that a claim about quality
cannot be fabricated; without verifiability, disclosure degenerates into cheap talk and the unraveling logic never
starts (Crawford and Sobel, 1982; Stocken, 2000). Fourth, (d) buyers are homogeneous in how much they value
the disclosed attribute, so that the price penalty for silence is felt by every seller with equal force.

The first three assumptions have received sustained attention. The fourth has not, even though it governs the
strength of the entire mechanism. What drives a seller to disclose is the revenue lost when buyers price silence
pessimistically, and that loss is proportional to the share of buyers who care about the attribute in the first place.
Whether unraveling describes any particular market is therefore an empirical question, and answering it requires
knowing which of these assumptions fails there.


2.2 Two Departures and Why They Compound
Two of the four assumptions are implausible in markets where quality is certified by a third party and buyers differ
in their exposure to the certified attribute.

The first departure concerns assumption (a). When some sellers genuinely lack private information, silence pools
those who will not disclose with those who cannot. Dye (1985) showed that this possibility breaks the buyer
inference on which unraveling depends, because a rational buyer confronted with silence must form a posterior
over two distinct populations, and how pessimistic that posterior should be depends on their relative masses
rather than on the act of silence itself. Jung and Kwon (1988) characterised the resulting equilibria and showed
that partial pooling, in which some informed sellers of poor quality withhold while the uninformed are silent by
necessity, is generically stable. Disclosure does not unravel to the bottom; it stops somewhere in the interior.


                                                          5
The second departure concerns assumption (d). If only a fraction of buyers price the disclosed attribute, a seller
who withholds it faces a diluted penalty, because part of the demand side is indifferent to what was withheld.
Fishman and Hagerty (2003) show formally that mandated disclosure can be ineffective, and even welfare-reducing,
when buyers differ in their capacity to use disclosed information. The disclosure incentive is thus scaled by the
composition of demand, and where that composition is unfavourable the incentive can be too weak to sustain
separation even among sellers who are perfectly well informed.
The two departures do not merely coexist; they compound. A larger mass of sellers who cannot disclose dilutes
the adverse inference buyers draw from silence, which raises the expected quality of the silent pool and makes
withholding more attractive to informed sellers of poor quality. Those sellers then join the pool, and while their
entry worsens its true average quality, buyers cannot separate them from the involuntarily silent. Meanwhile, a
demand side in which many buyers do not price the attribute weakens the penalty that would otherwise discipline
them. Put together, the consequence is stronger than persistence of non-disclosure. Silence can command
a premium over the worst disclosed quality, since the silent pool contains a substantial mass of sellers whose
silence carries no adverse information at all. The sellers who cannot disclose do not intend to shelter anyone.
Their presence is involuntary. But it is precisely what makes silence ambiguous, and ambiguity is what makes
concealment privately rational.
Because the two departures operate on different primitives, they map out a space of possible market outcomes
rather than a single prediction. Figure 1 organises that space along two dimensions, the share of sellers who hold
verifiable information and the share of buyers who price it. Full unraveling requires both to be high. Where either
is interior, silence survives in equilibrium, and where the informed share is low while buyer sensitivity is not, silence
can be rewarded rather than punished.

Two features of this taxonomy matter for what follows. It implies that the informational content of silence is a
market-level object rather than a universal one, so that two markets facing the same disclosure mandate can sit in
different cells and sustain opposite price patterns. And it implies that the two primitives are separately movable by
policy. Investment in certification infrastructure raises the share of sellers holding verifiable information, while the
share of buyers who price the attribute responds to the legal weight the certificate carries and to the cost of the
attribute itself. Which cell a given market occupies, however, cannot be settled by theory. It requires measuring
the composition of the silent pool and the sensitivity of buyers separately, which is what the data described in the
next section allow us to do.

3 The French Energy-Performance Disclosure Regime
This section sets out the institution, the data, and four features of the market that any candidate theory of
EPC disclosure has to accommodate. We lead with apartments throughout and treat houses as a corroborating
segment. The two display the same qualitative patterns, but the apartment market has the lower audit coverage,
the larger share of strategic concealment, and the stability across the reform that the identification strategy
exploits.


                                                           6
3.1 Institutional Background
The French diagnostic de performance énergétique (DPE) is a standardised energy audit of a dwelling, carried out
by an accredited third-party assessor and recorded in a national registry maintained by the Agence de la Transition
Écologique (ADEME). It assigns a letter grade from A to G summarising modelled energy consumption per square
metre. Two features make it unusual as a disclosure instrument. It is certified rather than self-reported, so a
displayed grade can be checked against the registry. And the registry is centralised and public, so the existence
of an audit is verifiable independently of both the seller and the listing platform. That second property is what
makes the empirical exercise in this paper possible.
Before July 2021, the French DPE was a paper exercise. Scores were methodologically inconsistent across asses-
sors, carried no legal weight, and were routinely ignored in negotiations: a buyer who later discovered a disclosed
rating was misleading had no legal recourse. The reform of July 1, 2021 changed the regime fundamentally. It
standardized the methodology, imposed stricter accreditation requirements on assessors, and, most importantly,
rendered disclosed EPC ratings legally opposable: a buyer can now use a disclosed rating as grounds for price
renegotiation or legal action. The DPE became, for the first time, a document with contractual force.
Two things the reform did not change matter as much as what it did. First, the obligation to commission a
DPE before marketing a property predates it. The requirement, and the liability attached to marketing a property
without the required diagnostic, were established by loi ALUR (Article L271-4 of the Code de la construction et
de l’habitation) well before 2021. The legal exposure a seller incurs by coming to market without a certificate is
therefore governed by a mandate that straddles the reform date unchanged, while what July 2021 altered is how
much a disclosed grade is worth to the buyer. Second, the reform sharpened a pre-existing regulatory threshold
rather than inventing one. Under loi Climat et Résilience, dwellings rated E, F, or G face a phased prohibition on
new lettings. This draws a bright line between the bottom of the grade distribution and the rest, and gives the
binary quality distinction used below a concrete regulatory anchor rather than an arbitrary cutoff.
One further rule took effect six months after the reform. From 1 January 2022, an advertisement for the sale of
a dwelling must state the estimated annual energy expenditure for standard use, with the reference year of the
energy prices used, and a dwelling rated F or G must carry the mention “logement à consommation énergétique
excessive”. A displayed rating therefore reaches the buyer together with a euro figure, while a listing that shows
no rating carries neither. Section 4.6 builds on this asymmetry.

3.2 Data and Measurement
Testing a theory of strategic concealment requires observing something buyers cannot, namely whether a silent
seller holds a certificate. We recover it by matching listings against the ADEME registry.

3.2.1 Sources and Sample Construction
The empirical analysis draws on two sources matched at the listing level, together with transaction records used
separately. Listing records are sourced from SeLoger, a major French residential real-estate platform covering
sales across metropolitan France. For each listing, we observe the listing price, property characteristics (Xi : area,
number of rooms, floor, construction era, location), the disclosure indicator d ∈ {0, 1}, and, when d = 1, the
displayed EPC letter (A through G). Transaction records are not matched to individual listings; we use them to
estimate the mapping from listing price to realized sale price (γ0 and γ1 in equation (Eq. 1)), which supports
treating the listing price as the relevant outcome for the disclosure decision. The ADEME registry is the official
national database of certified Diagnostic de Performance Énergétique (DPE) audits, maintained by France’s
environment and energy management agency and reported by accredited third-party assessors. We match listings
to the ADEME registry using address, property type, area, and floor, assigning to each listing an indicator K = 1
if a certified audit record exists at the time of listing, and K = 0 otherwise. For the structural estimation, listings
for which no address is available are dropped, as the ADEME match requires a geocoded address; these account
for a small share of the total sample.
The full listing sample covers 2021Q3 to 2024Q4, the post-reform period during which EPC disclosure is legally
binding and DPE scores are credible to buyers. The apartment sample comprises 843,621 listings and the house
sample 687,878 listings. For structural estimation we restrict to 2022 to 2024: the H2 2021 period (the six months
immediately following the reform) is excluded because the market had not yet fully repriced the DPE (hedonic


                                                          7
estimates of β̂1 are near zero or negative in that window), yielding 767,000 apartment listings and 633,000 house
listings for the structural sample. For 51.2% of apartment listings and 40.3% of house listings, the ADEME match
yields K = 1; the remainder are classified as genuinely uninformed (K = 0, no verified audit on record). For
identification of market-level price indices, we use the panel of hedonic price indices constructed in Patras (2024),
covering 110 markets (attraction zones, zones d’emploi) with monthly observations from October 2018 through
July 2024.

Three features stand out. First, strategic hiding is rare but economically meaningful: 3.5% of apartment listings
and 1.1% of house listings come from informed sellers who chose not to disclose. Pooling across listings, the
hiding rate among informed sellers (K = 1) is 6.9% for apartments and 2.7% for houses; the corresponding
unweighted means across markets, reported as σ̂0,m in the results, are 7.7% and 3.7%. Second, silence is
uncommon relative to non-verification: only 16.7% of apartment listings and 8.6% of house listings are silent
sellers with no registry record, and these are the non-disclosers who provide involuntary cover for strategic hiders.
Third, and quantitatively dominant, 32.2% of apartment listings and 51.2% of house listings display a score with
no matching registry record. Taken together, sellers without a post-2021 registry record account for 48.8% of
apartment listings and 61.1% of house listings, but the large majority of them do disclose a score.

Three features of this match bear on its credibility. It is a match to an administrative registry rather than to a
survey or a self-report, so the information-status indicator does not depend on seller cooperation and cannot be
strategically misreported. It requires a geocoded address, so the matched sample is the subset of listings that
can be located at the address level rather than the full listing universe; sample scopes are stated alongside the
statistics reported below. And, most consequentially, K = 0 must be read as no post-July-2021 certificate in
the public registry rather than as no certificate exists. The registry was populated from the reform date onward,
while a certificate issued earlier could remain legally valid at the time of listing, so a seller could hold and display
a valid certificate that the registry cannot corroborate. The data bear this out. The share of listings displaying a
score with no registry record falls monotonically as the registry accumulates, from 62.5% of apartment listings in
2021Q3 to 18.4% in 2024Q4, mirrored by a rise in verified disclosers from 7.4% to roughly one half.

This coverage gap has a known direction. A seller holding a still-valid pre-reform certificate who withholds it is
recorded as genuinely uninformed rather than as a strategic hider, so the measured hiding rate σ̂0,m understates
the true one. Because the equilibrium belief e(σ0 ) is decreasing in σ0 , an understated hiding rate implies an
overstated belief and hence an overstated concealment cost. The coverage gap therefore reinforces, rather than
threatens, the interpretation of ĉ0,m as an upper bound. It is also one reason the structural sample begins in
2022, since the second half of 2021, where the gap is most severe, is excluded.

4 A Structural Interpretation
4.1 Motivation
To recover the forces behind the patterns documented in Section 3, we specify and estimate a model of the seller’s
disclosure decision. The model is a housing-market application of Dye (1985), as equilibrium-characterised by Jung
and Kwon (1988), with one structural addition, heterogeneity in buyer EPC sensitivity (ρ). The binary quality
space (θ ∈ {0, 1}) and the seller-information heterogeneity (π) are adapted directly from those antecedents.
What goes beyond them is the closed-form expression for the non-disclosure premium and its invariance to market
composition in the partial-pooling regime (Proposition 4.2), the strict cross-regime ordering of the premium
(Eq. Eq. 7), and an identification strategy that uses the directly observed audit status K to recover c0 and ρ
market by market.

We allow for, but do not impose, strategic concealment. Nothing in the specification requires informed sellers
to withhold. The hiding rate is an equilibrium object, the model admits full unravelling as a special case, and
whether concealment occurs, and how much, is what the estimation determines.

Two considerations motivate this structure. The first is resemblance. The facts in Section 3.3 line up with the
Dye–Jung-Kwon configuration, in which a silent pool is overwhelmingly silent by constraint rather than by choice,
a small but non-trivial mass of informed sellers who withhold, and a premium attached to silence rather than a
penalty. The second is institutional. The binary quality space has an empirical anchor in the rental-ban threshold
introduced by loi Climat et Résilience: dwellings rated E, F, or G face a progressive prohibition on new lettings,
which draws a bright line between the bottom of the grade distribution and the rest and maps onto θ ∈ {0, 1}
without an arbitrary cutoff.


                                                                         12
A competing explanation must also clear a demanding timing test. Any account that attributes the post-reform
widening to a supply-side shift has to generate an abrupt change at July 2021 in both the quality premium and
the non-disclosure premium, in the same markets and on the same calendar date, while leaving the composition
of the listing pool undisturbed. The share of listings without a disclosed score is, however, essentially flat across
the reform in the apartment segment (Section 3.3.2), which is the segment where identification is sharpest. A
shift in the mix of sellers would generically move the two gaps by different amounts, and would show up in that
share. Neither is observed.
The mechanism itself is the one set out in Section 2: sellers who cannot disclose dilute the adverse inference buyers
draw from silence, which makes withholding attractive to informed sellers of poor quality, whose entry the buyer
cannot separate from the involuntarily silent. France sits in the bottom-right cell of Figure 1: incomplete registry
coverage combined with positive buyer EPC-sensitivity. Even where the EPC-sensitive buyer share approaches
one, as it does in most apartment markets after the reform, incomplete coverage alone sustains the premium,
because so long as a non-trivial share of non-disclosers hold no certificate, buyers cannot fully penalise silence.
Full unravelling requires both conditions; France satisfies only one.
Finally, we are explicit about what the model does not settle. It takes no stance on why an informed seller
withholds, since deliberate concealment and an unrecorded but still-valid certificate are observationally similar in
the listing data, and we do not attempt to separate them. It assumes no falsification, so a displayed score is taken
at face value. And it maintains Corollary 4.1, σ1∗ = 0, rather than testing it; Section 4.6 relaxes the buyer side
and shows where this restriction survives. Informed non-disclosers are assigned low quality by revealed preference,
which is an assumption and not a result. Appendix A reports estimates that instead read the withheld quality
from the registry, so the restriction can be assessed. Nor does the design pin down ρm from the interior in most
markets, where the data are consistent with full buyer sensitivity but cannot establish how close to one it lies,
and we maintain ρ̂m = 1 for those markets and treat ĉ0,m as an upper bound accordingly. We do not purport to
estimate the one true model of housing disclosure; we propose one way of reading these data through a workhorse
framework.

4.2 Setup
4.2.1 Environment and Timing
A seller owns a property with observable attributes X and an unobservable energy performance level θ ∈ {0, 1},
where θ = 1 denotes high efficiency. Whether the seller knows θ depends on whether she has obtained a certified
Diagnostic de Performance Énergétique (DPE) audit prior to listing. With probability π ∈ (0, 1), the seller is
informed (K = 1) and knows θ; with probability 1 − π, she is uninformed (K = 0) and does not. The parameter
π is not a choice variable: it reflects structural features of local audit markets, including the cost and availability of
accredited assessors, the time pressure sellers face (relocation, financial distress, inheritance), and the regulatory
requirements in force. It is measured directly from the ADEME registry match, as described in Section 3.2.
The seller simultaneously chooses a disclosure decision d ∈ {0, 1} and a listing price plist . French property law
requires that all mandatory diagnostic documents, including the DPE, be appended to the sales agreement at
signing (loi ALUR, Article L271-4 CCH), so the disclosure decision and the listing price are set jointly at the time
the property is placed on the market. This institutional timing motivates the simultaneous-choice formulation:
the seller does not first decide whether to disclose and then price, but sets a complete strategy that internalizes
how the disclosure decision shifts buyer beliefs and thereby affects both the probability of sale and the expected
transaction price.
Assumption (No Falsification). Disclosed EPC scores are verifiable against the ADEME registry. An informed
seller with K = 1 may choose d ∈ {0, 1}, while an uninformed seller with K = 0 is constrained to d = 0: she
cannot disclose what she does not possess. This rules out costless misreporting.

The key friction is that buyers observe (d, plist , X) but not the seller’s information status K: they cannot distinguish
a seller who chose not to disclose from one who could not. A buyer then arrives and decides whether to purchase
at plist . With probability ρ ∈ (0, 1), the buyer is EPC-sensitive and incorporates energy performance into her
valuation; with probability 1−ρ, the buyer is EPC-insensitive and conditions her bid solely on observable attributes.
Sellers cannot observe buyer type ex ante and optimize against expected demand. The parameter ρ is market-


                                                           13
specific, reflecting the local composition of the buyer pool, and is identified from the differential price response
to the July 2021 reform across markets, as detailed in Section 4.4.

4.2.4 Buyer Beliefs and the Non-Disclosing Pool
When observing a non-disclosing listing, buyers cannot determine whether the seller is strategically concealing
an unfavorable audit or simply lacks a certified EPC. They form a posterior belief about expected quality using
Bayes’ rule. The structure of this belief depends on the composition of the non-disclosing pool, which is itself an
equilibrium object.

Who Hides? Each seller is characterized by two attributes: information status K ∈ {0, 1} and quality θ ∈ {0, 1}.

Assumption 4.1 (Independence) K ⊥ θ, with Pr(K = 1) = π and Pr(θ = 1) = θ̄.
Independence is most plausible for transactions driven by exogenous life events (succession following a death,
court-ordered liquidation, or divorce settlement), in which legal proceedings impose binding timing constraints
that preclude any quality-contingent decision to obtain a certified EPC. In such cases, the seller’s information
status K is determined by administrative capacity and procedural deadlines rather than the energy performance
of the dwelling; a seller under a judicial mandate to liquidate an estate has neither the incentive nor the discretion
to condition audit acquisition on θ. In France, forced transactions of this type constitute a substantial share of
the resale market, providing a natural mechanism through which K ⊥ θ obtains in the data.

4.3 Equilibrium
4.3.1 Disclosure Incentives
A seller’s disclosure decision reduces to one comparison: does withholding the audit, priced off the market’s pool
belief e, fetch more than disclosing the true quality θ would? A low-quality seller (θ = 0) gains by pooling with
uninformed sellers and capturing a share of their quality premium; a high-quality seller (θ = 1) loses, because
pooling with low-quality hiders drags the belief e below 1. That asymmetry is the source of everything that
follows.
Proposition 4.1 (Disclosure Incentives and Seller Sorting) Under the benchmark αk = βk , a seller of type
(K, θ) strictly prefers disclosure if and only if θ > e(σ0∗ ). In particular:
  (i) An informed seller with θ = 1 strictly prefers disclosure for any e(σ0∗ ) < 1.
  (ii) An informed seller with θ = 0 strictly prefers non-disclosure whenever e(σ0∗ ) > 0.
Proof. See Appendix E. □
Proposition 4.1 is the familiar adverse-selection asymmetry, worked out for this setting. A high-quality seller has
nothing to gain from ambiguity. Disclosure reveals her type exactly; non-disclosure pools her with low-quality
hiders, and since e(σ0∗ ) < 1 for any non-degenerate pool, that comparison always binds. A low-quality seller faces
the opposite incentive — concealment lets her price off e(σ0∗ ), which is strictly above her true quality of zero
whenever any uninformed sellers are present, so the gain from pooling is strictly positive. The hiding cost c1 > 0
that falls only on high-quality concealers reinforces this separation but is not what drives it under the benchmark
αk = βk : pool inference alone is enough to make high-quality sellers disclose, and c1 only starts to matter once
αk < βk , the distrust-discount case.
Corollary 4.1 (High-Quality Sellers Always Disclose) In any equilibrium, σ1∗ = 0.
Proof. Follows directly from Proposition 4.1(i). □

All three equilibria in Proposition 4.2 come from the same comparison. The hiding gain ∆(e) = ρβ1 e is evaluated
at the two extremes of pool contamination and set against the hiding cost c0 . If c0 dominates even at the cleanest
pool (σ0 = 0, e = θ̄), no one hides; if the gain survives even the dirtiest pool (σ0 = 1, e = (1 − π)θ̄/(1 − π θ̄)),
everyone hides; in between, an interior share hides and the market settles there. The premium inherits a strict
ordering across the three regimes, ρβ1 θ̄ > c0 > ρβ1 (1 − π)θ̄/(1 − π θ̄) (Cost-Deterred Hiding above, Complete
Adverse Selection below, and Partial Pooling pinned at c0 in between), and the interior value depends on neither
π nor ρ.
Cost-Deterred Hiding is where the hiding cost is prohibitive even against the cleanest pool imaginable, one made
up entirely of uninformed sellers drawn from the unconditional quality distribution. No informed low-quality seller
finds concealment worth it, so σ0∗ = 0 and the pool stays uncontaminated at e∗ = θ̄, and the premium ρβ1 θ̄ is just
the unconditional quality belief scaled by buyers’ EPC sensitivity. The deterrence result echoes Milgrom (1981)
and Grossman (1981), but the mechanism differs, since their unraveling runs through a belief penalty, where
silence triggers the worst possible inference, while here it runs through the cost c0 , precisely because uninformed
sellers keep silence from ever carrying that worst-type inference. This is the only regime where the non-disclosing
pool is informationally clean.
Complete Adverse Selection is the opposite corner. The hiding gain survives even the dirtiest pool the model
can produce. Every informed low-quality seller hides, σ0∗ = 1, and buyer beliefs collapse to their floor, e∗ =
(1 − π)θ̄/(1 − π θ̄), strictly below θ̄, taking the premium down to its lower bound with them. That floor is
nonetheless strictly positive, because the uninformed sellers in the pool keep diluting the adverse inference and
block the complete belief collapse that Milgrom–Grossman unraveling would otherwise imply. This is the Dye
(1985) mechanism, in which a residual, non-strategic silent population keeps silence partially informative even
once every strategic hider has already piled in.
The indifference condition ∆(e(σ0∗ )) = c0 pins down Partial Pooling, the interior case between the two corners.
Φ is positive at σ0 = 0 and negative at σ0 = 1, so strict monotonicity delivers a unique such crossing, where the
marginal hider is exactly indifferent. The underlying mechanism is a feedback loop. As more low-quality sellers
hide, e(σ0 ) falls, the pool looks worse, and ∆(e) shrinks, until the shrinking gain reaches the hiding cost. A
fraction σ0∗ hides and pools with the uninformed; the remaining 1 − σ0∗ disclose rather than keep contaminating
the pool for a gain that no longer covers c0 .
What makes this regime useful is that the equilibrium premium equals c0 exactly, and stays there regardless of π or
ρ. That invariance is just the indifference condition doing its job. The price gap between the non-disclosing pool
and disclosed low quality has to equal the hiding cost, or the marginal seller would strictly prefer one action to the
other. Take a rise in π. Holding σ0∗ fixed, it would mechanically dirty the pool and push e down, but equilibrium
restores Φ = 0 by lowering σ0∗ instead, so fewer hiders mean a cleaner pool, e rebounds, and the premium lands
back on c0 . A change in ρ is absorbed the same way. The non-disclosure premium is therefore a revealed hiding
cost rather than a free function of market composition. The observable price gap between non-disclosing and
disclosed-low sellers identifies c0 directly, and this is the only regime where π, ρ, and c0 are all jointly identified.

 (ii) In the Partial Pooling equilibrium: ∂σ0∗ /∂c0 < 0, ∂σ0∗ /∂ρ > 0, and ∂σ0∗ /∂π < 0.

Part (i) works through two distinct channels. The effect of π runs through the belief channel, since a rising share
of informed sellers makes non-disclosure more informative of low quality, so e∗ falls and the gain from concealment
dissipates. The effect of ρ runs through the revenue channel instead, since a larger share of EPC-sensitive buyers
widens the price spread between quality levels and raises the value of concealment.
Part (ii) follows from the implicit function theorem applied to Φ(σ0∗ ) = 0. Because Φ′ (σ0∗ ) < 0, ∂σ0∗ /∂c0 < 0
follows directly. A higher ρ raises ∆ at any fixed e, which shifts Φ upward and raises the interior equilibrium, so
∂σ0∗ /∂ρ > 0. π works through the same belief channel as in part (i). A higher π increases the contamination
weight π(1− θ̄) in the denominator of e(σ0 ), which reduces ∆(e(σ0 )) and shifts Φ downward, so ∂σ0∗ /∂π < 0. The
parameters π and c0 are therefore policy substitutes, each reducing strategic hiding through a distinct channel,
π through beliefs and c0 through costs, with marginal effects on σ0∗ that are both negative and quantifiable from
the estimated model.
Corollary 4.2 (Effect of ρ on the non-disclosure premium) (i) The non-disclosure premium ∆ = p∗0 − p∗1
     is weakly increasing in ρ: strictly so in the Cost-Deterred Hiding and Complete Adverse Selection regimes
     (∂∆/∂ρ > 0), and zero in the Partial Pooling regime (∂∆/∂ρ = 0, with the premium pinned at c0 ).
 (ii) The parameter interval sustaining the Partial Pooling regime, [ρ β1 e(1), ρ β1 θ̄], is weakly expanding in ρ:
      its width ρ β1 [θ̄ − e(1)] is strictly increasing in ρ whenever π > 0.
Proof. See Appendix E. □
A larger share of buyers who price the certificate raises what silence is worth, but only at the corners. Inside
Partial Pooling the premium is pinned to the concealment cost, so a rise in ρ shows up as more sellers hiding
rather than a wider price gap. Part (ii) adds that higher ρ widens the range of parameters over which Partial
Pooling survives at all. Empirically, this predicts that within partial-pooling markets the non-disclosure premium
should be uncorrelated with ρ̂m even as the hiding rate rises with it, and that markets with higher ρ̂m should be
less likely to sit in a corner regime — a pattern we check directly against the regime classification in Section 4.5.
Proposition 4.4 (Equilibrium effect of π) As π increases, the lower boundary ∆(e(1)) of the Partial Pooling
interval falls (∂e(1)/∂π < 0), so the condition for Complete Adverse Selection (c0 < ∆(e(1))) becomes harder
to satisfy and the equilibrium moves toward the Cost-Deterred Hiding regime. For any fixed hiding cost c0 , a
sufficiently large increase in π drives the equilibrium out of the Partial Pooling region toward full unraveling.
Proof. See Appendix E. □
Better registry coverage makes silence more informative, because a larger fraction of non-disclosers are hiding by
choice rather than by constraint. As a result, buyers discount silence more heavily, concealment becomes less
attractive, and the market moves toward disclosure. The testable content is a cross-market comparative static.
Markets with higher π̂m should display lower hiding rates σ̂0,m and should be disproportionately represented among
the markets classified as fully unravelling in Section 4.5.

equilibrium hiding rate.
Proof. See Appendix E. □
The premium in (Eq. 7) generates a transfer from EPC-sensitive buyers to the non-disclosing pool, but it does
not accrue symmetrically within that pool. The informed low-quality hider receives the premium but nets zero,
because the indifference condition ∆(e(σ0∗ )) = c0 ensures that the gross revenue gain from concealment is
exactly consumed by the hiding cost. The uninformed seller, who bears no hiding cost, is the residual claimant
and pockets the full premium c0 as a pure rent. The welfare cost of the equilibrium splits into two distinct
components. The first is an allocative distortion. EPC-sensitive buyers pay a positive premium for assets whose
true energy performance falls below the pooled belief e(σ0∗ ), a misallocation that would be eliminated if the
concealed information were transmitted. The second is a deadweight loss, since the hiding cost c0 is incurred by


                                                         19
informed sellers at listing time without any counterpart in buyer valuations. This is a social loss only to the extent
that c0 represents real resources destroyed, such as legal fees, prolonged negotiation, or administrative burden.
Any portion that is instead a transfer, such as the expected value of a fine accruing to the state, is a redistribution
rather than a loss. We do not identify this split, so the deadweight-loss component is an upper bound. A formal
efficiency characterization of these two components, and of the optimal subsidy that addresses them, is developed
in Section 5.


4.4 Identification
The model leaves two objects to be recovered market by market: ρm , the share of buyers who actually price the
certificate, and c0,m , what it costs an informed seller to keep an unfavourable rating off the listing. Everything
else the model needs is something we can count. The ADEME match tells us which sellers hold a certificate
(πm ), what those certificates say (θ̄m ), and how often a seller holding a poor one lists without it (σ0,m ). The
identification problem is therefore two parameters wide, and the two parameters are reached by different routes.
Three ingredients do the work. The July 2021 reform supplies the price variation: differencing each market’s
cross-group price gaps around a known date turns them into demand-side objects, purged of the pre-existing level
differences that separate one local market from another. The registry supplies the beliefs, because we observe
about the non-disclosing pool exactly what a rational buyer would be forming beliefs about, so the equilibrium
belief e∗m can be computed rather than estimated. The third ingredient sits outside the price system altogether.
It is the certified energy consumption of the dwellings themselves, and it is what allows buyer sensitivity to be
separated from the size of the underlying quality difference. Without it, ρm cannot be recovered at all, for a
reason internal to the model rather than a shortcoming of the data: the sensitive-buyer share enters every price the
model generates in one place only, so it cancels out of any ratio of price gaps one might form. The concealment
cost is the easier of the two. It follows from the disclosed-price gap and the observed hiding rate, and needs
neither the anchor nor ρm .


4.4.1 The July 2021 Reform as a Natural Experiment
The change in the legal status of the DPE described in Section 3.1 is the source of identification: it created a
precise, legally documented change in the value of a disclosed EPC score to buyers, with a known implementation
date of July 1, 2021. Crucially, the reform operated on the demand side, that is, on the weight a disclosed
rating carries in buyers’ valuations and legal recourse, and not on the seller’s cost of withholding. The obligation
to provide a DPE at sale, and the compliance and liability exposure attached to coming to market without the
required diagnostic, were already in force under loi ALUR (Article L271-4 CCH) well before 2021. These are
precisely the components of the concealment cost c0,m . What July 2021 changed was not whether a seller must
disclose, nor the cost she bears for withholding, but how much a disclosed rating now matters to the buyer. The
reform therefore shifts ρm while leaving c0,m , governed by the pre-existing disclosure mandate, unchanged across
the reform date. This is what licenses reading the DiD change in the premium as the demand response that
identifies ρm , holding the hiding cost fixed. Whether the supply side of the market responded concurrently is a
maintained assumption we cannot directly test with the data at hand; we return to it below.4
The identifying assumption is the standard parallel-trends condition: in the absence of the reform, the cross-
group price gaps β1,m,t (energy-efficient vs. energy-intensive disclosed properties) and ∆m,t (undisclosed vs.
energy-intensive) would have continued on their pre-reform trajectory. The DiD does not require that buyers
were entirely inattentive to EPC scores before July 2021; it requires only that their relative pricing of disclosure
groups was not already trending. Any post-reform shift in the gaps, relative to that pre-reform baseline, is then
attributable to the change in buyer incentives induced by legal opposability.
Parallel trends licenses reading the two DiDs as the reform’s effect. The structural mapping below reads them,
in addition, as the post-reform energy components of the two gaps, which requires one further maintained
assumption.

4.4.3 Beliefs Read Directly from the Registry
The equilibrium belief e∗m , the quality a buyer rationally attributes to a listing that shows no rating, is normally

4.5.2 Structural Estimates and Economic Interpretation
Buyer sensitivity emerges from the anchor as a genuine point estimate in each market rather than as a value pressed
against a boundary. About half the buyers in the average market price energy performance into their offers —
0.495 for apartments, 0.458 for houses — and markets spread around those averages in a way the estimator does
not manufacture, with a cross-market standard deviation of 0.25 and 0.27 respectively. A ratio-based construction
returns nothing of the kind; it piles the whole distribution onto the upper bound. That bound is now something the
data can refuse. Since the sensitive share cannot exceed one, the anchored estimate requires the disclosed-price
gap to stay below the energy-bill benchmark, and the requirement fails in only 1 of 45 apartment markets and
5 of 97 house markets. Almost everywhere, the running-cost saving an efficient dwelling delivers is large enough
on its own terms to account for the price advantage certified efficiency commands, without supposing that every
buyer is paying attention.
One consequence of the closed-form estimator is worth stating plainly, because it closes off a finding the model
might otherwise appear to offer. The concealment cost is the disclosed-price gap scaled by a belief that is itself
confined, by construction, between the value it would take if every informed low-quality seller hid and the value it
would take if none did. The cost can therefore never land outside the Partial Pooling band, and every market in
the sample is classified as Partial Pooling. Cost-Deterred Hiding and Complete Adverse Selection remain possible
corner regimes in the model, but a market can only be sorted into one of them when buyer sensitivity has been
clipped to a boundary. With sensitivity point-identified, that sorting cannot arise here, and we report no regime
heterogeneity as an empirical finding. This classification is a property of the baseline. Under limited attention the
Partial Pooling band narrows by the factor ψ (Corollary 4.5), so in apartments a market sits in Partial Pooling
only for a narrow range of hiding costs.
The cost of keeping an unfavourable rating off the listing is economically material and strikingly similar across the
two segments: 0.024 log points, roughly 2.4% of property value, for apartments, and 0.035 log points, or 3.5%,
for houses. Two segments that otherwise trade very differently landing within about a percentage point of each
other points to a cost set by the legal disclosure regime rather than by the property. This is the baseline reading;
under the limited-attention extension the hiding cost implied for apartments is larger (Section 4.6.5).
Nothing in this construction forces the concealment cost to sit below the non-disclosure premium, and it need
not. The two are built from different moments, and the distance between them is the model’s over-identifying
restriction (Lemma B.1), which we report rather than resolve by fiat. In house markets that wedge averages 0.4
log points and cannot be distinguished from zero (t = 1.10), so the restriction survives. In apartment markets
it is 6.1 log points and precisely estimated (t = 6.78): non-disclosers there command substantially more than
the disclosed-price gap and the registry-measured belief say they should under Partial Pooling. Truncating the
post-reform window to one or two years instead of the full three leaves both results standing (Appendix A), so
neither turns on the endpoint we happened to choose. If anything the two move apart as the window lengthens,

The apartment wedge has a specific shape. It is not that silent apartments sell slightly above what the registry-

The contrast between the property types is itself informative. The energy-efficiency premium in house markets
runs about 40 percent above what it does in apartments on the estimation sample, 0.058 against 0.041 log points
(a ratio that rises to roughly three, 0.051 against 0.017, over all event-study markets). We do not test why, and
more than one channel fits. Houses are larger and older on average, so heating weighs more heavily in a buyer’s
running expenses. They also sit predominantly outside city centres, in markets where demand pressure is weaker
and price formation depends less on locational scarcity, which leaves more of the price free to respond to an
attribute such as energy performance. In tight urban apartment markets the opposite holds: location dominates
valuation and compresses the share any single characteristic can claim. Separating these explanations would
require variation we do not exploit here.
What follows from the larger premium is easier to pin down. A seller who hides a poor rating in a house market
forgoes more by doing so, and the data reflect that exactly. The hiding rate among informed low-quality sellers
is well under half the apartment figure, 6.8% against 15.2%, and strategic hiders make up 1.1% of house listings
against 3.5% of apartment listings. The non-disclosure premium follows in turn, 0.039 for houses against 0.085 for
apartments, because a thinner pool of strategic hiders leaves buyers less pessimistic about what silence conceals.
Higher quality premium, lower hiding rate, lower non-disclosure premium: the ordering of hiding rates is what the
model’s equilibrium comparative statics predict when markets differ in the return to verified quality, although the
level of the apartment premium is not (Section 4.6).

4.6 Limited Attention to Silence
The estimates in Section 4.5 leave one result unexplained, and its shape is specific enough to point toward a
mechanism. In apartment markets the reform raised the price of a listing that shows no rating, relative to one
rated E to G, by more than it raised the price of a verified A-to-D listing. We call a listing that shows no rating
silent; in the notation of Section 4.2 it is a non-disclosing listing, d = 0. Averaged over the 45 apartment markets
of Table 4, the two shifts are 8.5 and 4.1 log points, and the first exceeds the second in 37 of them. In house
markets the order is reversed on average, 3.9 against 5.8. This subsection asks what would have to be true of
buyers for silence to be worth more than a verified efficient rating.
One answer is that buyers attend to energy when a listing puts it in front of them, and much less when it does
not. We allow for this mechanism without imposing it: full attention remains a special case, and the data decide
between the two. The extension changes the buyer side of the model and nothing else. It nests the model of
Sections 4.2–4.3 as a special case and preserves its regimes. It leaves ρ̂m unchanged as a number but reads it as
effective attention to displayed ratings, and it reads the concealment cost off the non-disclosure premium rather
than off β̂1,m ê∗m (Proposition 4.10). It also turns the over-identifying wedge λ̂m of Section 4.4 into an estimate
of how much attention silent listings receive.

What these amendments share is that the buyer facing a silent listing still thinks about its energy performance;
she only reasons about it imperfectly. A large body of evidence suggests that consumers often do not think about
costs that are not put in front of them, and that when they do not, they act as if those costs were small rather
than average. Buyers under-react to sales taxes that are not included in posted prices (Chetty et al., 2009), to
road tolls collected electronically (Finkelstein, 2009) and to shrouded shipping fees (Brown et al., 2010). Energy
operating costs are a documented instance in durable-goods markets (e.g., Allcott, 2011; Sallee, 2014; Allcott and
Taubinsky, 2015). Models of focusing, salience and sparsity formalise the same idea, that the attributes a choice
set makes salient receive more weight than those it leaves in the background (e.g., Kőszegi and Szeidl, 2013;
Bordalo et al., 2013; Gabaix, 2014). In financial markets, limited attention changes how disclosed and undisclosed
information is priced (e.g., Hirshleifer and Teoh, 2003; DellaVigna and Pollet, 2009).

Three features of the French setting make this behaviour plausible for apartment buyers in particular. First, a
listing that discloses shows the rating as a coloured label and, from January 2022, an estimate of the annual
energy bill in euros, so disclosure itself puts energy into the buyer’s comparison of dwellings.9 A listing that shows
nothing does not. Second, heating in many apartment buildings is collective and paid through service charges, so
apartment buyers are likely to see energy as part of a monthly charge rather than as an attribute of the dwelling.
Third, a missing rating is common: about 95 percent of silent apartment listings match no post-reform certificate
in the registry. Silence is therefore unremarkable rather than conspicuous.

The mechanism is easiest to see in three steps. Suppose first that buyers attend to energy as often for a silent
listing as for a rated one. They then price silence at the expected cost of the silent pool, and the model is exactly
the one estimated above. Suppose next that they attend half as often. Half of them still price silence at the
expected cost of the pool, while the other half price it at whatever they implicitly assume when energy never
comes up. If that default is “nothing out of the ordinary”, the silent listing gains on every rated listing, efficient
ones included. Suppose finally that buyers never attend to energy on a silent listing. It is then priced as if it
carried the default cost, which can lie below the cost of an A-rated dwelling, and silence beats every rating.

We embed display-dependent attention in the buyer side of Section 4.2. This lets us read the over-identifying
wedge as a measure of the attention silent listings receive. The estimators of Section 4.4 keep their numerical
values, but under the extension they measure different objects (Proposition 4.10). Everything not stated below
is as in Section 4.2: seller types, the No Falsification assumption, Assumption 4.1, the payoff (Eq. 1) and the
demand system (Eq. 3).


Environment and timing. The environment is that of Section 4.2, with one addition on the buyer side: after
seeing the listing, an EPC-sensitive buyer either attends to its energy performance or does not, with a probability
that depends on what the listing shows. The timing is as follows.

  (1) Nature draws the seller’s quality θ and information status K, independently, with Pr(θ = 1) = θ̄ and
      Pr(K = 1) = π.

  (2) The seller lists the property, choosing the display d (only if K = 1) and the listing price.

  (3) A buyer arrives. She is EPC-sensitive with probability ρ. If sensitive, she attends to energy with a probability
      ωd that depends on the display.

  (4) She values the property given what she attends to, and buys or not.

Definition 4.2 (Default Energy Cost) The default energy cost C def ≥ 0 is the energy cost an EPC-sensitive
buyer subtracts from a listing’s value when she does not attend to energy. It is measured in the same units as Cθ .


                                                          30
The term follows the limited-attention literature, in which an agent who does not attend to an attribute replaces
it with a default value rather than with an inference (Gabaix, 2014, 2019). The default is therefore not a belief
about the dwelling’s rating. It is what the buyer implicitly assumes when the question of energy never arises.
Three benchmark values fix ideas. With C def = 0 no energy cost is priced at all, which does not mean the buyer
believes the dwelling consumes nothing, only that energy plays no part in her valuation. With C def equal to the
prior mean cost θ̄C1 + (1 − θ̄)C0 , the inattentive buyer behaves like one who assumes an average dwelling, which
is cursedness by another name. With C def = C̄ sil she happens to subtract exactly what an attentive buyer would
infer, and attention becomes irrelevant. The same default applies to rated and silent listings, because a buyer who
does not attend to energy, by definition, does not use what the listing shows about it. Which value is relevant
is an empirical question: Proposition 4.6(iii) shows that the extension can explain the apartment wedge only if
C def < C1 , and Proposition 4.10(iii) shows what the data can say about its level.

do not model heterogeneity in hiding costs across sellers, and we read the band as a limitation of the single-cost

The comparative statics separate three channels. The revenue channel (ρω1 ) works as in Section 4.3: a larger
effective attention to displayed ratings raises the gain from concealment and the hiding rate. The belief channel
(π) keeps its sign but is scaled by ψ, because a higher share of informed sellers makes silence more informative
only to buyers who think about what silence means. The attention channel (ψ, C def ) is new: more attention
to silence, or a less optimistic default, lowers the gain from hiding and the hiding rate. These are the margins
on which a salience policy acts (Section 5.6). The last statement in part (i) is the formal reason the single-cost
model becomes fragile in apartments: when silence is barely read, a small change in the hiding cost produces a
large swing in the hiding rate, because nothing on the buyer side pushes back.

High-quality hiding and non-strategic silence. Proposition 4.6(ii) and Corollary 4.3 say when high-quality sellers
may hide: only if silence sells above a verified efficient rating. The registry shows similar hiding rates for the two
grades, 6.3 against 8.0 percent in apartments and 2.4 against 2.9 percent in houses. In apartments silence pays
a high-quality seller 4.5 log points more than her rating, yet she displays, because in Partial Pooling her slack is
b + c1 (Proposition 4.8). The pattern in the registry is not evidence for strategic hiding by high-quality sellers:
house sellers of high quality stay silent although silence costs them about 1.9 log points relative to their rating, so
some silence is not strategic at all, for instance listings whose certificate is still pending. The same non-strategic
silence is in the hiding rate of low-quality sellers, so the interior hiding rates we use may overstate strategic hiding.

5 Policy Extension: Renovation Subsidies
5.1 What the Model Adds
The core model treats θ as exogenous, but the policy question is what happens once quality becomes a choice.
An informed seller who holds a low-quality certificate can hide it, disclose it, or renovate and disclose a verified
high rating, and a subsidy is the natural instrument when renovation costs more than the market pays for it. This
section works through that choice in the model of Section 4.6, in which a buyer’s attention to energy depends on
whether the listing displays a rating. The model of Sections 4.2–4.3 is the case ψ = ω1 = 1, so every statement
below reduces to its full-attention counterpart at that point. We use the attention model throughout for reasons
of fit: it reproduces the apartment premium that the full-attention model cannot reach, and in houses, where ψ̂ is
0.95, the two models nearly coincide (Section 4.6.5). The hiding cost is a single c0 per market, held at the value
that reproduces the observed hiding rate at the estimated attention, which is 8.5 log points in apartments and 3.9
in houses (2.4 and 3.5 in the full-attention model). Uninformed sellers have no strategy set, and the thresholds
are computed holding beliefs fixed, except in Section 5.4, which lets the pool respond.
Two caveats frame what the model adds. First, wherever the renovation threshold is the price gap b between a
verified efficient and a verified inefficient rating (Proposition 5.1), that gap is the energy-efficiency premium β̂1
that Section 4.4 estimates directly, so the model does not produce a new number there. What it adds is the
identification of which price gap governs renovation (not the non-disclosure premium ∆,    ˆ and not ∆  ˆ + c0 , which
is what the definition of the threshold suggests on first reading), the conditions under which that reading fails,
and the counterfactuals of Sections 5.4 and 5.6, which need a model to say what happens to hiding and to the
population of eligible sellers. Second, the instruments in Table 9 act on different margins of the seller’s problem,
and only one of them moves the threshold outside Complete Adverse Selection.

The Cost-Deterred Hiding and Partial Pooling case is the central result of this section, and the mechanism is the
one that Section 5.2 makes visible. In Partial Pooling the marginal informed low-quality seller is exactly indifferent
between hiding and disclosing a low rating, so h = 0 and her best option without renovating is worth a disclosed
low rating. Renovating gains her b and nothing else. The hiding cost has cancelled, because the equilibrium
hiding rate adjusts until the net gain from hiding is zero. In Cost-Deterred Hiding she does not hide and the same
comparison applies. Raising the hiding cost therefore moves the hiding rate along the indifference condition and
leaves the threshold where it was: tightening enforcement against concealment cannot buy renovation in these
regimes, and renovation requires a subsidy that improves the renovate-and-disclose payoff. Complete Adverse
Selection is the textbook case. When every informed low-quality seller hides and hiding pays strictly, renovating
means giving up the rent h, the threshold falls to b − h, and enforcement, by lowering h, raises the threshold one

Inside Partial Pooling the indifference of the marginal hider pins down the belief e∗ , and with it the number of
silent informed sellers. Renovators therefore come out of the stock of sellers who would have disclosed a low
rating. A subsidy buys renovation but not disclosure until that stock is exhausted: the premium does not move,
and the share of the remaining informed low-quality sellers who hide rises. The stock is large. Disclosed poor
ratings are 85 percent of informed low-quality apartment listings and 93 percent of house listings, so up to that
share can renovate at the partial-equilibrium subsidy S ∗ without any effect on hiding. Beyond it the remaining
sellers hide and the threshold falls, so the subsidy needed for full take-up exceeds S ∗ by ψb(θ̄ − e∗ ), which is
at most the width of the Partial Pooling band (Corollary 4.5). At the estimates this correction averages 0.4
percent of b in apartments (e0.5 per m2 ) and 0.9 percent in houses (e1.3), so the partial-equilibrium subsidy
is the general-equilibrium one to within about one percent of the rating premium. Uniqueness follows because
renovation decisions are strategic substitutes through the silent pool: each renovation lowers the number of
disclosed ratings and raises the gain from hiding for those left, which lowers the threshold for the next renovator.
The proposition has an implication that does not rely on any calibrated quantity: in markets where a subsidy
reaches more informed low-quality sellers, the registry hiding rate among those who remain should rise while the
non-disclosure premium stays flat. Testing it requires programme take-up by commune and registry data after the
subsidy.

5.6 Other Instruments and Counterfactuals
The subsidy is not the regulator’s only instrument, and the others act on different margins of the seller’s problem
(Table 9). Enforcement against hiding raises the cost of concealment and changes who hides. Raising the share of
informed sellers π changes how many sellers can renovate at all. Two further instruments act on buyers: making
silence as salient as a rating raises the attention buyers pay to a listing that shows no rating, and full attention
raises the attention they pay to every listing. Three questions follow. Does each instrument move the renovation
threshold R̄, and with it the subsidy a seller needs? Does it change who hides? And would a simple measure such
as making silence salient reduce what the state has to spend on subsidies? The next proposition answers the first
two questions for each instrument, with the hiding cost held at its calibrated value, and the closing subsection
returns to the third.

What the exercises do not show. Three limits qualify the results. The first is that R̄ = b requires the market to
be outside Complete Adverse Selection, which holds at the calibrated hiding cost by construction (Section 5.3).
The second is that the zero effect of salience on renovation is an implication of one hiding cost per market. With
costs that vary across sellers, an informed seller who strictly prefers hiding would lose her rent by renovating
and disclosing, so making hiding less attractive could change her decision; we do not model that margin, and
Section 5.7 bounds what it could change. The third is that the benchmark renovation costs are illustrative. The
close of the exercise is the contrast the model delivers: salience changes who hides and, to first order, not who
renovates, whereas a subsidy changes who renovates and not who hides, until the stock of disclosed poor ratings
is exhausted (Proposition 5.2).

5.7 What Salience Could Save
The result that salience leaves the subsidy unchanged follows from the single hiding cost, and it should not be
read as a finding. With one cost per market a seller in Partial Pooling is indifferent between hiding and disclosing,
so hiding earns her no rent and renovating destroys none. If hiding costs differ across sellers, those whose cost
lies below the gain from hiding earn a strictly positive rent that renovation gives up. Their renovation threshold is
below b and they need a larger subsidy. Making silence salient lowers the gain from hiding, shrinks that rent, and
lowers the subsidy needed to move them, so the saving to the state is the fall in these rents. We do not observe
the distribution of hiding costs and we do not model it. The next proposition bounds the saving using only what
we observe, the hiding rate and the price of silence.

E to G in apartments, 7.0, 8.7 and 11.1 percent, as a gain from hiding that rises with consumption predicts.
Year-by-year hiding rates, once certificate dates are re-pulled, would exploit variation in the gain over time. A
direct measure of what concealment costs sellers would give the distribution itself. Until then the finding is that,
even at the bound, the saving is small against the budget, and the apartment figure rests mostly on one market.

6 Conclusion
Four years after France mandated energy performance disclosure, silence is still rewarded. Properties listed without
an EPC score trade at a premium of 6.9 percent above disclosed low-efficiency properties for apartments and 3.4
percent for houses. Standard unraveling theory predicts the opposite. This paper resolves the puzzle.
The resolution requires two departures from the classical framework, each empirically motivated and individually
insufficient. First, not all sellers are informed. At listing time, only 51.8% of apartment sellers and 41.7% of house
sellers hold a certified energy audit. When buyers observe a missing EPC score, they face genuine ambiguity.
The non-disclosing seller may be concealing an unfavorable rating or may simply not know her property’s energy
performance. Uninformed sellers therefore provide strategic hiders with involuntary cover, preventing silence from
being read as the worst possible signal. Second, not all buyers price energy performance into their offers. When
the share of energy-sensitive buyers is moderate, the revenue penalty from concealment is diluted, making hiding
profitable even against a skeptical pool. Neither departure alone is sufficient. Uninformed sellers keep silence
ambiguous, but buyer heterogeneity ensures that exploiting this ambiguity is financially worthwhile. In apartment
markets a third ingredient is needed, because no belief about silence can price a silent listing above a verified
efficient one: buyers attend to energy much less when a listing shows no rating than when it shows one.
These two forces generate three equilibrium regimes. In the partial-pooling equilibrium that describes the bulk
of French housing markets, the fraction of informed low-quality sellers that hides adjusts endogenously until the
marginal hider is exactly indifferent between concealment and disclosure. This indifference condition delivers the
paper’s central theoretical result. The non-disclosure premium equals exactly the concealment cost in partial
pooling, invariant to the share of informed sellers and the share of energy-sensitive buyers. We identify the
concealment cost directly from the disclosed-price gap and the registry-measured belief, without needing buyer
energy-sensitivity to be separately estimated, and test the indifference prediction against it rather than assume
it. The two segments concur less than an earlier reading of these facts suggested. Concealment costs are 2.4%
of property value for apartments and 3.5% for houses, of the same order across two segments that differ in buyer
energy-sensitivity (49.5% for apartments, 45.8% for houses, identified from an external energy-cost anchor rather
than from the price data used to construct the premium itself); but the indifference prediction itself holds for
houses and is decisively rejected for apartments, where the non-disclosure premium exceeds what the concealment
cost and the registry-measured belief predict by a wide and precisely estimated margin. One attention parameter
per segment, measuring how much buyers attend to energy on listings that show no rating, accounts for both the
rejection in apartments and the fit in houses (Section 4.6). Strategic hiding is itself uncommon. 15.2% of informed
apartment sellers with unfavorable ratings withhold them, versus 6.8% for houses. The non-disclosure premium
survives not because strategic hiding is pervasive but because genuine uninformedness is. The uninformed seller,
who pays no concealment cost yet receives the same premium as the strategic hider, is the residual claimant on


                                                         51
the information rent; the informed hider, in the segment where the model fits, merely breaks even.
The policy implications depart from textbook prescriptions in one consequential way. Although the renovation
threshold mechanically incorporates the concealment cost, the seller’s indifference condition causes that cost to
cancel exactly in partial pooling. The renovation incentive reduces to the verified-quality premium, the price gap
between high-quality and low-quality disclosed properties, and is invariant to both the concealment cost and the
share of informed sellers. Tightening enforcement against non-disclosure therefore does not move the renovation
threshold in the empirically dominant regime. The regulator faces three distinct instruments: renovation subsidies
(closing the gap between the verified-quality premium and the renovation cost), enforcement against strategic
hiding (relevant only outside partial pooling), and audit-infrastructure investment (scaling how many sellers the
other two instruments reach, not the per-seller incentive itself). These act on distinct margins. Limited attention
adds a further instrument: making silence as salient as a rating, which acts on buyers and lowers hiding without
moving the renovation threshold, whereas a higher weight on displayed ratings could raise it, within a bound the
data do not pin down (Section 5.6). A uniform national subsidy exploits none of this structure. The minimum
cost-sharing requirement varies widely, from markets where the private incentive already suffices to markets where
the verified-quality premium is small, and aggregate programme costs among the sellers who can renovate run
to e0.44 billion a year at the light renovation benchmark and e1.72 billion at the medium benchmark, between
9 and 43 percent of the current annual budget of MaPrimeRénov’. Efficient targeting must condition on local
market composition, directing public expenditure to markets where the private renovation gap is largest, with
audit investment scaling how many sellers any subsidy reaches.
The broader lesson extends beyond energy labels. Disclosure mandates discipline strategic silence only when
sellers are actually informed. When a substantial share genuinely does not know their product’s quality, the
mandate produces ambiguity rather than transparency. Involuntary non-disclosers shelter strategic hiders, and
silence cannot be read as bad news. Where buyers do not attend to what a listing leaves out, silence is not
read at all, and making absence visible is a complement to the other instruments. The policy challenge is not to
punish opacity more severely but to produce knowledge in the first place. In housing energy markets, as in any
market where non-disclosure mixes strategy with ignorance, audit-infrastructure investment is the instrument that
expands the reach of every other tool in the regulator’s toolkit.

The Cost Form and Why the Normalisation Binds
Valuations enter prices only through differences across listings. Any amount a buyer subtracts from every listing
alike therefore shifts all her valuations by the same constant and drops out of every price gap. The bonus form
of Section 4.2 uses this normalisation twice:
    it folds the energy cost of an inefficient dwelling, C0 , into the intercept β0 ;
    it writes the insensitive buyer’s valuation as v0 = δ0 + n δn Xn , which in cost form amounts to subtracting

     the same constant C ins from every listing.
Both choices are innocuous in Section 4.2, because each buyer treats every listing the same way: a sensitive buyer
always prices energy, and an insensitive one never does.
The normalisation stops being innocuous as soon as the same buyer may price energy on one listing and not on
another. A buyer who ignores energy on a silent listing but prices it on a rated one subtracts different amounts
from the two. The difference involves the level of what she subtracts when she ignores energy, not only β1 .

All propositions of Section 4.6 are derived under the linear pricing approximation of (Eq. 39). Under the exact

Step 5 (Each subsidy falls by at most min{λ, b, R̂}). The function r 7→ (R̂ − b + min{r, b})+ is non-decreasing,
flat for r ≥ b and for r ≤ b − R̂, with slope one in between. So a fall in r of at most λ (Step 4) lowers it by at
most min{λ, b, R̂}: the slope region has length min{b, R̂}.
Step 6 (The bound). Only the share σ̂0 of sellers has a positive rent (Steps 1 and 4). Averaging Step 5 over
sellers gives the bound σ̂0 min{λ, b, R̂}.
Step 7 (Cases with zero saving).
    Cost-Deterred Hiding. σ̂0 = 0.
    A programme that reaches only the share 1 − σ̂0 of sellers who disclose today. It pays them R̂ − b before
     and after, because their rent is zero.
    One common cost, in Partial Pooling. The rent is zero for every seller.

Alternative Readings of the Policy Exercise
Exact pricing. The exact-pricing solver of Section 4.3 takes the renovation threshold from the full-attention
equilibrium rather than setting it to the rating premium. It gives mean thresholds of e118 (apartments) and
e124 (houses) per m2 , against e122 and e128 under linear pricing, and a share of markets requiring a subsidy at
R̂ = 250 of 98 and 91 percent, against 98 and 90. The thresholds differ by 0.12 (apartments) and 0.18 (houses)
log points on average. The gap is linear versus exact pricing, because attention does not enter the threshold in
the regimes the data support. Figure 20 plots the two thresholds against each other.

Hiding cost read off the premium. Reading the hiding cost off each market’s premium, c0 = ∆        ˆ m , instead of
calibrating it to the observed hiding rate, places 25 apartment markets in Cost-Deterred Hiding, 0 in Partial
Pooling and 20 in Complete Adverse Selection, 9 of them on the branch where high-quality sellers may also hide
and the threshold depends on which equilibrium is selected; the mean threshold is 0.66 of b. For houses the counts
are 45, 11 and 41, with 12 on that branch, and a mean ratio of 0.76. This reading also returns negative costs in
3 apartment and 21 house markets, so we do not use it.
