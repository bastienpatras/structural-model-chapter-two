\leftalignedtitle{When Silence Pays: Mandatory Disclosure and Strategic Concealment in
  Housing Markets
}

\leftalignedauthor{
    Bastien Patras, \href{mailto:bastien.patras@sciencespo.fr}{bastien.patras@sciencespo.fr}$^\dagger$\\
    {\small $^\dagger$PhD Student, Sciences Po Department of Economics, Paris, France}\\
}
\vspace{1em}

\abs{
\textbf{Abstract}\\[0.5em]
Standard unraveling theory predicts that mandatory disclosure separates high-quality from low-quality products: silence signals bad news and non-disclosers should trade at-or-below the worst disclosed category. We document the opposite. Following France's 2021 enforcement of Energy Performance Certificate disclosure, properties listed without a certified score trade at a premium of 7 percentage points above disclosed low-efficiency properties for apartments and 3 for houses. The puzzle resolves because non-disclosure pools informed sellers strategically concealing unfavorable scores with uninformed sellers who genuinely lack a certified audit. Unable to distinguish between them, buyers treat silence as ambiguous rather than damning. We build and structurally estimate a model of the disclosure game, bringing it to the data through a variable unobservable from the buyer's side: private listing data from SeLoger, France's leading residential platform, matched to the ADEME registry of certified energy audits, give us direct observation of each seller's information status at listing time. We derive a closed-form expression for the non-disclosure premium that, in the empirically dominant partial-pooling equilibrium, collapses to the structural concealment cost, invariant to market composition. We extend the framework to endogenous renovation, deriving a local minimum incentive-compatible subsidy and showing that renovation subsidies, enforcement against hiding, and audit-infrastructure investment are complementary instruments acting on distinct margins. We conclude by showing that uniform national programmes are generically inefficient, with cost-shares ranging from zero to nearly 80\% across French housing markets.
}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Standard unraveling theory offers a stark prediction: in markets with verifiable quality information, sellers with good products disclose, silence signals bad news, and disclosure unravels until it is effectively universal \citep{grossman_informational_1981,milgrom_good_1981}. The classical implication is that any seller who fails to disclose should be priced at-or-below the worst disclosed category, since buyers are entitled to expect the worst. The French housing market presents a sharp challenge to this view. Since 2021, France has required sellers to disclose Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) scores in all property listings. Yet four years after implementation, 48.8\% of apartment listings lack a certified energy audit on record, and properties listed without an EPC score trade at a difference-in-differences premium of roughly 7 log points for apartments and 3 log points for houses \emph{above} disclosed low-efficiency (EFG-rated) properties \citep{patras_mandatory_2024}. Of these non-disclosing listings, 3.5\% are strategic hiders: sellers who hold a certified audit but withhold it. The remainder are genuinely uninformed. The market does not punish opacity: it rewards it.

The puzzle is sharpened by the broader empirical record on energy labels in housing markets. A large body of work establishes that energy-efficient properties command meaningful price premia wherever EPCs have been studied: \citet{brounen_kok_2011} document a 2 to 5\% premium for A/B-rated homes in the Netherlands following that country's 2008 mandate; \citet{hyland_lyons_2013} find premia of up to 9\% in Ireland; \citet{fuerst_energy_2016} confirm similar gradients in Wales; \citet{frondel_power_2018} show that Germany's shift from voluntary to mandatory disclosure reduced prices for non-disclosed listings, precisely as classical theory predicts; and \citet{myers_mandatory_2022} demonstrate that mandatory disclosure requirements in the United States increase the capitalization of energy efficiency and stimulate renovation investment. The consensus reading of this literature is that EPC mandates make quality salient, rewarding efficiency and penalizing waste. What none of these papers can explain is why \emph{silence} (the deliberate omission of an EPC score) commands a premium. If anything, it should be the worst signal of all.

The gap lies in a foundational assumption that the classical framework and the EPC literature share: non-disclosure is always a \emph{choice}. A seller who has not yet obtained a certified energy audit cannot disclose, regardless of her property's true performance. \citet{dye_disclosure_1985} first identified this distinction in the context of corporate earnings announcements, showing that when firms may simply lack private information, the market cannot interpret silence as unambiguously bad news. \citet{jung_kwon_1988} formalize the resulting equilibria: partial pooling (in which strategically hiding low-quality sellers shelter behind genuinely uninformed non-disclosers) is generically stable. When buyers observe a missing EPC score, they face irreducible ambiguity: they cannot determine whether the seller is concealing unfavorable information or simply lacks a certified audit. Non-disclosure is therefore an informationally ambiguous event: it pools two economically distinct populations whose relative weight, not the signal itself, determines buyers' posterior about unobserved quality.

Yet Dye-type models are set in corporate finance and accounting, where investors are treated as homogeneous in how much they value the disclosed signal. Housing markets are fundamentally different. A buyer purchasing a vacation property in the south of France, occupied a few weeks per year, may rationally ignore EPC scores; a buyer of a primary residence in northern France with cold winters will price energy costs directly. \citet{loewenstein_disclosure_2014} survey evidence that disclosure interventions systematically fail when recipients are inattentive or heterogeneous in their responses; \citet{benshahar_failure_2011} document this pattern across many regulated markets, from mortgage disclosures to nutritional labels; and \citet{fishman_mandatory_2003} show formally that mandatory disclosure can be ineffective, or even welfare-reducing, when buyers differ in their capacity to process the disclosed information. When a large share of buyers is effectively indifferent to the EPC signal, the revenue penalty from concealment is diluted, sustaining hiding even when buyers hold pessimistic beliefs about the non-disclosing pool.

We formalize this intuition by extending the framework of \citet{dye_disclosure_1985} and \citet{jung_kwon_1988} to the housing-market context. Their key insight, that partial pooling is generically stable when sellers may lack private information, carries directly to the EPC setting, where a large share of sellers genuinely have no certified audit at listing time. We retain their two-quality setting (properties are either energy-efficient or energy-intensive) and the distinction between sellers who hold a certified audit and those who do not, and enrich the framework in one structural direction: heterogeneity in buyer EPC sensitivity. Some buyers, including primary-residence owners in cold climates and investors facing the impending rental ban on low-rated properties, price energy efficiency directly into their offers; others, such as buyers of vacation homes in mild climates, rationally discount the EPC signal. This extension is absent from the corporate-finance setting of Dye-type models and is empirically motivated by extensive evidence that disclosure interventions systematically fail when recipient attention is heterogeneous \citep{loewenstein_disclosure_2014, benshahar_failure_2011, fishman_mandatory_2003}. It is also necessary for identification: the share of energy-sensitive buyers varies across local housing markets and is recovered from the differential price response to the 2021 reform. Together, the local audit coverage rate and the share of energy-sensitive buyers jointly determine whether markets unravel, partially separate, or sustain pooling equilibria with premia to opacity. When both are high, the Milgrom--Grossman benchmark is recovered; for intermediate values, our empirically relevant regime, partial separation with a persistent non-disclosure premium is the generic outcome. A novel implication of the partial-pooling equilibrium, absent from the prior literature, is that the equilibrium premium collapses to a single sufficient statistic: the seller's cost of withholding a certified audit, invariant to market composition. This invariance result, established formally in the equilibrium analysis, is what allows us to identify the concealment cost directly from the observed price gap without separately recovering the audit coverage rate and buyer sensitivity.

A distinctive feature of this paper is the vantage point from which we observe the disclosure game. Testing a theory of strategic concealment requires seeing both sides of the information asymmetry simultaneously: the seller's true information, the seller's strategic choice, and the market's response. Most empirical work in this space sees only one or two of these. Studies of information asymmetry in housing infer agent advantages from equilibrium price deviations \citep{levitt_syverson_2008}; analyses of certification intermediaries characterise incentives without observing individual information endowments \citep{lizzeri_1999}; and work on the energy efficiency gap must proxy for seller awareness through indirect variation in energy prices \citep{myers_asymmetric_2020}. We observe all three dimensions directly. Our dataset combines (i)~residential listing records covering hundreds of thousands of French properties, with listing prices, characteristics, and each seller's disclosure decision; (ii)~transaction records capturing actual sale prices and time-on-market, allowing us to trace the market's response from listing through closing; and (iii)~the ADEME registry of certified energy audits, an official third-party database maintained by France's environment and energy management agency, compiled independently of the listing market. By matching properties across all three sources, we directly observe the latent variable $K$ (whether a seller possesses a certified audit at listing time) that buyers can never see. This lets us classify every listing into one of four empirically meaningful groups: truthful disclosers, strategic hiders (informed sellers with a certified audit who omit it), genuinely uninformed sellers (no audit exists), and the rare misreporters (audit exists but displayed score diverges). The result is a dataset in which the full information structure of the disclosure game is observed, not inferred: a vantage point that, to our knowledge, is unique in the empirical disclosure literature.

We extend the framework to a policy context motivated by the large gap between socially and privately optimal rates of residential energy renovation \citep{allcott_is_2012, gerarden_assessing_2017}. The standard diagnosis points to split incentives, credit constraints, and inattention to future energy costs. We identify a complementary channel rooted in the disclosure game itself. A seller holding a poor energy rating can either renovate and disclose a high rating, earning the quality premium, or forgo publishing and earn the non-disclosure premium instead. The non-disclosure premium therefore functions as an opportunity cost of renovation, raising the private hurdle above what the engineering cost alone would require.

One might expect enforcement against hiding and renovation subsidies to be interchangeable instruments: both reduce the net return to concealment relative to upgrading. We show, however, that this textbook substitutability fails in the empirically dominant equilibrium regime. Because the equilibrium adjusts to enforcement through the fraction of sellers who hide, the renovation threshold (the minimum price improvement that makes upgrading privately worthwhile) depends only on the price gap between disclosed high-quality and disclosed low-quality properties, and is independent of enforcement intensity. This provides a new information-based account of the energy efficiency gap \citep{allcott_is_2012, gerarden_assessing_2017}, one whose policy logic departs from the conventional treatment in \citet{gillingham_palmer_2014}: renovation subsidies, enforcement against hiding, and audit-infrastructure investment act on three distinct margins and are complements rather than substitutes. We quantify these margins across French housing markets and provide a structural account of the documented free-riding in France's renovation tax credits \citep{nauleau_2014}: a uniform national subsidy that ignores the local price gap between quality levels over-compensates markets where private incentives already suffice and under-compensates those where the gap is largest.

\section{Setting and Stylized Facts}
\label{sec:facts}
% ============================================================

This section presents four stylized facts that motivate the theoretical framework developed in Section~\ref{sec:model}. The facts are drawn from a panel of 843{,}621 apartment listings and 687{,}878 house listings covering metropolitan France between July 2021 and December 2024, sourced from SeLoger, France's leading residential listings platform, and matched at the property level to the registry maintained by the \emph{Agence de la Transition \'Ecologique} (ADEME), France's national agency for ecological transition. The ADEME registry is the official, legally authoritative repository of every certified DPE issued in France; because audit certification is centralized there rather than left to the listing platform or the seller, the registry provides an independent, third-party measure of whether each property has been audited at the time of listing. Matching listings against this registry lets us observe directly, for the first time in the empirical disclosure literature, whether each seller is informed about her property's energy performance, separately from whether she chooses to disclose. This constitutes the central data contribution of the paper. Details of data construction, matching procedure, and sample restrictions are deferred to Section~\ref{sec:data}; here we focus on the empirical regularities themselves. Each figure presents apartments and houses side by side. The two segments differ markedly in the level of audit coverage and in the magnitude of the documented effects, but they display the same qualitative patterns, a robustness that we exploit throughout the paper. Each fact is paired with a specific structural feature that the model in Section~\ref{sec:model} must accommodate.

\subsection{Non-Disclosing Listings Command a Price Premium, Widened by the 2021 Reform}
\label{ss:fact1}

The starting puzzle is that silence about energy performance commands a price premium relative to the worst certified scores in both property types. Figure~\ref{fig:price_levels} plots the volume-weighted average of market-level hedonic price indices per square metre for each EPC disclosure category from January 2019 to June 2024, where each index holds property characteristics constant within a local market so that comparisons across disclosure categories reflect differences in the disclosure decision rather than differences in property type or location. For apartments (panel~(a)), non-disclosing listings occupy the highest price tier across the \emph{entire} sample window: the ordering Non-disclosed $>$ Efficient (A/B/C) $>$ Energy-intensive (E/F/G) is visible as early as 2019, well before the reform, and the gap widens sharply after July 2021. For houses (panel~(b)), the pre-reform picture differs: non-disclosers begin near the bottom of the price distribution and rise toward the middle post-reform, reflecting the higher baseline audit coverage in the house segment documented in Section~\ref{ss:fact3}. The pre-reform level premium for apartments is consistent with the concentration of non-audited stock in high-price urban markets; the controlled gap tells a sharper story. Figure~\ref{fig:fact1_premium} plots the hedonic-controlled log price gap between non-disclosing and energy-intensive listings, purging observable composition differences. Before the reform, the controlled gap fluctuates near zero in both segments (a mean of $+0.002$ log points for apartments and $+0.008$ for houses), consistent with buyers treating absent EPCs as equivalent to the worst displayed scores when the DPE carried no legal weight. After July 2021, when DPE certificates became legally opposable under \emph{loi Climat et R\'esilience}, the gap widens monotonically and stabilizes around $+0.044$ log points for apartments and $+0.037$ log points for houses by mid-2024. The reform thus coincides with a sharp widening of both the raw level gap and the composition-adjusted gap in both segments. Classical unraveling predicts the opposite sign: silence should signal bad news and trade \emph{below} verified low-quality listings.

\subsection{Non-Disclosure Persists Long After Mandatory Disclosure}
\label{ss:fact2}

Classical unraveling further predicts that, once disclosure is mandatory and verifiable, the share of non-disclosing listings should converge rapidly toward zero. The French data reject this prediction in both segments, though with different magnitudes. Figure~\ref{fig:fact2_missing} plots the monthly share of listings without a certified EPC from October 2018 through July 2024. For apartments (panel~(a)), the share averages $35.9\%$ before the reform and $33.8\%$ afterward; over the final twelve months of the sample, it stabilizes at $28.9\%$. For houses (panel~(b)), the corresponding numbers are $24.6\%$ pre-reform, $14.4\%$ post-reform, and a final-twelve-month mean of $9.7\%$. Houses thus display better baseline audit coverage and faster post-reform convergence, but in both segments the share remains structurally bounded away from zero more than three years after disclosure became legally required. Almost three-tenths of apartment listings and one-tenth of house listings still come to market without a certified audit, indicating that a substantial fraction of sellers cannot disclose, because they do not possess a certified audit at the time of listing.

\subsection{Direct Decomposition of the Listing Pool by Information Status}
\label{ss:fact3}

A central methodological contribution of this paper is that, by matching every listing against the ADEME registry, we can directly observe whether a seller is informed ($K=1$, audit exists) or uninformed ($K=0$, no audit on record). Crossing this latent information status with the seller's disclosure choice $d \in \{0,1\}$ yields a four-cell classification of which only three cells are economically distinct: truthful disclosers ($K=1, d=1$), strategically hiding sellers ($K=1, d=0$), and genuinely uninformed sellers ($K=0, d=0$).\footnote{The fourth cell ($K=0, d=1$), comprising sellers who display an EPC score without a matched ADEME record, is dominated by registry-match failures rather than genuine misreporting and is excluded from the estimation sample (see Section~\ref{sec:data}).} Figure~\ref{fig:fact3_decomposition} reports the resulting decomposition for both segments. In apartments (panel~(a)), $79.8\%$ of listings are truthful disclosers, $16.7\%$ are genuinely uninformed, and $3.5\%$ are strategically hiding. In houses (panel~(b)), the corresponding shares are $90.4\%$, $8.5\%$, and $1.1\%$. Houses systematically display higher baseline disclosure and a smaller strategic-hiding share, but the qualitative structure is identical: a non-trivial mass of strategic hiders coexists with a much larger mass of genuinely uninformed sellers in both segments.

\noindent Two features of this decomposition are central to the analysis. First, the non-disclosing pool ($d=0$) is mechanically composite in both segments: roughly five-sixths of apartment non-disclosers and seven-eighths of house non-disclosers are genuinely uninformed, while the remainder are strategically concealing. The vast majority of silent listings are silent by constraint, not by choice. Second, the relative weight of these two subgroups varies substantially across property types and, as we show below, across local markets, with direct implications for the equilibrium informativeness of non-disclosure.

\subsection{What the Facts Demand of the Theory}
\label{ss:facts_demand}

Taken together, the four stylized facts impose tight discipline on any candidate theory of EPC disclosure. The documented non-disclosure premium rules out classical full-unraveling models with skeptical buyers, in which silence must trade at or below the worst certified score. The persistence of non-disclosure after the reform rules out frictionless implementations in which enforcement is complete and disclosure is universal at any positive cost of concealment. The composite nature of the non-disclosing pool requires the theory to recognize that silence aggregates two economically distinct subpopulations: informed sellers who choose to conceal unfavorable information, and uninformed sellers who cannot disclose because no certified audit exists. Their joint mass determines the informational content of non-disclosure and cannot be collapsed into a single type. Finally, the substantial cross-market heterogeneity in non-disclosure rates and price gaps rules out a uniform national treatment of these structural parameters: estimation must be conducted market by market. The model developed in the next section is designed to satisfy these four requirements simultaneously.

% ============================================================
\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
% ============================================================

The model is a housing-market application of \citet{dye_disclosure_1985}, as equilibrium-characterized by \citet{jung_kwon_1988}, with one structural addition: heterogeneity in buyer EPC sensitivity ($\rho$). The binary quality space ($\theta\in\{0,1\}$) and seller-information heterogeneity ($\pi$) are adapted directly from those antecedents. The binary quality space has a natural empirical anchor in the rental-ban threshold introduced by \emph{loi Climat et R\'esilience}: properties rated E, F, or G face a progressive ban on new leases, creating a bright-line regulatory discontinuity between low- and high-efficiency categories that maps cleanly onto $\theta\in\{0,1\}$. The results that go beyond Dye--Jung-Kwon are: the closed-form expression for the non-disclosure premium and its invariance to market composition in the partial-pooling regime (Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}), the strict cross-regime ordering of the premium (Eq.~\ref{eq:premium}), and the structural identification strategy that uses the directly-observed audit status $K$ to recover $c_0$ and $\rho$ at the market level.

\subsection{Motivating Intuition}

The two failures interact and reinforce each other. When the informed-seller share and the EPC-sensitive buyer share are both interior, the non-disclosing pool consists of two economically distinct groups: informed sellers who choose to conceal unfavorable information, and uninformed sellers who cannot disclose because no certified audit exists. Buyers observe only whether a score is present; they cannot distinguish between these groups. A higher uninformed-seller share dilutes the adverse inference buyers draw from non-disclosure, raising the expected quality of the non-disclosing pool and making concealment more attractive for informed low-quality sellers. The result is not merely that non-disclosure persists: silence commands a \textit{premium}. Uninformed sellers do not intend to provide cover; they have no choice. But their mandatory presence in the pool is precisely what makes silence ambiguous and concealment privately rational. Critically, the two parameters are independently movable by policy: the informed-seller share can be raised through audit infrastructure investment, the EPC-sensitive buyer share through energy price reforms or buyer education, and each instrument has distinct, characterizable effects on equilibrium outcomes.

\noindent France operates in the bottom-right cell: incomplete audit coverage combined with positive buyer EPC-sensitivity. Matching residential listings to the ADEME registry reveals that 48.8\% of apartment listings had no certified energy audit on record at the time of the reform, placing the informed-seller share well below one. On the demand side, the July 2021 reform rendered EPC scores legally opposable, shifting the effective EPC-sensitive buyer share from near zero (when the DPE was merely informational) to a strictly positive level that varies across markets and climates. Even when the EPC-sensitive buyer share is close to one, as estimated for most apartment markets post-reform, the partial-information condition of incomplete audit coverage is sufficient to sustain the premium: as long as a non-trivial share of non-disclosers are genuinely uninformed, buyers cannot fully penalize silence, and the non-disclosure premium persists in equilibrium. Full unraveling requires both conditions to hold simultaneously; France satisfies only one.

\subsection{Environment and Timing}

A seller owns a property with observable attributes $X$ and an unobservable energy performance level $\theta \in \{0,1\}$, where $\theta=1$ denotes high efficiency. Whether the seller knows $\theta$ depends on whether she has obtained a certified Diagnostic de Performance \'{E}nerg\'{e}tique (DPE) audit prior to listing. With probability $\pi\in(0,1)$, the seller is \emph{informed} ($K=1$) and knows $\theta$; with probability $1-\pi$, she is \emph{uninformed} ($K=0$) and does not. The parameter $\pi$ is not a choice variable: it reflects structural features of local audit markets, including the cost and availability of accredited assessors, the time pressure sellers face (relocation, financial distress, inheritance), and the regulatory requirements in force. It is measured directly from the ADEME registry match, as described in Section~\ref{sec:data}.

The seller simultaneously chooses a disclosure decision $d\in\{0,1\}$ and a listing price $p^{\text{list}}$. French property law requires that all mandatory diagnostic documents, including the DPE, be appended to the sales agreement at signing (loi ALUR, Article L271-4 CCH), so the disclosure decision and the listing price are set jointly at the time the property is placed on the market. This institutional timing motivates the simultaneous-choice formulation: the seller does not first decide whether to disclose and then price, but sets a complete strategy that internalizes how the disclosure decision shifts buyer beliefs and thereby affects both the probability of sale and the expected transaction price.

\noindent\textbf{Assumption (No Falsification).} \textit{Disclosed EPC scores are verifiable against the ADEME registry. An informed seller with $K=1$ may choose $d\in\{0,1\}$, while an uninformed seller with $K=0$ is constrained to $d=0$: she cannot disclose what she does not possess. This rules out costless misreporting.}

\medskip
The key friction is that buyers observe $(d, p^{\text{list}}, X)$ but not the seller's information status $K$: they cannot distinguish a seller who chose not to disclose from one who could not. A buyer then arrives and decides whether to purchase at $p^{\text{list}}$. With probability $\rho\in(0,1)$, the buyer is \emph{EPC-sensitive} and incorporates energy performance into her valuation; with probability $1-\rho$, the buyer is \emph{EPC-insensitive} and conditions her bid solely on observable attributes. Sellers cannot observe buyer type ex ante and optimize against expected demand. The parameter $\rho$ is market-specific, reflecting the local composition of the buyer pool, and is identified from the differential price response to the July 2021 reform across markets, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:identification}.

\subsection{Payoffs and Hiding Costs}

In French residential markets, listing prices serve as anchors for negotiation rather than posted take-it-or-leave-it offers: the transaction price conditional on a sale typically falls below the listing price by a margin that depends on local market conditions and property characteristics.\footnote{The negotiation margin in France is empirically small: the majority of properties transact at or very near list price, and the average discount rarely exceeds three to five percent. \citet{merlo_bargaining_2004} document similar patterns for England, showing that most residential transactions involve little or no negotiation once a listing price is set. \citet{genesove_loss_2001} provide evidence that list prices act as anchors that constrain final transaction prices. For France specifically, \citet{friggit_marche_2013} documents that the average negotiation margin has remained narrow and declining, with a large share of properties selling without any price reduction.} Buyer arrival and match quality are stochastic, so the seller faces uncertainty over both whether a sale occurs and, conditional on a sale, at what price. The seller's problem therefore separates into a transaction probability $\Gamma$ and an expected transaction price $\phi$, each depending on the listing price and disclosure decision:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\{d,\,p^{\text{list}}\}} \;\Pi = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[p^{\text{trans}}\mid d,p^{\text{list}},X\right]}_{\phi(p^{\text{list}})} \cdot \underbrace{\Pr(T{=}1\mid d,p^{\text{list}},X)}_{\Gamma(p^{\text{list}})} - C(\theta,d)
    \label{eq:payoff}
\end{equation}
where $\phi(p^{\text{list}}) = \gamma_0+\gamma_1 \cdot p^{\text{list}}+\sum_n\gamma_n X_n$ is the expected transaction price conditional on a sale occurring, and $C(\theta,d)=\mathbf{1}[d=0]\cdot(c_0+\theta c_1)$ is the hiding cost, paid only when the seller withholds information.

The hiding cost has two components. The term $c_0\geq 0$ applies to all non-disclosers, regardless of quality. In the French institutional context, $c_0$ captures the regulatory burden imposed by loi ALUR (legal exposure if a buyer subsequently discovers a concealed audit via the public ADEME registry, notaire liability checks, and the informational ambiguity that prolongs negotiation and increases sale failure risk). The term $c_1\geq 0$ is an additional opportunity cost borne exclusively by high-quality sellers ($\theta=1$) who choose to hide: by not disclosing, they forgo the revenue premium that verified high quality would command from EPC-sensitive buyers. This asymmetry, $c_1>0$, is economically decisive: it means that hiding is strictly cheaper for low-quality sellers than for high-quality ones, so the net gain from concealment is larger for $\theta=0$ sellers at any given pool belief. This differential is the sorting pressure that drives full separation of high-quality sellers in equilibrium (Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose}). Hiding costs are paid unconditionally on sale: they represent compliance burdens and reputational exposure that arise at listing time, not at closing. A note on the dual role of $c_0$ is in order. As a structural primitive, $c_0$ is an institutional cost of concealment that we treat as fixed within the prevailing enforcement regime and recover from the equilibrium price gap (Section~\ref{sec:identification}); as a policy object, it is the margin a regulator shifts through stricter enforcement, and we study counterfactual variations in $c_0$ as such in Section~\ref{sec:policy}. The two uses are consistent: estimation reads $c_0$ off the current regime, while the policy exercise perturbs it around that baseline.

\subsection{Buyer Valuations and Demand}

The partition of buyers into EPC-sensitive and EPC-insensitive types reflects a well-documented empirical regularity: willingness to pay for energy efficiency varies sharply with how much the buyer will bear the ongoing energy costs. Primary-residence buyers in northern France with long heating seasons, and investor-buyers who face the impending F/G rental ban under the \textit{loi Climat et R\'{e}silience}, have strong incentives to incorporate EPC scores into their bids. Vacation-home buyers who occupy the property a few weeks per year, and buyers in mild climates, may rationally discount energy performance. This heterogeneity determines $\rho$, the share of the local buyer pool that is EPC-sensitive.

EPC-sensitive buyers ($B=1$) condition their valuation on energy performance:
\begin{equation}
    v_1(\theta,d) = \begin{cases}
        \beta_0 + \beta_1\cdot\theta + \textstyle\sum_n\beta_n X_n & \text{if } d=1 \;\text{(verified quality)},\\
        \beta_0 + \beta_1\cdot\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid d{=}0] + \textstyle\sum_n\beta_n \cdot X_n & \text{if } d=0 \;\text{(inferred quality)}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
We adopt the benchmark $\alpha_k=\beta_k$: buyers value quality equally whether it is certified or inferred. This is a conservative assumption in a precise sense: under the alternative $\alpha_k<\beta_k$ (a distrust discount on unverified quality), the non-disclosure premium would be smaller for any given pool composition, so our identified $\rho$ is a lower bound on the true EPC-sensitive buyer share. The non-disclosure premium we document therefore understates what a fully skeptical buyer would generate. EPC-insensitive buyers ($B=0$) value the property solely on observables: $v_0(X)=\delta_0+\sum_n\delta_n X_n$. The aggregate transaction probability mixes the two buyer types:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma(p^{\text{list}}) = \rho\cdot G\!\left(v_1(d)-p^{\text{list}}\right) + (1-\rho)\cdot G\!\left(v_0-p^{\text{list}}\right)
    \label{eq:demand}
\end{equation}
where $G(\cdot)$ is the logistic CDF governing idiosyncratic taste shocks. The parameter $\rho$ enters the demand function as a weight on the EPC-sensitive buyer valuation: a higher $\rho$ amplifies the revenue difference between a disclosed high-quality listing and a non-disclosing listing, raising the stakes of the disclosure decision for the seller. It is this amplification channel through which $\rho$ governs disclosure incentives. Empirically, $\rho$ is a market-level structural parameter identified from the cross-market variation in the price response to the July 2021 reform, as developed in Section~\ref{sec:identification}.

\subsection{Buyer Beliefs and the Non-Disclosing Pool}

When observing a non-disclosing listing, buyers cannot determine whether the seller is strategically concealing an unfavorable audit or simply lacks a certified EPC. They form a posterior belief about expected quality using Bayes' rule. The structure of this belief depends on the composition of the non-disclosing pool, which is itself an equilibrium object.

\subsubsection{Who Hides?}

Each seller is characterized by two attributes: information status $K \in \{0,1\}$ and quality $\theta \in \{0,1\}$. We maintain the following assumption throughout.

\begin{assumption}[Independence]\label{ass:independence}
$K \perp \theta$, with $\Pr(K=1) = \pi$ and $\Pr(\theta=1) = \bar{\theta}$.
\end{assumption}

\noindent Independence is most plausible for transactions driven by exogenous life events (succession following a death, court-ordered liquidation, or divorce settlement), in which legal proceedings impose binding timing constraints that preclude any quality-contingent decision to obtain a certified EPC. In such cases, the seller's information status $K$ is determined by administrative capacity and procedural deadlines rather than the energy performance of the dwelling; a seller under a judicial mandate to liquidate an estate has neither the incentive nor the discretion to condition audit acquisition on $\theta$. In France, forced transactions of this type constitute a substantial share of the resale market, providing a natural mechanism through which $K \perp \theta$ obtains in the data.

\subsubsection{The Belief Function}

Applying Bayes' rule to the pool composition in Table~\ref{tab:who_hides}, the expected quality among non-disclosers is:
\begin{equation}
    e(\sigma_0, \sigma_1) \equiv \Pr(\theta=1 \mid d=0) = \frac{\pi \bar{\theta} \sigma_1 + (1-\pi) \bar{\theta}}{\pi \bar{\theta} \sigma_1 + \pi (1-\bar{\theta}) \sigma_0 + (1-\pi)}
    \label{eq:belief_general}
\end{equation}

The numerator counts the high-quality mass in the pool: informed high-quality sellers who choose to hide ($\pi\bar{\theta}\sigma_1$) together with uninformed sellers who happen to have high quality ($(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}$). The denominator is the total non-disclosing mass. The belief $e$ is therefore a weighted average across two economically distinct populations. When uninformed sellers dominate the pool ($\pi$ small or $\sigma_0$ small), non-disclosure looks like a constraint rather than a choice and beliefs remain favorable to the seller. When strategic low-quality hiders dominate ($\pi$ large and $\sigma_0$ large), the pool is contaminated and beliefs deteriorate. This is why unraveling is incomplete: non-disclosure is inherently ambiguous when $\pi < 1$, whereas in classical disclosure theory all sellers are informed and silence is unambiguous bad news.

\begin{lemma}[Belief Monotonicity]\label{lem:belief_monotone}
$e(\sigma_0,\sigma_1)$ is strictly decreasing in $\sigma_0$ and strictly increasing in $\sigma_1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Increasing $\sigma_0$ raises the denominator (more low-quality hiders enter the pool) while leaving the numerator unchanged, so $e$ falls. Increasing $\sigma_1$ raises the numerator and the denominator by the same term $\pi\bar{\theta}$, and since $e < 1$ the numerator effect dominates, so $e$ rises. The strict monotonicity in $\sigma_0$ is the self-correcting force that ensures equilibrium uniqueness in Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium}: as more informed low-quality sellers choose to hide, buyer beliefs worsen, attenuating the benefit of hiding and restoring incentive compatibility.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Disclosure Incentives}

The seller's disclosure decision turns on a single comparison: whether the price attainable by withholding the audit result, $p^*_{d=0}$, priced off the pool belief $e$, exceeds the price attainable by disclosing true quality $\theta$. A low-quality seller ($\theta=0$) gains from non-disclosure by pooling with uninformed sellers and extracting the associated quality premium; a high-quality seller ($\theta=1$) loses from non-disclosure because pooling with low-quality hiders depresses the belief $e$ below 1. This asymmetry drives the central result.

\begin{proposition}[Disclosure Incentives and Seller Sorting]
\label{prop:premium}
Under the benchmark $\alpha_k=\beta_k$, a seller of type $(K,\theta)$ strictly prefers disclosure if and only if $\theta > e(\sigma_0^*)$. In particular:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item An informed seller with $\theta=1$ strictly prefers disclosure for any $e(\sigma_0^*)<1$.
    \item An informed seller with $\theta=0$ strictly prefers non-disclosure whenever $e(\sigma_0^*)>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

Proposition~\ref{prop:premium} reflects a standard adverse-selection asymmetry. For a high-quality seller, disclosure reveals quality exactly, whereas non-disclosure pools her with low-quality hiders; the condition $e(\sigma_0^*)<1$ is satisfied for any non-degenerate pool and so is always binding. For a low-quality seller, concealment allows her to price off the pool belief $e(\sigma_0^*)>0$, which is strictly greater than her true quality $\theta=0$; the gain from pooling is therefore strictly positive whenever uninformed sellers are present. The additional hiding cost $c_1>0$ borne by high-quality concealers reinforces but does not drive this separation under the benchmark $\alpha_k=\beta_k$: the pool-inference argument alone is sufficient to induce full separation of high-quality sellers, with $c_1$ becoming binding only when $\alpha_k < \beta_k$ (the distrust discount case).

\begin{corollary}[High-Quality Sellers Always Disclose]
\label{cor:hq_disclose}
In any equilibrium, $\sigma_1^*=0$.
\end{corollary}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} Follows directly from Proposition~\ref{prop:premium}(i). $\Box$

Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose} is a partial unraveling result: the top of the quality distribution fully separates. An informed high-quality seller gains nothing from ambiguity and is strictly harmed by being pooled with low-quality hiders; she therefore always discloses. The non-disclosing pool is consequently free of high-quality informed sellers in any equilibrium, and the strategic question reduces entirely to the behavior of informed low-quality sellers.

Since $\sigma_1^*=0$, the equilibrium belief simplifies. Defining $\sigma_0\equiv\Pr(d=0\mid K=1,\theta=0)$, the non-disclosing pool consists exclusively of uninformed sellers of both quality types and informed low-quality hiders:
\begin{equation}
    e(\sigma_0) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0 + (1-\pi)}
    \label{eq:belief_simplified}
\end{equation}
The belief $e(\sigma_0)$ is strictly decreasing in $\sigma_0$: each additional low-quality hider enters the denominator with weight $\pi(1-\bar{\theta})$ while leaving the numerator unchanged, diluting pool quality and depressing buyer beliefs.

\subsection{Equilibrium Characterization}

Let $\Delta(e)\equiv\rho\cdot\beta_1\cdot e$ denote the revenue gain from non-disclosure for a low-quality seller facing pool belief $e$. Define the net hiding gain:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\sigma_0) \;\equiv\; \Delta\!\left(e(\sigma_0)\right) - c_0 \;=\; \frac{\rho\,\beta_1\,(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0 + (1-\pi)} - c_0.
    \label{eq:Phi}
\end{equation}
The function $\Phi$ is strictly decreasing in $\sigma_0$: as more informed low-quality sellers conceal, pool quality $e$ deteriorates, the gain from hiding falls, and the incentive to conceal dissipates. This monotone feedback delivers a unique equilibrium without reliance on equilibrium refinements. The three equilibrium regimes follow directly from comparing $\Phi$ to zero at the two boundary values: $\Phi(0)=\Delta(\bar{\theta})-c_0$ measures the net gain when the pool is at its cleanest, and $\Phi(1)=\Delta(e(1))-c_0$ when it is maximally contaminated.

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium Existence and Uniqueness]
\label{prop:equilibrium}
There exists a unique equilibrium, characterized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item If $c_0>\Delta(\bar{\theta})$, the unique equilibrium is \emph{Cost-Deterred Hiding}: $\sigma_0^*=0$ and $e^*=\bar{\theta}$.
    \item If $c_0<\Delta(e(1))$, the unique equilibrium is \emph{Complete Adverse Selection}: $\sigma_0^*=1$ and $e^*=(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}/(1-\pi\bar{\theta})$.
    \item If $\Delta(e(1))\leq c_0\leq\Delta(\bar{\theta})$, the unique equilibrium is \emph{Partial Pooling}: $\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$ is the unique solution to $\Delta(e(\sigma_0))=c_0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

\centering

\label{tab:equilibria}

\begin{tabular}{lcccc}

\midrule

Neither corner solution fits the data: universal concealment would require the non-disclosure premium to exceed $c_0$ even against the most contaminated pool, while universal disclosure would require concealment to be unprofitable even against the cleanest. What we observe instead is an interior equilibrium in which some low-quality sellers disclose and the remainder pool with uninformed non-disclosers. Substituting~\eqref{eq:Phi} into $\Phi(\sigma_0^*)=0$ yields the closed-form hiding rate:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_0^* = \frac{1}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})}\left[\frac{\rho\,\beta_1\,(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{c_0} - (1-\pi)\right].
    \label{eq:pp_sigma}
\end{equation}

We derive the non-disclosure premium as follows:
\begin{equation}
    p_0^*-p_1^* \;=\;
    \begin{cases}
        \rho\beta_1\bar{\theta}
            & \text{Cost-Deterred Hiding }(\sigma_0^*=0), \\[4pt]
        c_0
            & \text{Partial Pooling }(\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)), \\[4pt]
        \rho\beta_1\dfrac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}
            & \text{Complete Adverse Selection }(\sigma_0^*=1),
    \end{cases}
    \quad \text{with}\quad \rho\beta_1\frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}} < c_0 < \rho\beta_1\bar{\theta}.
    \label{eq:premium}
\end{equation}

The three equilibria characterized in Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium} arise from a single comparison: the hiding gain $\Delta(e)=\rho\beta_1 e$ evaluated at the two extremes of pool contamination against the structural hiding cost $c_0$. When the gain is dominated by $c_0$ even at the cleanest possible pool ($\sigma_0=0$, $e=\bar{\theta}$), no informed seller hides; when it survives the most contaminated pool ($\sigma_0=1$, $e=(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}/(1-\pi\bar{\theta})$), every informed seller hides; in between, an interior fraction hides and the system self-equilibrates. The non-disclosure premium inherits a strict ordering across regimes,
\begin{equation*}
    \underbrace{\rho\beta_1 \bar{\theta}}_{\text{Cost-Deterred Hiding}}
    \;>\;
    \underbrace{c_0}_{\text{Partial Pooling}}
    \;>\;
    \underbrace{\rho\beta_1\,\dfrac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}}_{\text{Complete Adverse Selection}},
\end{equation*}
with the interior value depending neither on $\pi$ nor on $\rho$.

In the \emph{Cost-Deterred Hiding} regime, the hiding cost is prohibitive even against the cleanest conceivable non-disclosing pool, the one populated entirely by uninformed sellers drawn from the unconditional quality distribution. No informed low-quality seller finds it profitable to suppress her type, so $\sigma_0^*=0$ and the non-disclosing pool is uncontaminated by strategic behavior: $e^*=\bar{\theta}$. The premium $\rho\beta_1\bar{\theta}$ reflects the full unconditional quality belief, scaled by the share of EPC-sensitive buyers and their marginal valuation. Relative to \citet{milgrom_good_1981} and \citet{grossman_informational_1981}, this regime delivers a form of deterrence (informed bad types refrain from concealment) but through a fundamentally different mechanism: unraveling in those models is sustained by a belief-penalty channel, in which silence triggers the worst possible inference, whereas here deterrence runs through the cost channel $c_0$ because uninformed sellers prevent silence from carrying the worst-type inference. This is the only regime in which the non-disclosing pool is informationally clean.

In the \emph{Complete Adverse Selection} regime, the hiding gain survives the most contaminated pool the model can sustain. Every informed low-quality seller hides, $\sigma_0^*=1$, and buyer beliefs about the non-disclosing pool collapse to the floor $e^*=(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}/(1-\pi\bar{\theta})$, strictly below $\bar{\theta}$. The non-disclosure premium accordingly hits its lower bound. This lower bound is nonetheless strictly positive: the uninformed seller mass dilutes the adverse inference and prevents the complete belief collapse that Milgrom--Grossman unraveling would imply. The mechanism is exactly the one identified by \citet{dye_disclosure_1985}, in which a residual silent population that is not strategically motivated keeps silence partially informative, and it operates here even when strategic hiders saturate.

In the \emph{Partial Pooling} regime, $\Phi(\sigma_0)$ is positive at $\sigma_0=0$ and negative at $\sigma_0=1$. Strict monotonicity yields a unique interior crossing $\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$ at which the marginal hider is exactly indifferent: $\Delta(e(\sigma_0^*))=c_0$. The underlying mechanism is a feedback loop. As more informed low-quality sellers join the non-disclosing pool, $e(\sigma_0)$ falls, perceived pool quality deteriorates, and the gain $\Delta(e)$ from hiding shrinks. Entry into the pool stops precisely when this gain has been driven down to the hiding cost. The fraction $\sigma_0^*$ of low-quality types hides and pools with the uninformed; the remaining $1-\sigma_0^*$ disclose, accepting the low-quality price rather than continuing to contaminate the pool.

The structural payoff of this regime is that the equilibrium premium equals exactly $c_0$, invariant to $\pi$ and $\rho$ throughout the interior. The reason is the indifference condition itself: in equilibrium, the price difference between the non-disclosing pool and the disclosed low-quality outcome must equal the hiding cost, otherwise the marginal seller would strictly prefer one action over the other. To see the invariance, consider a rise in $\pi$. A larger informed share would, holding $\sigma_0^*$ fixed, mechanically contaminate the pool and depress $e$. Equilibrium restores $\Phi=0$ by reducing $\sigma_0^*$: fewer hiders produce a cleaner pool, $e$ rebounds, and the premium returns to $c_0$. An analogous adjustment absorbs changes in $\rho$. The non-disclosure premium therefore functions as a revealed hiding cost rather than a free function of market composition, and the observable price gap between non-disclosing and disclosing low-quality sellers directly identifies $c_0$. This is the only regime in which $\pi$, $\rho$, and $c_0$ are jointly identified.

\subsection{Comparative Statics}

\begin{proposition}[Comparative Statics]
\label{prop:comparative_statics}
The following comparative statics hold:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item In the \emph{Complete Adverse Selection} equilibrium: $\partial(p_0^*-p_1^*)/\partial\pi < 0$ and $\partial(p_0^*-p_1^*)/\partial\rho > 0$.
    \item In the \emph{Partial Pooling} equilibrium: $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial c_0 < 0$, $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial\rho > 0$, and $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial\pi < 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

\begin{itemize}
    \item Part~(i) reflects two distinct transmission channels. The effect of $\pi$ operates through the \textit{belief channel}: as the share of informed sellers rises, non-disclosure becomes more informative of low quality, $e^*$ falls, and the gain from concealment dissipates. The effect of $\rho$ operates through the \textit{revenue channel}: a larger share of EPC-sensitive buyers widens the price spread between quality levels, raising the value of concealment.
    \item Part~(ii) follows from the implicit function theorem applied to $\Phi(\sigma_0^*)=0$. Since $\Phi'(\sigma_0^*)<0$, one obtains $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial c_0 < 0$ directly. The effect $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial\rho > 0$ follows because a higher $\rho$ increases $\Delta$ at any fixed $e$, shifting $\Phi$ upward and raising the interior equilibrium. The effect of $\pi$ operates through the belief function: a higher $\pi$ increases the contamination weight $\pi(1-\bar{\theta})$ in the denominator of $e(\sigma_0)$, reducing $\Delta(e(\sigma_0))$ and thereby shifting $\Phi$ downward, so $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial\pi < 0$. The parameters $\pi$ and $c_0$ are therefore \textit{policy substitutes}: each reduces strategic hiding through a distinct channel ($\pi$ through beliefs, $c_0$ through costs), and their marginal effects on $\sigma_0^*$ are both negative and quantifiable from the estimated model.
\end{itemize}

\begin{corollary}[Effect of $\rho$ on the non-disclosure premium]
\label{cor:rho}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item The non-disclosure premium $\Delta = p_0^* - p_1^*$ is weakly increasing in $\rho$: strictly so in the Cost-Deterred Hiding and Complete Adverse Selection regimes ($\partial\Delta/\partial\rho > 0$), and zero in the Partial Pooling regime ($\partial\Delta/\partial\rho = 0$, with the premium pinned at $c_0$).
    \item The parameter interval sustaining the Partial Pooling regime, $[\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1),\,\rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}]$, is weakly expanding in $\rho$: its width $\rho\,\beta_1[\bar{\theta}-e(1)]$ is strictly increasing in $\rho$ whenever $\pi>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium effect of $\pi$]
\label{prop:pi}
As $\pi$ increases, the lower boundary $\Delta(e(1))$ of the Partial Pooling interval falls ($\partial e(1)/\partial\pi < 0$), so the condition for Complete Adverse Selection ($c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$) becomes harder to satisfy and the equilibrium moves toward the Cost-Deterred Hiding regime. For any fixed hiding cost $c_0$, a sufficiently large increase in $\pi$ drives the equilibrium out of the Partial Pooling region toward full unraveling.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium effect of $c_0$]
\label{prop:c0}
An increase in $c_0$ shifts $\Phi(\sigma_0) = \Delta(e(\sigma_0)) - c_0$ uniformly downward. The upper boundary condition for Partial Pooling ($c_0 \leq \Delta(\bar{\theta})$) becomes harder to satisfy and the condition for Cost-Deterred Hiding ($c_0 > \Delta(\bar{\theta})$) becomes easier, so higher hiding costs push the equilibrium toward the Cost-Deterred Hiding regime. In the interior of the Partial Pooling regime, $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial c_0 < 0$: higher costs reduce the equilibrium hiding rate.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

The premium in~\eqref{eq:premium} generates a transfer from EPC-sensitive buyers to the non-disclosing pool, but it does not accrue symmetrically within that pool. The informed low-quality hider receives the premium but nets zero: the indifference condition $\Delta(e(\sigma_0^*))=c_0$ ensures that the gross revenue gain from concealment is exactly consumed by the hiding cost. The uninformed seller, who bears no hiding cost, is the residual claimant and pockets the full premium $c_0$ as a pure rent. The welfare cost of the equilibrium therefore has two distinct components. First, an allocative distortion: EPC-sensitive buyers pay a positive premium for assets whose true energy performance falls below the pooled belief $e(\sigma_0^*)$, a misallocation that would be eliminated if the concealed information were transmitted. Second, a deadweight loss: the hiding cost $c_0$ is incurred by informed sellers at listing time without any counterpart in buyer valuations. This is a social loss only to the extent that $c_0$ represents real resources destroyed (legal fees, prolonged negotiation, administrative burden); any portion that is a transfer, such as the expected value of a fine accruing to the state, is a redistribution rather than a loss. We do not identify this split, so the deadweight-loss component is an upper bound. A formal efficiency characterization of these two components and the optimal subsidy that addresses them is developed in Section~\ref{sec:policy}.

\subsection{Sources and Sample Construction}

The empirical analysis draws on three sources matched at the listing level. Listing records are sourced from SeLoger, a major French residential real-estate platform covering sales across metropolitan France. For each listing, we observe the listing price, property characteristics ($X_i$: area, number of rooms, floor, construction era, location), the disclosure indicator $d\in\{0,1\}$, and, when $d=1$, the displayed EPC letter (A through G). Transaction records allow us to connect listings to realized sale prices and time-on-market. The ADEME registry is the official national database of certified Diagnostic de Performance Énergétique (DPE) audits, maintained by France's environment and energy management agency and reported by accredited third-party assessors. We match listings to the ADEME registry using address, property type, area, and floor, assigning to each listing an indicator $K=1$ if a certified audit record exists at the time of listing, and $K=0$ otherwise. For the structural estimation, listings for which no address is available are dropped, as the ADEME match requires a geocoded address; these account for a small share of the total sample.

The full listing sample covers 2021Q3 to 2024Q4, the post-reform period during which EPC disclosure is legally binding and DPE scores are credible to buyers. The apartment sample comprises 843,621 listings and the house sample 687,878 listings. For structural estimation we restrict to 2022 to 2024: the H2~2021 period (the six months immediately following the reform) is excluded because the market had not yet fully repriced the DPE (hedonic estimates of $\hat\beta_1$ are near zero or negative in that window), yielding 767,000 apartment listings and 633,000 house listings for the structural sample. For 51.2\% of apartment listings and 40.3\% of house listings, the ADEME match yields $K=1$; the remainder are classified as genuinely uninformed ($K=0$, no verified audit on record). For identification of market-level price indices, we use the panel of hedonic price indices constructed in \citet{patras_mandatory_2024}, covering 110 markets (attraction zones, \textit{zones d'emploi}) with monthly observations from October 2018 through July 2024.

\section{Identification}
\label{sec:identification}
% ============================================================

The two structural objects to identify are $\rho_m$, the market-specific share of EPC-sensitive buyers, and $c_{0,m}$, the seller hiding cost. The structural equation $\Delta_m = \rho_m \cdot \beta_{1,m} \cdot e^*_m$ relates these to observables: $\Delta_m$ and $\beta_{1,m}$ are equilibrium price gaps, and $e^*_m$ is directly observed from the ADEME registry. This might appear to make $\rho_m$ point-identified from a single cross-section via $\rho_m = \Delta_m / (\beta_{1,m} \cdot e^*_m)$. The problem is that cross-sectional price gaps reflect \emph{level} differences across markets, not the demand-induced component we need. Any market characteristic that simultaneously shapes the composition of the undisclosed pool and is correlated with buyer EPC-sensitivity would bias a cross-sectional estimate of $\rho_m$. Purging this confound requires variation that isolates the \emph{change} in price gaps attributable to buyers becoming EPC-sensitive, not their levels.

The July 2021 DPE reform provides this variation, and the two-step approach uses it as follows. In the first step, a difference-in-differences estimator compares price gaps in each market before and after the reform, stripping out market-specific level differences and isolating the reform-induced shift in buyer demand locally. This yields $\hat\rho_m = \hat\Delta_m / (\hat\beta_{1,m} \cdot \hat{e}^*_m)$, where the hat quantities are DiD estimates (post minus pre period means). In the second step, the hiding cost $c_{0,m}$ is recovered from the equilibrium hiding rate $\hat\sigma_{0,m}$ via a closed-form inversion of the seller indifference condition (given the equilibrium characterized in Section~\ref{sec:model}). No additional external variation is required: $\hat\sigma_{0,m}$ is directly observed as the fraction of informed low-quality sellers who withhold their EPC, and the inversion is exact. Together, three observables $(\hat\beta_{1,m},\,\hat\Delta_m,\,\hat\sigma_{0,m})$ and one registry-based input $(\hat{e}^*_m)$ map to two structural parameters $(\hat\rho_m,\,\hat{c}_{0,m})$, market by market.

\subsection{The July 2021 Reform as a Natural Experiment}

Before July 2021, the French DPE was a paper exercise. Scores were methodologically inconsistent across assessors, carried no legal weight, and were routinely ignored in negotiations: a buyer who later discovered a disclosed rating was misleading had no legal recourse. The reform of July 1, 2021 changed the regime fundamentally. It standardized the methodology, imposed stricter accreditation requirements on assessors, and, most importantly, rendered disclosed EPC ratings legally opposable: a buyer can now use a disclosed rating as grounds for price renegotiation or legal action. The DPE became, for the first time, a document with contractual force.

This shift is the source of identification: it created a precise, legally documented change in the value of a disclosed EPC score to buyers, with a known implementation date of July 1, 2021. Crucially, the reform operated on the \emph{demand} side --- the weight a disclosed rating carries in buyers' valuations and legal recourse --- and not on the seller's cost of withholding. The obligation to provide a DPE at sale, and the compliance and liability exposure attached to coming to market without the required diagnostic, were already in force under loi ALUR (Article L271-4 CCH) well before 2021. These are precisely the components of the concealment cost $c_{0,m}$. What July 2021 changed was not whether a seller must disclose, nor the cost she bears for withholding, but how much a \emph{disclosed} rating now matters to the buyer. The reform therefore shifts $\rho_m$ while leaving $c_{0,m}$, governed by the pre-existing disclosure mandate, unchanged across the reform date. This is what licenses reading the DiD change in the premium as the demand response that identifies $\rho_m$, holding the hiding cost fixed. Whether the supply side of the market responded concurrently is a maintained assumption we cannot directly test with the data at hand; we return to it below.\footnote{An alternative strategy based on a Bartik instrument (interacting wholesale gas price variation with the pre-reform share of low-efficiency properties per market) fails at the first stage ($F$-statistic: 1.0 to 1.6). The French \emph{bouclier tarifaire} (energy price shield, October 2021 to 2023) decoupled household energy costs from the TTF gas price spike driven by the Russian invasion of Ukraine, removing the demand-side variation the instrument was designed to exploit.}

The identifying assumption is the standard parallel-trends condition: in the absence of the reform, the cross-group price gaps $\beta_{1,m,t}$ (energy-efficient vs.\ energy-intensive disclosed properties) and $\Delta_{m,t}$ (undisclosed vs.\ energy-intensive) would have continued on their pre-reform trajectory. The DiD does not require that buyers were entirely inattentive to EPC scores before July 2021; it requires only that their relative pricing of disclosure groups was not already trending. Any post-reform shift in the gaps, relative to that pre-reform baseline, is then attributable to the change in buyer incentives induced by legal opposability.

The pre-trend evidence supports this assumption directly, and more strongly than a single test would. Both $\beta_{1,m,t}$ and $\Delta_{m,t}$ are statistically flat across all markets in the 33 months before the reform (Figure~\ref{fig:ATT}). These two gaps measure distinct economic objects: $\beta_{1,m,t}$ captures buyers' willingness to pay for verified high quality, while $\Delta_{m,t}$ reflects the informational wedge created by strategic non-disclosure. They respond to different primitives of the model. A supply-side confound (a shift in $\pi_m$, $\bar\theta_m$, or seller composition at the reform date) would generically move these two gaps by different amounts and in potentially different directions, since the mechanisms through which supply composition enters each gap differ. The fact that both gaps are jointly flat before July 2021 and both shift upward simultaneously after the reform, at the same markets and on the same calendar date, is difficult to reconcile with a supply-side story. It is exactly what the demand-side interpretation predicts: legal opposability increases the return to EPC quality for sensitive buyers, raising both the price of verified high quality ($\beta_{1,m,t}$) and the information premium extracted by non-disclosers ($\Delta_{m,t}$) at the same moment.

We acknowledge that supply-side stability (the assumption that $\pi_m$, $\bar\theta_m$, and $c_{0,m}$ did not shift discontinuously at the reform) is maintained rather than directly tested. Our listing data begin in 2021Q3, so pre-reform ADEME match rates are not observable. The timing of audit decisions provides some reassurance: certified DPE audits are commissioned and completed before listing, with a gap of weeks to months, so the audit stock at any listing date reflects decisions made prior to that date. But this is a maintained assumption, and we treat it as such. The consequence of a supply-side shift at the reform is bounded: any resulting bias in $\hat\rho_m$ is upward, reinforcing the interpretation of $\hat\rho_m$ as an upper bound rather than a point estimate.\footnote{Because the disclosure obligation and its associated liability predate the reform (loi ALUR, Article L271-4 CCH), we treat $c_{0,m}$ as governed by that long-standing mandate and unchanged across July 2021. In the conservative case that opposability nonetheless nudged the perceived cost of withholding upward, $\hat\Delta_m$ would absorb both the demand response and the cost change, causing $\hat\rho_m = \hat\Delta_m / (\hat\beta_{1,m}\cdot\hat{e}^*_m)$ to overstate true buyer sensitivity. This is the same direction of bias as from supply-side instability: both reinforce the upper-bound interpretation.}

\subsection{DiD Identification of $\rho_m$}

The identification uses a panel of monthly hedonic price indices constructed by Chapter~1 from listing-level data covering all three disclosure groups. For each market $m$, group $g\in\{H,\,L,\,\emptyset\}$, and month $t$, the index $\bar{p}_{m,t}^g$ is the predicted log price per m$^2$ for a reference property conditional on hedonic controls $X_i$. The $H$ index covers energy-efficient disclosed properties (EPC grades A to D, $d=1$, $K=1$); the $L$ index covers energy-intensive disclosed properties (grades E to G, $d=1$, $K=1$); and the $\emptyset$ index covers all non-disclosing listings ($d=0$), a pooled group that blends strategic hiders ($K=1$) and genuinely uninformed sellers ($K=0$). By conditioning on observables, the cross-group gap $\bar{p}_{m,t}^g - \bar{p}_{m,t}^{g'}$ isolates the EPC-attributable price wedge net of compositional differences between groups.

From these indices, two time-varying log price gaps are defined:
\begin{align}
\beta_{1,m,t} &= \bar p_{m,t}^{H} - \bar p_{m,t}^{L} \quad \text{(energy-efficiency premium)} \\
\Delta_{m,t}  &= \bar p_{m,t}^{\emptyset} - \bar p_{m,t}^{L} \quad \text{(non-disclosure premium)}
\end{align}
The reform-induced shifts in each gap are estimated by differencing period means:
\begin{align}
\hat\beta_{1,m} &= \overline{\beta_{1,m,t}}^{\,\text{post}} - \overline{\beta_{1,m,t}}^{\,\text{pre}}
  \label{eq:did_beta1} \\
\hat\Delta_m    &= \overline{\Delta_{m,t}}^{\,\text{post}} - \overline{\Delta_{m,t}}^{\,\text{pre}}
  \label{eq:did_delta}
\end{align}
where the pre-period spans October~2018 through June~2021 (33~months) and the post-period spans July~2021 through July~2024 (37~months). A volume filter requiring at least 20 average monthly transactions per disclosure group per market retains 40 apartment markets and 100 house markets; markets with $\hat\beta_{1,m}\leq 0$ are excluded from structural inversion.

To validate the parallel pre-trends assumption and characterise the dynamic profile of the reform, the period-mean estimates are complemented with a month-by-month event study. For each market $m$ and relative month $k$ (where $k=0$ is July 2021), the normalised gaps
\begin{align}
\tilde\beta_{1,m,k} &= \beta_{1,m,t(k)} - \beta_{1,m,t(-1)}, \qquad
\tilde\Delta_{m,k}  = \Delta_{m,t(k)}  - \Delta_{m,t(-1)}
\end{align}
are set to zero at $k=-1$ (the last pre-reform month) by construction. Figure~\ref{fig:ATT} plots the cross-market means with 95\% confidence intervals over $k\in[-33,\,+36]$. The pre-reform coefficients ($k<0$) are statistically indistinguishable from zero for both gaps, confirming parallel pre-trends. The post-reform coefficients increase gradually from $k=0$, consistent with progressive capitalisation of the legally opposable DPE. The period-mean estimates in Equations~\eqref{eq:did_beta1} and \eqref{eq:did_delta} average these relative-month profiles over the respective windows.

Given these estimates, the structural equation $\Delta_m = \rho_m\cdot\beta_{1,m}\cdot e^*_m$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:premium}) implies a closed-form estimator for buyer sensitivity. Since the reform shifts $\rho_m$ while $e^*_m$ is a supply-side object measured directly from the ADEME registry (Section~\ref{sec:beliefs} below), the DiD isolates the post-reform demand response:
\begin{equation}
    \hat\rho_m = \frac{\hat\Delta_m}{\hat\beta_{1,m} \cdot \hat{e}^*_m}
    \label{eq:rho_estimator}
\end{equation}
The constraint $\hat\rho_m \leq 1$ binds in the majority of markets. For apartments, the unconstrained ratio exceeds unity in all 40 markets (cross-market mean of approximately 7.7); for houses, 92 of 107 markets with $\hat\beta_{1,m}>0$ are clipped (cross-market mean ratio of approximately 0.58). Two compounding sources drive the excess: composition bias in $\hat\Delta_m$, because the non-disclosure price index pools strategic hiders with genuinely uninformed sellers and inflates the numerator; and thin-cell attenuation of $\hat\beta_{1,m}$, because sparse disclosure-group cells compress the denominator. These effects are quantitatively consistent with the magnitudes observed, but they preclude point identification of $\rho_m$ in most markets. In clipped markets, $\rho_m = 1$ is maintained as a working assumption, and $\hat{c}_{0,m}$ is interpreted as an upper bound: since the indifference condition $c_{0,m} = \rho_m\cdot\beta_{1,m}\cdot\hat{e}^*_m(\hat\sigma_{0,m})$ is strictly increasing in $\rho_m$, setting $\rho_m = 1$ overstates $c_{0,m}$ relative to any interior value. Point identification is achieved in 8 house markets where the inversion yields an interior solution; these are characterised separately in the Results.

\subsection{Measuring Equilibrium Beliefs}
\label{sec:beliefs}

Computing $\hat\rho_m$ and $\hat{c}_{0,m}$ requires $\hat{e}^*_m$, the expected quality buyers assign to a non-disclosing listing. In standard signalling models this belief must be jointly estimated with the structural parameters. Here it is directly observable: the ADEME registry provides the researcher with the same information structure that $e^*_m$ encodes for buyers.

For each market $m$, the four-group classification of Section~\ref{sec:data} yields three sample frequencies: $\hat\pi_m$ (the share of listings with $K=1$, the ADEME match rate); $\hat{\bar\theta}_m$ (the share of informed sellers with high quality, $\theta=1$, EPC $\leq$ D); and $\hat\sigma_{0,m}$ (the share of informed low-quality sellers, $K=1$, $\theta=0$, who hide by setting $d=0$). Substituting into the belief function (Equation~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}, at $\sigma_1^*=0$ per Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose}):
\begin{equation}
    \hat{e}^*_m = \frac{(1-\hat\pi_m)\,\hat{\bar\theta}_m}{\hat\pi_m\,(1-\hat{\bar\theta}_m)\,\hat\sigma_{0,m} + (1-\hat\pi_m)}
    \label{eq:estar}
\end{equation}
The numerator is the uninformed high-quality mass in the non-disclosing pool; the denominator is the total non-disclosing mass. No model estimation is involved: $\hat{e}^*_m$ is a function of directly observed sample proportions.

\subsection{Closed-Form Identification of $c_{0,m}$}

One might expect $\hat{c}_{0,m} = \hat\Delta_m$, since the equilibrium indifference condition gives $c_{0,m} = \Delta_m$ for a marginal hider. This logic fails because the ``undisclosed'' category in the price index pools \emph{two heterogeneous groups}: strategic hiders ($K=1$, $\theta=0$, $d=0$), who are the economically relevant group, and genuinely uninformed sellers ($K=0$, $d=0$), who constitute approximately 85\% of all non-disclosers. $\hat\Delta_m$ is therefore a weighted average of the strategic hider premium and the uninformed-seller premium, an upper bound on $c_{0,m}$, not a point estimate.\footnote{A listing-level DiD targeting $K=1$ non-disclosers directly is infeasible: the listing data begin in 2021, providing no pre-reform baseline at the listing level.}

The route to $c_{0,m}$ uses the equilibrium hiding rate $\hat\sigma_{0,m}$ among informed low-quality sellers ($K=1$, $\theta=0$). This group is identified precisely by the ADEME match and is immune to contamination from uninformed sellers. In the Partial Pooling regime, the equilibrium indifference condition
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\!\left(e^*(\hat\sigma_{0,m})\,;\,\hat\rho_m\right) = c_{0,m}
    \label{eq:indifference}
\end{equation}
has a unique solution in $c_{0,m}$ for any observed $\hat\sigma_{0,m}\in(0,1)$. Given the first-stage estimate $\hat\rho_m$, this yields the closed-form estimator:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{c}_{0,m} = \hat\rho_m \cdot \hat\beta_{1,m} \cdot \hat{e}^*_m(\hat\sigma_{0,m})
    \label{eq:c0_estimator}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{e}^*_m(\hat\sigma_{0,m})$ is the belief evaluated at the observed mixing rate. The estimator is exact: one free parameter ($c_{0,m}$) is matched to one moment ($\hat\sigma_{0,m}$) via the equilibrium condition. No numerical optimisation is required.

For house markets, the data provide two distinct upper bounds on $c_{0,m}$. The first is the equilibrium inversion under the maintained $\hat\rho_m = 1$ assumption:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{c}_{0,m}^{\,\text{inv}} = \hat\beta_{1,m} \cdot \hat{e}^*_m(\hat\sigma_{0,m})
    \label{eq:c0_interval}
\end{equation}
The second is $\hat\Delta_m$, the DiD non-disclosure premium, which is an upper bound on $c_{0,m}$ because uninformed sellers (approximately 75\% of non-disclosers for houses) inflate the undisclosed pool's average price above what strategic hiders alone would generate. The cross-market means are $\hat{c}_{0,m}^{\,\text{inv}} = 0.018$ log points and $\hat\Delta_m = 0.034$ log points. Internal consistency requires $\hat{c}_{0,m}^{\,\text{inv}} \leq \hat\Delta_m$, which holds in all but a handful of markets. The tighter bound $\hat{c}_{0,m}^{\,\text{inv}} = 0.018$ is the preferred estimate and is used in the policy counterfactuals of Section~\ref{sec:policy}.

The structural estimates use listing-level data from 2022 to 2024.\footnote{The year 2021 is excluded from the listing-level analysis because the market had not yet fully repriced the reform: the pooled hedonic estimate of $\hat\beta_{1}$ for apartments in H2~2021 is approximately $-0.012$, indicating the EPC quality premium had not yet turned positive, which renders the structural equation $\Delta_m = \rho_m\cdot\beta_{1,m}\cdot e^*_m$ unreliable for that period.} The estimation sample comprises 40 apartment markets and 100 house markets.

Bootstrap standard errors are computed with $B=500$ within-market resamples. In each draw, listings in market $m$ are resampled with replacement and all market-level moments $(\hat\pi_m, \hat{\bar\theta}_m, \hat\sigma_{0,m})$ are recomputed. The first-stage estimate $\hat\rho_m$ is held fixed at its DiD value across bootstrap iterations; only $\hat\sigma_{0,m}$ is resampled to propagate sampling uncertainty into $\hat{c}_{0,m}$ via Equation~\eqref{eq:c0_estimator}. The 500 replications yield percentile-based 95\% confidence intervals.

$\hat\Delta_m$ (6.9\% for apartments and 3.4\% for houses) measures how much more
non-disclosers earn over low-quality disclosed properties compared with the pre-reform
baseline; because the undisclosed pool commingles strategic hiders with genuinely uninformed
sellers, this figure is an upper bound on the true concealment cost rather than a direct
estimate of it. Panel~B translates these reduced-form inputs into seller-side parameters: $\hat\rho_m = 95.0\%$ for apartments and 71.8\% for
houses indicate that nearly all buyers in the average market price energy performance into
their offers, while the inverted hiding cost $\hat{c}_{0,m}$ settles at approximately
1.6--1.8\% of property value for both types, a strikingly narrow range given how differently
apartments and houses are traded. Panel~C
illuminates the supply side: just over half of apartment sellers (51.8\%) and 42\% of house
sellers hold a certified energy audit at listing ($\hat\pi_m$), and among those informed
sellers, roughly 27\% own a high-quality property ($\hat{\bar\theta}_m \approx 26.5$--28.5\%).
The most striking contrast across property types lies in the strategic-hiding rate
$\hat\sigma_{0,m}$: 7.7\% of informed apartment sellers who hold a bad EPC choose to withhold
it, versus only 3.7\% for houses, a difference that mirrors the three-fold gap in
$\hat\beta_{1,m}$, since a larger forgone quality premium makes concealment a costlier choice
for house sellers.

\subsection{Structural Estimates and Economic Interpretation}

The first-stage estimator $\hat\rho_m = \hat\Delta_m / (\hat\beta_{1,m} \cdot \hat{e}^*_m)$, clipped to $[0,1]$, yields means of 0.950 for apartments and 0.718 for houses. These averages, however, conceal a pattern of boundary saturation. For apartments, 38 of 40 markets reach the $\hat\rho_m = 1$ upper boundary; only the 2 full-unraveling markets pull the mean below unity. For houses, 67 of 75 partial-pooling markets also reach the boundary, with 25 full-unraveling markets contributing $\hat\rho_m = 0$ and 8 markets achieving interior identification. The boundary reflects a structural feature of the data: the non-disclosure premium $\hat\Delta_m$ typically exceeds $\hat\beta_{1,m} \cdot \hat{e}^*_m$, so the data are consistent with full buyer EPC-sensitivity but cannot pin down exactly how close to one $\rho_m$ truly is. We maintain $\hat\rho_m = 1$ as the working assumption for these markets and treat $\hat{c}_{0,m}$ as an upper bound on the true hiding cost accordingly.

Under this convention, hiding costs are small in magnitude: 0.016 log points (approximately 1.6\% of property value) for apartments and 0.018 log points (1.8\%) for houses. The near-identical level across property types suggests that hiding costs are governed primarily by the legal disclosure regime and audit infrastructure, rather than by property-specific characteristics. For house markets, the data additionally provide a second and independent upper bound from the DiD: $\hat\Delta_m = 0.034$ log points. The tighter bound from the equilibrium inversion (0.018) lies strictly below this DiD bound, providing a consistency check: the two estimates are compatible with the same underlying $c_{0,m}$.

The contrast between property types is itself informative. Houses have an energy-efficiency premium three times larger than apartments ($\hat\beta_{1,m} \approx 0.058$ vs.\ 0.017 log points), reflecting their larger surface areas and older building stock, which make heating costs directly material to buyers' running expenses. A higher forgone premium makes concealment more expensive for low-quality sellers, and the data reflect this exactly: the hiding rate $\hat\sigma_{0,m}$ is half as large for houses (3.7\% vs.\ 7.7\%), and the share of strategic hiders is correspondingly lower (1.1\% vs.\ 3.5\% of all listings). The non-disclosure premium $\hat\Delta_m$ is also lower for houses (0.034 vs.\ 0.069), since a thinner pool of strategic hiders means buyers' beliefs about withheld quality are less pessimistic. The entire pattern (higher quality premium, lower hiding rate, lower non-disclosure premium) is the qualitative prediction of the model under equilibrium comparative statics across property types.

One further check concerns the gap between the non-disclosure premium $\hat\Delta_m$ and the structural hiding cost $\hat{c}_{0,m}$. The raw DiD estimate exceeds the inverted hiding cost by a factor of roughly 4 for apartments and 2 for houses. This systematic gap is not a contradiction: it is what the model predicts. Uninformed sellers (approximately 83\% of non-disclosers for apartments and 89\% for houses) earn a non-disclosure premium simply by being in the market, pooling with strategic hiders and inflating the undisclosed category's average price above what strategic concealment alone would generate. $\hat\Delta_m$ is the market-level average of this pooled premium; $\hat{c}_{0,m}$ is the cost borne by the marginal informed hider, recovered by targeting only the informed-seller hiding rate $\hat\sigma_{0,m}$ in the equilibrium inversion. The two objects measure different things, and the fact that $\hat\Delta_m$ greatly exceeds $\hat{c}_{0,m}$ is precisely the compositional mechanism the model is designed to capture.

\section{Policy Extension: Renovation Subsidies}
\label{sec:policy}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Motivation}

The core model treats $\theta$ as exogenous. From a policy perspective, the key distortion is that informed low-quality sellers can earn rents by pooling with the uninformed instead of upgrading the underlying asset. A seller with $\theta=0$ faces three options: hide at cost $c_0$ and capture a share of the non-disclosure premium, disclose her low quality and accept the discount, or renovate at cost $R(X)$ and disclose verified high quality. The wedge that renovation must overcome is the verified-quality premium $\Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}1) - \Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}0)$: the price difference between disclosed high and disclosed low quality. Whenever the engineering cost of renovation exceeds this verified-quality premium, the seller declines to invest, and a renovation subsidy is the natural instrument to close the gap.

A subtler point, developed formally below, is that the renovation threshold's apparent dependence on the hiding cost is illusory in the partial-pooling regime. The renovation comparison pits renovate-and-disclose against hiding: an informed low-quality seller never strictly prefers to disclose her low quality when a positive opacity premium exists, since hiding yields the inflated non-disclosure pool revenue at the cost $c_0$, and that premium-net-of-cost is weakly positive whenever any seller hides in equilibrium. Strategy~1 (disclose-low) is therefore weakly dominated by Strategy~2 (hide), and the threshold $\bar R = \Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}1) - \Pi(d{=}0;e^*) + c_0$ comes from comparing renovation to hiding alone. The $+c_0$ in this formula is misleading: $\Pi(d{=}0;e^*)$ is itself an equilibrium revenue that adjusts to the hiding cost through the equilibrium hiding rate $\sigma_0^*$. In partial pooling, the indifference condition $\Pi(d{=}0;e^*) - c_0 = \Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}0)$ causes the $c_0$ term to cancel exactly, and the threshold collapses to the verified-quality premium $\Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}1)-\Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}0)$. The regulator nevertheless retains a meaningful disclosure-policy lever: \emph{verification production}, that is, the rate at which sellers obtain EPC audits and enter the informed population $\pi$. This margin does not affect the marginal seller's renovation threshold either, but it scales the population of sellers subject to that threshold and, over time, converts the housing stock from uninformed to verified status. The three resulting policy instruments (renovation subsidies, hiding-cost enforcement, and verification enforcement) act on three different margins of the seller's problem and are best understood as complements rather than substitutes.

\subsection{Extended Seller Problem}

We introduce renovation $r\in\{0,1\}$ such that $\theta'=1$ if $r=1$ (for a $\theta=0$ seller). The informed low-quality seller's extended problem is:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\{r,d,p^{\text{list}}\}}\;\Pi(r,d,p^{\text{list}}\mid\theta,X) = \phi(p^{\text{list}})\cdot\Gamma(p^{\text{list}}\mid d,\theta')-C(\theta',d)-(R(X,r)-S)
    \label{eq:seller_problem}
\end{equation}
The subsidy $S\geq 0$ reduces the effective renovation cost. Renovating and then hiding is strictly dominated by renovating and disclosing: a seller who upgrades to $\theta=1$ has no reason to conceal this.

\noindent The relevant non-renovation alternative is Strategy~2 (hide), not Strategy~1 (disclose-low). Whenever the non-disclosure pool commands a positive opacity premium $\Delta(e^*) = \rho\beta_1 e^* > 0$, informed low-quality sellers prefer hiding to disclosing low: the gross revenue gain from being in the non-disclosure pool at least covers the hiding cost. Formally, in any equilibrium with $\sigma_0^* > 0$ (Partial Pooling or Complete Adverse Selection), the equilibrium hiding rate satisfies $\Pi(d=0;e^*) - c_0 \ge \Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$, with equality at the partial-pooling indifference. Strategy~1 is therefore weakly dominated by Strategy~2, and the renovation decision reduces to a binary comparison between Strategy~3 (renovate-and-disclose) and Strategy~2 (hide). Strategy~3 dominates Strategy~2 if and only if
\begin{equation}
    \Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \bigl[R(X)-S\bigr]
    \;>\;
    \Pi(d=0;e^*) - c_0,
    \label{eq:threshold_condition}
\end{equation}
which holds if and only if
\[
    R(X) - S \;\leq\;
    \underbrace{\Pi(d=1,\theta=1)-\Pi(d=0;e^*)}_{\text{market premium for verified high quality}}
    +\underbrace{c_0}_{\text{hiding cost avoided}}
    \;\equiv\;
    \bar{R}(\pi,\rho,X).
\]

$\bar R$ is the total private return the market offers an informed low-quality seller for renovating and disclosing: the revenue gain from upgrading out of the non-disclosure pool to verified high-quality status, plus the hiding cost no longer paid. Absent a subsidy, renovation is privately optimal whenever $R(X) \le \bar R$. The minimum incentive-compatible subsidy is the gap $R(X) - \bar R$: the transfer required to restore private incentives when the engineering cost exceeds the market reward.

The dependence of $\bar R$ on $c_0$ is the central object for policy. The original formula~\eqref{eq:threshold_condition} appears to make $\bar R$ increase one-for-one with the hiding cost, since the $+c_0$ term enters linearly. This appearance is misleading: $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ is itself an equilibrium revenue evaluated at the equilibrium pool quality $e^* = e(\sigma_0^*)$, and the equilibrium hiding rate $\sigma_0^*$ adjusts to $c_0$. Once the equilibrium response is taken into account, $\bar R$ in the empirically dominant partial-pooling regime is \emph{invariant} to $c_0$: the apparent dependence is absorbed by the indifference condition. The following lemma makes this precise.

\subsection{The Renovation Threshold and the Minimum Incentive-Compatible Subsidy}

\begin{definition}[Renovation Threshold]
The renovation threshold $\bar{R}(\pi,\rho,X)$ is the maximum renovation cost at which an informed low-quality seller prefers to renovate-and-disclose over hiding, absent subsidies:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{R}(\pi,\rho,X)\;\equiv\;\underbrace{\Pi(d=1,\theta=1)-\Pi(d=0;e^*)}_{\text{Market premium for verified high quality}}+\underbrace{c_0}_{\text{Hiding cost avoided}}.
    \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Equilibrium reduction in partial pooling]
\label{lem:threshold_regimes}
In the partial-pooling equilibrium, the indifference condition $\Pi(d=0;e^*) - c_0 = \Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$ implies that the renovation threshold reduces to the verified-quality premium:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{R}^{\text{PP}}(\rho,X) \;=\; \Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \Pi(d=1,\theta=0),
    \label{eq:threshold_pp}
\end{equation}
which depends on $\rho$ and $X$ alone and is invariant to $c_0$ and $\pi$. In Complete Adverse Selection ($\sigma_0^*=1$), the threshold retains the form~\eqref{eq:threshold} with $e^* = (1-\pi)\bar\theta/(1-\pi\bar\theta)$ locally constant in $c_0$, so $\partial \bar R^{\text{CAS}}/\partial c_0 = 1$.
\end{lemma}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

\medskip

The partial-pooling reduction~\eqref{eq:threshold_pp} is the central technical result of this section. It rests on the equilibrium indifference identity: the marginal informed-low seller is, by construction of the regime, exactly indifferent between hiding and disclosing-low, so the hide payoff $\Pi(d=0;e^*) - c_0$ equals the disclose-low payoff $\Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$. Substituting this identity into~\eqref{eq:threshold} causes the hiding cost to cancel exactly, leaving the verified-quality premium $\Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$: the revenue gain a seller obtains by upgrading from a verified-low to a verified-high listing.

In the empirically dominant interior regime, the renovation incentive is governed entirely by the price gap between verified high and verified low quality. The non-disclosure premium $\Delta = \rho\beta_1 e^*$, which in partial pooling equals $c_0$ by the same indifference, plays no role in the renovation calculus: the seller's gross premium $\Delta$ for being in the non-disclosure pool and her cost $c_0$ for hiding cancel because the regime is constructed to make them cancel. Raising the hiding cost shifts the equilibrium hiding rate $\sigma_0^*$ along the indifference curve (with $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ adjusting endogenously to preserve indifference) but does not change the renovation threshold. Policymakers cannot ``buy'' renovation by tightening hiding-cost enforcement in this regime; renovation requires a direct subsidy that improves the renovate-and-disclose payoff relative to the seller's continuation value at low quality.

\begin{definition}[Minimum incentive-compatible subsidy]
\label{def:minimum_subsidy}
The minimum incentive-compatible subsidy is the smallest transfer making renovation weakly preferred to hiding, for a seller with renovation cost $\hat{R}(X)$, holding equilibrium beliefs fixed:
\begin{equation}
    S^*(\pi,\rho,X)\;\equiv\;\max\!\left\{\hat{R}(X)-\bar{R}(\pi,\rho,X),\;0\right\}
    \label{eq:subsidy}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{R}(X)$ denotes the actual renovation cost from engineering estimates or program data.
\end{definition}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Remark ($S^*$ is incentive-compatibility, not welfare optimum).}\label{rem:welfare}
The object $S^*$ is the minimum transfer that flips the marginal informed-low seller's decision rule from concealment to renovation, given everyone else's equilibrium behaviour. It is \emph{not} a Ramsey-optimal subsidy. A full welfare problem would maximise
\[
    \max_S \; \int_X \mathbf{1}\{\hat R(X) \le \bar R + S\} \cdot \big[\Delta W(X) - \lambda S\big]\, dF(X),
\]
where $\Delta W(X)$ is the social gain per renovation (private surplus change plus any energy-use externality), $\lambda\ge 1$ is the marginal cost of public funds, and $F$ is the within-market distribution of renovation costs. The corner solution $S^* = \hat R - \bar R$ emerges only in the limit of degenerate $F$, $\lambda=1$, and zero externality. Throughout this section we treat $S^*$ as the operative \emph{positive} object (the smallest subsidy that induces a given seller to renovate) and leave the full Ramsey characterisation to future work.

\noindent The renovation threshold $\bar{R}$ is the market's implicit valuation of quality information from the seller's perspective. In the partial-pooling equilibrium it equals the verified-quality premium $\Pi(d=1,\theta=1)-\Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$, the revenue gain from a buyer-verifiable upgrade from low to high quality. In Complete Adverse Selection it retains the original definition~\eqref{eq:threshold} with the equilibrium pool quality at the corner. When actual renovation costs fall below the threshold, the market already rewards quality revelation enough to make private renovation worthwhile. When $\hat{R}(X)>\bar{R}$, the market premium for renovation is insufficient and targeted public support is required. The minimum incentive-compatible subsidy $S^*$ is precisely the difference: it fills the gap between what the market offers and what renovation costs, doing no more than necessary to tip the seller's calculus toward quality improvement.

\subsection{Comparative Statics: Renovation and Disclosure Operate on Different Margins}

\begin{proposition}[Subsidy comparative statics by regime]
\label{prop:subsidy}
The minimum incentive-compatible subsidy $S^*(\pi,\rho,X)$ satisfies $\partial S^*/\partial \hat R(X) = \mathbf{1}\{\hat R(X) > \bar R\}$ throughout, together with the following regime-conditional comparative statics on the renovation threshold $\bar R$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Partial Pooling} (the empirically relevant interior regime): $\partial \bar R/\partial c_0 = 0$, $\partial \bar R/\partial \pi = 0$, and $\partial \bar R/\partial \rho > 0$. Hence
        \[
            \frac{\partial S^*}{\partial c_0} = 0, \qquad
            \frac{\partial S^*}{\partial \pi} = 0, \qquad
            \frac{\partial S^*}{\partial \rho} \leq 0
            \qquad \text{when } \hat R(X) > \bar R.
        \]
    \item \textbf{Complete Adverse Selection} ($\sigma_0^*=1$, corner): $\partial \bar R/\partial c_0 = 1$, $\partial \bar R/\partial \pi > 0$, $\partial \bar R/\partial \rho > 0$. Hence $\partial S^*/\partial c_0 = -1$ and $\partial S^*/\partial \pi \le 0$ when $\hat R > \bar R$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. $\Box$

\medskip

The threshold result of Proposition~\ref{prop:subsidy} should not be read as evidence that disclosure policy is irrelevant for renovation outcomes. It is irrelevant only for one specific decision: the marginal informed low-quality seller's choice between concealment and renovation, holding her informed status fixed. The broader policy problem involves three distinguishable instruments acting on three distinguishable margins.

The first is the intensive margin of renovation propensity. The subsidy $S$ acts directly on the seller's renovation calculus through the inequality $\hat R(X) - S \le \bar R$. Conditional on being informed and low quality, the probability of renovation is governed entirely by $S$ and by the cross-property distribution of $\hat R(X)$.

The second is the composition of disclosure decisions among the informed. The hiding cost $c_0$, raised through fines, audits of suspected concealers, or strengthened legal exposure under loi ALUR, acts on the share of informed-low sellers who hide versus disclose-low. By Proposition~\ref{prop:comparative_statics}, tightening hiding penalties shrinks the strategic-hiding share. By Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold_regimes}, however, this leaves the renovation threshold unchanged in partial pooling: the equilibrium pool revenue $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ adjusts to keep the hider exactly indifferent with the disclose-low alternative, so the $c_0$ in~\eqref{eq:threshold} is exactly offset by the equilibrium movement of $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$. Only in the Complete Adverse Selection corner, where $\sigma_0^*=1$ is at the boundary and $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ is locally constant in $c_0$, does $c_0$ enter the threshold and the textbook substitutability $\partial S^*/\partial c_0 = -1$ obtain.

The third is the extensive margin of verification production. Mandatory EPC enforcement, audit infrastructure investment, and inspection of cadastral records raise $\pi$, the share of sellers who are informed about their property's energy performance, which is the principal long-run instrument in France: every transaction since 2007 in principle triggers an EPC, and the ADEME registry grows monotonically over time. The effect on aggregate renovation is direct and first-order:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Renovation flow} \;=\; \underbrace{\pi}_{\text{informed share}} \cdot \underbrace{(1-\bar\theta)}_{\text{low among informed}} \cdot \underbrace{F_R\big(\bar R(\rho, X) + S\big)}_{\text{share with }\hat R\le\bar R + S},
    \label{eq:renovation_flow}
\end{equation}
where $F_R$ is the within-market distribution of engineering renovation costs. Raising $\pi$ scales the renovation base linearly even when the per-seller threshold is invariant, because additional sellers become informed and enter the strategic disclosure problem in the first place. Each newly verified low-quality property is a potential subsidy recipient that previously was not.

There is also a stock-evolution effect that operates only along the verification-production margin. As $\pi$ rises and audits accumulate, the housing stock becomes increasingly characterised by verified energy labels. Buyers can condition prices on observed $\theta$ for a growing share of the market; the uninformed pool that sustains the non-disclosure premium shrinks; and the equilibrium gradually drifts from partial pooling toward cost-deterred hiding as the gain from concealing low quality erodes. Renovations undertaken today, once disclosed at the next sale, become permanent reclassifications from $\theta=0$ to $\theta=1$ in the verified stock. The static threshold analysis above takes the share of informed sellers as given; the verification enforcement margin is precisely what makes this share endogenous to policy over the medium run.

\medskip

The minimum incentive-compatible subsidy $S^*(\pi,\rho,X)$ measures how much transfer it takes to flip the renovation decision of a single informed low-quality seller, given the equilibrium pool composition, and the intensive margin alone determines that object. Hiding-cost enforcement does not substitute for the subsidy in the empirically dominant partial-pooling regime: at the indifference margin, the apparent $+c_0$ in the threshold is exactly cancelled by the equilibrium movement of $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$, and the renovation incentive collapses to the verified-quality premium. Verification production is genuinely complementary: $\pi$ does not change the threshold either, but it directly expands the population subject to it and dynamically converts the housing stock toward verified status. Disclosure enforcement and renovation subsidies are therefore complements that scale each other's reach, not substitutes.

\medskip Finally, the model suggests that a uniform national subsidy is generically inefficient. The threshold $\bar{R}(\pi,\rho,X)$ varies across markets through $\rho$ (the share of EPC-sensitive buyers) and through property characteristics $X$, even in partial pooling where $\pi$ and $c_0$ drop out of the marginal threshold. A uniform national subsidy therefore necessarily over-subsidises markets with high $\rho$ (where the verified-quality premium is already large) and under-subsidises markets with low $\rho$ (where it is small). The verification-production margin reinforces this: at any given $S$, the renovation flow~\eqref{eq:renovation_flow} also scales with the local $\pi$, so subsidies and audit-infrastructure investment should be co-targeted on markets where both $\pi$ is low and $\rho$ delivers a sufficient verified-quality premium to make subsidised renovation cost-effective. Efficient policy requires targeting on observable proxies for market composition: local climate and energy-cost exposure for $\rho$, audit infrastructure (notaire density, ADEME registry coverage) for $\pi$, and property-level engineering estimates for $\hat{R}(X)$.

\subsection{Renovation Subsidies in French Housing Markets: Empirical Magnitudes and Spatial Heterogeneity}

In the partial-pooling regime, Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold_regimes} reduces the renovation threshold $\bar{R}^{\text{PP}}$ to the verified-quality premium $\Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}1) - \Pi(d{=}1,\theta{=}0)$, with $c_0$ and $\pi$ cancelling through equilibrium indifference. Because $\beta_1$ measures the quality premium in log-point terms against a local price-per-m\textsuperscript{2} base, the nominal threshold $\bar{R}_m$ scales with the local price level: high-price urban markets have the largest verified-quality premia in absolute terms, even when the proportional premium $\beta_1$ is similar across locations. Across the 40 estimable apartment markets, $\bar{R}_m$ averages \euro{}165/m\textsuperscript{2} (median \euro{}151/m\textsuperscript{2}); across the 100 house markets, the mean is \euro{}128/m\textsuperscript{2} and the median \euro{}134/m\textsuperscript{2} (Figure~\ref{fig:subsidy_threshold_dist}). The PP markets cluster more narrowly around their medians, reflecting the equilibrium compression that the indifference condition imposes on pool revenue. Cross-market dispersion is nonetheless substantial: apartment thresholds range from roughly \euro{}80 to \euro{}350/m\textsuperscript{2}, so markets at the upper tail of the distribution are intrinsically more amenable to privately funded renovation than those at the lower tail.

For houses, 91 of 100 markets (91\%) require support, with a conditional median of \euro{}116/m\textsuperscript{2} (approximately \euro{}13{,}287 per unit at 113~m\textsuperscript{2}). By $\hat{R} = 500$, every apartment market requires a subsidy and 98 of 100 house markets do likewise; the conditional medians rise to \euro{}349/m\textsuperscript{2} (apartments, \euro{}22{,}192 per unit) and \euro{}366/m\textsuperscript{2} (houses, \euro{}42{,}060 per unit). The cost-sharing ratio $S^*_m / \hat{R}$ is the natural decision variable for the regulator: at the light benchmark it stands at roughly 40\% for the median apartment market and 46\% for the median house market; at the medium benchmark both rise to approximately 70\%. This non-linear escalation in required cost-sharing as intervention depth increases has a direct programme-design implication: a commitment to a fixed cost-sharing rate is more defensible than a commitment to a fixed euro amount, because it adjusts automatically to the depth of the intervention and to local market conditions. Figure~\ref{fig:subsidy_regime_breakdown} decomposes it by equilibrium regime.

The regime decomposition is informative for understanding the structural mechanism, though it is secondary to spatial targeting for programme design. In PP, the threshold is invariant to $c_0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold_regimes}, so cross-market dispersion in $S^*_m$ is driven entirely by variation in $\beta_1$ and local prices. CDH markets retain the full formula~\eqref{eq:threshold} and carry larger thresholds; they are consequently over-represented in the ``no subsidy needed'' category at $\hat{R} = 250$ and require smaller subsidies at $\hat{R} = 500$ than their sample share would suggest. This CDH group is a minority (2 apartment markets, 25 house markets) and is, by construction, the segment where the private renovation incentive is already strongest. The policy challenge lies in the PP majority, where the renovation threshold is pinned at the verified-quality premium and public intervention must bridge the gap between that premium and the renovation cost.

A uniform national subsidy is inefficient because the cost-sharing requirement varies substantially across markets. Figure~\ref{fig:subsidy_maps} maps $S^*_m / \hat{R}$, the share of the renovation invoice the regulator must cover, at the two cost benchmarks; bubble size encodes the local verified-quality premium $\bar{R}_m$. The support of the cost-sharing distribution is wide. At $\hat{R} = 250$~\euro{}/m\textsuperscript{2}, 22.5\% of apartment markets and 9\% of house markets need no subsidy at all, while markets at the upper end of the distribution require cost-shares approaching 80\%. A flat per-m\textsuperscript{2} grant set at the cross-market mean would simultaneously over-pay the lower tail of this distribution, where the private incentive already suffices, and under-pay the upper tail, where renovations remain unfunded. Both forms of misallocation are costly: the former through fiscal waste on infra-marginal markets, the latter through forgone renovations in the markets where the gap is largest. This cross-market dispersion is systematic rather than idiosyncratic, since the cost-sharing ratio is mechanically low where $\bar{R}_m$ is high, and $\bar{R}_m$ is high where local prices per m\textsuperscript{2} are large and buyer EPC-sensitivity $\rho_m$ is strong. These conditions hold in the high-price urban markets of metropolitan France, which carry the largest bubbles on the maps; the cost-sharing ratio is correspondingly higher in lower-price peripheral and rural markets, where small bubbles signal that the verified-quality premium delivers only a modest fraction of the renovation cost. An efficient programme would differentiate the per-m\textsuperscript{2} subsidy along these structural axes rather than apply a uniform amount, channelling more public expenditure to markets where the private renovation gap is largest. The bubble-size dimension also points to the co-targeting logic of \S\ref{sec:policy}: markets with small bubbles are typically markets with low verification penetration $\pi$, combining a small per-renovation base with a small eligible population. Effective deployment therefore requires simultaneously raising $\pi$ through audit-infrastructure investment and concentrating the cost-sharing transfer on the markets where both margins are most constrained.

These estimates should be read as order-of-magnitude upper bounds. The per-listing aggregation abstracts from the extensive-margin participation decision of sellers, and the realised fiscal cost under any actual programme would be smaller. More fundamentally, Remark~\ref{rem:welfare} establishes that $S^*_m$ is an incentive-compatibility object rather than a Ramsey-optimal transfer: with a shadow cost of public funds $\lambda > 1$, the regulator will set the operative subsidy below $S^*_m$, trading off the verified-quality-premium gap against the deadweight cost of taxation. The maps and distributions above identify where market failures are largest; they are properly read as identifying the targets of maximum social return per euro of public expenditure, not as direct programme prescriptions.

\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

Four years after France mandated energy performance disclosure, silence is still rewarded: properties listed without an EPC score trade at a premium of 7 log points above disclosed low-efficiency properties for apartments and 3 log points for houses. Standard unraveling theory predicts the opposite. This paper resolves the puzzle.

The resolution requires two departures from the classical framework, each empirically motivated and individually insufficient. First, not all sellers are informed: at listing time, only 51.8\% of apartment sellers and 41.7\% of house sellers hold a certified energy audit. When buyers observe a missing EPC score, they face genuine ambiguity: the non-disclosing seller may be concealing an unfavorable rating or may simply not know her property's energy performance. Uninformed sellers therefore provide strategic hiders with involuntary cover, preventing silence from being read as the worst possible signal. Second, not all buyers price energy performance into their offers. When the share of energy-sensitive buyers is moderate, the revenue penalty from concealment is diluted, making hiding profitable even against a skeptical pool. Neither departure alone is sufficient: uninformed sellers keep silence ambiguous, but buyer heterogeneity ensures that exploiting this ambiguity is financially worthwhile.

These two forces generate three equilibrium regimes. In the partial-pooling equilibrium that describes the bulk of French housing markets, the fraction of informed low-quality sellers that hides adjusts endogenously until the marginal hider is exactly indifferent between concealment and disclosure. This indifference condition delivers the paper's central structural result: the non-disclosure premium equals exactly the concealment cost, invariant to the share of informed sellers and the share of energy-sensitive buyers. The observable price gap between non-disclosing and disclosed low-quality properties is therefore a direct measurement of the structural concealment cost, not a free function of market composition. Inverting this condition, we recover concealment costs of 1.6\% of property value for apartments and 1.8\% for houses, a strikingly narrow range across two segments that differ markedly in buyer energy-sensitivity (95\% for apartments, 71.8\% for houses). Strategic hiding is itself rare: only 7.7\% of informed apartment sellers with unfavorable ratings withhold them, and only 3.7\% of house sellers do so. The non-disclosure premium survives not because strategic hiding is pervasive but because genuine uninformedness is. The uninformed seller, who pays no concealment cost yet receives the same premium as the strategic hider, is the residual claimant on the information rent; the informed hider merely breaks even.

The policy implications depart from textbook prescriptions in one consequential way. Although the renovation threshold mechanically incorporates the concealment cost, the seller's indifference condition causes that cost to cancel exactly in partial pooling: the renovation incentive reduces to the verified-quality premium, the price gap between high-quality and low-quality disclosed properties, and is invariant to both the concealment cost and the share of informed sellers. Tightening enforcement against non-disclosure therefore does not move the renovation threshold in the empirically dominant regime. The regulator faces three distinct instruments: renovation subsidies (closing the gap between the verified-quality premium and the renovation cost), enforcement against strategic hiding (relevant only outside partial pooling), and audit-infrastructure investment (expanding the share of sellers who are informed). These are complements, not substitutes. A uniform national subsidy exploits none of this structure: the minimum cost-sharing requirement ranges from zero in markets where the private incentive already suffices to nearly 80\% where the verified-quality premium is small, and aggregate programme costs reach 15.5 billion euros at the light renovation benchmark, three to eight times the current annual budget of MaPrimeR\'enov'. Efficient targeting must condition on local market composition, directing public expenditure to markets where both the private renovation gap and the unverified seller population are largest.

The broader lesson extends beyond energy labels. Disclosure mandates discipline strategic silence only when sellers are actually informed. When a substantial share genuinely does not know their product's quality, the mandate produces ambiguity rather than transparency: involuntary non-disclosers shelter strategic hiders, and silence cannot be read as bad news. The policy challenge is not to punish opacity more severely but to produce knowledge in the first place. In housing energy markets, as in any market where non-disclosure mixes strategy with ignorance, audit-infrastructure investment is the instrument that expands the reach of every other tool in the regulator's toolkit.

\section{Data and Model Objects}

Our dataset includes residential property listings from various French housing markets. For each listing $i$ in market $m$, we observe:

\begin{itemize}
\item $p$: listing price
\item $X$: vector of property characteristics (such as size, age, location, etc.)
\item $d \in {0,1}$: indicator of whether the Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) score is displayed in the listing
\item $\text{EPC}^{\text{listing}}$: the EPC score displayed on the listing (if $d_i = 1$)
\end{itemize}

What sets this approach apart is our ability to match these listings with data from ADEME (the French Environment and Energy Management Agency), which maintains an official registry of certified energy audits. These audits are reported by third-party assessors who are presumed to be honest and impartial. We match listings to the ADEME registry based on key characteristics that are registered in both databases, such as addresses, floor, item type, and area\footnote{We provide a full explanation of our matching strategy in the appendix}. This allows us to assess the accuracy of the EPC scores displayed in listings by comparing them to the official certified energy audits. This allows us to observe notably:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $K \in \{0,1\}$: whether a verified EPC exists at listing time
    \item $\theta$: the true (verified) EPC score (if $K = 1$)
\end{itemize}

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

All results below adopt the benchmark $\alpha_k=\beta_k$.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Preliminary: Optimal Price Sensitivity to Buyer Valuation}

\begin{proof}[Optimal price sensitivity to shifts in buyer valuation]

The optimal price $p^*$ satisfies the first-order condition:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \cdot p^*} = 1 - G(v_1 - p^*)
\end{equation}

Define the left-hand side as $\mathrm{LHS}(p) = \frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \cdot p}$ and the right-hand side as $\mathrm{RHS}(p; v_1) = 1 - G(v_1 - p)$. The optimal price is determined by the intersection $\mathrm{LHS}(p^*) = \mathrm{RHS}(p^*; v_1)$.

\textbf{Monotonicity of each side in $p$.}

The left-hand side is strictly decreasing in $p$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathrm{LHS}}{\partial p} = -\frac{\gamma_1^2}{(\gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \cdot p)^2} < 0
\end{equation}

The right-hand side is strictly increasing in $p$. Since $G$ is the logistic CDF, $G'(x) = G(x)(1 - G(x)) > 0$ for all $x$. Therefore:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathrm{RHS}}{\partial p} = G'(v_1 - p) > 0
\end{equation}

A strictly decreasing function intersects a strictly increasing function at most once, so $p^*$ is unique.

\textbf{Effect of $v_1$ on the right-hand side.}

At any fixed $p$, the right-hand side is strictly decreasing in $v_1$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathrm{RHS}}{\partial v_1} = -G'(v_1 - p) < 0
\end{equation}

An increase in $v_1$ shifts the RHS curve downward.

\textbf{Comparative static.}

Using the chain rule, differentiating the equilibrium condition with respect to $v_1$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathrm{LHS}}{\partial p} \cdot \frac{\partial p^*}{\partial v_1} = \frac{\partial \mathrm{RHS}}{\partial p} \cdot \frac{\partial p^*}{\partial v_1} + \frac{\partial \mathrm{RHS}}{\partial v_1}
\end{equation}

Rearranging:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial p^*}{\partial v_1} = \frac{\frac{\partial \mathrm{RHS}}{\partial v_1}}{\frac{\partial \mathrm{LHS}}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial \mathrm{RHS}}{\partial p}} = \frac{-G'(v_1 - p^*)}{-\frac{\gamma_1^2}{(\gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \cdot p^*)^2} - G'(v_1 - p^*)} > 0
\end{equation}

The numerator is negative. The denominator is negative (sum of two negative terms). Therefore $\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial v_1} > 0$.
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Lemma A.1 (Profit is Increasing in Sensitive-Buyer Valuation)}
\label{lem:profit}

\textit{Holding $v_0$ and $\rho$ fixed, the gross profit function $\tilde{\Pi}(v_1; v_0, \rho)$ is strictly increasing in $v_1$ whenever $\rho > 0$.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma A.1]
Let $p_{\mathrm{list}}^*(v_1)$ denote the optimal price given sensitive-buyer valuation $v_1$. By the envelope theorem, the effect of a small increase in $v_1$ on maximized profit is:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \tilde{\Pi}}{\partial v_1} = \phi(p_{\mathrm{list}}^*) \cdot \rho \cdot G'(v_1 - p_{\mathrm{list}}^*)
\end{equation}
where we evaluate the partial derivative of the objective holding $p$ fixed at the optimum.

All three terms are positive: $\phi(p^*) > 0$ (expected transaction price is positive), $\rho \in [0,1] \geq 0$ (by assumption), and $G'(\cdot) > 0$ (logistic density is everywhere positive). Therefore $\frac{\partial \tilde{\Pi}}{\partial v_1} > 0$.
\end{proof}

Since $\tilde{\Pi}$ is increasing in $v_1$, the profit gain from disclosure is positive if and only if $v_1(d=1) > v_1(d=0)$: sensitive buyers value the property more when quality is revealed than when it is hidden. In this case, disclosure raises profit, and the seller discloses (absent hiding costs). Conversely, the profit gain is negative if $v_1(d=1) < v_1(d=0)$: sensitive buyers value the property more under hiding. This occurs when buyers' inference about non-disclosers is more favorable than the truth. In this case, hiding raises profit, and the seller hides (absent sufficiently high hiding costs).

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:premium} (Disclosure Incentives and Seller Sorting)}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma A.1, $p^*_0 > p^*_1$ if and only if $v_1(d=0) > v_1(d=1, \theta)$. Since $v_1(d=0) - v_1(d=1, \theta) = \beta_1 \cdot (\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] - \theta)$ and $\beta_1 > 0$, this holds if and only if $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] > \theta$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{For a low-quality seller} ($\theta = 0$): the condition is $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] > 0$, which holds whenever the non-disclosing pool contains any high-quality sellers.
    \item \textbf{For a high-quality seller} ($\theta = 1$): the condition is $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] > 1$, which is impossible since $\theta \in \{0,1\}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Corollary \ref{cor:hq_disclose} (High-Quality Sellers Always Disclose)}

\begin{proof}
From Proposition \ref{prop:premium}, the non-disclosure premium exists if and only if $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] > \theta$.

For a high-quality seller ($\theta = 1$), this condition requires $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] > 1$. But $\theta \in \{0, 1\}$, so $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] \leq 1$ always. Therefore the condition never holds: high-quality sellers never earn a premium from hiding.

In fact, the inequality is strict whenever the non-disclosing pool contains any low-quality sellers. In that case $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0] < 1$, so:
\begin{equation}
    v_1(d=1, \theta=1) - v_1(d=0) = \beta_1 \cdot (1 - \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid d=0]) > 0
\end{equation}

Disclosure yields a strictly higher valuation, and therefore strictly higher profit. Adding hiding costs $C(\theta=1) = c_0 + c_1 \geq 0$ only reinforces this: disclosure dominates hiding.
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:equilibrium} (Equilibrium Existence and Uniqueness)}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose}, $\sigma_1^* = 0$ in any equilibrium. The strategic problem reduces to characterising $\sigma_0^* \in [0,1]$, the hiding probability of informed low-quality sellers. The incentive to hide is
\[
    \Phi(\sigma_0) \;\equiv\; \Delta\!\left(e(\sigma_0)\right) - c_0
    \;=\; \frac{\rho\,\beta_1\,(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0 + (1-\pi)} - c_0,
\]
where $e(\sigma_0)$ is the equilibrium pool belief (Equation~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}). Since $e(\sigma_0)$ is strictly decreasing in $\sigma_0$ (Section~\ref{sec:beliefs}) and $\Delta$ is strictly increasing in $e$, $\Phi$ is \emph{strictly decreasing} in $\sigma_0$, with boundary values
\[
    \Phi(0) = \Delta(\bar{\theta}) - c_0, \qquad
    \Phi(1) = \Delta\!\left(\tfrac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}\right) - c_0.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{Case 1: Cost-Deterred Hiding.} Suppose $c_0 > \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, i.e., $\Phi(0) < 0$.

Since $\Phi$ is strictly decreasing, $\Phi(\sigma_0) \leq \Phi(0) < 0$ for all $\sigma_0 \in [0,1]$. Hiding is strictly unprofitable at every hiding rate, including the most favourable case $\sigma_0 = 0$. Disclosure strictly dominates for every informed low-quality seller, so the unique equilibrium is $\sigma_0^* = 0$ with equilibrium belief $e^* = \bar{\theta}$.

\textit{Interpretation.} All informed low-quality sellers disclose; the non-disclosing pool consists exclusively of uninformed sellers. Buyers correctly infer quality $\bar{\theta}$ from non-disclosure. Unlike the classical Grossman--Milgrom outcome, silence is uninformative rather than damning: non-disclosure carries no stigma because it reveals nothing beyond the seller's lack of a certified audit.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 2: Complete Adverse Selection.} Suppose $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, i.e., $\Phi(1) > 0$.

Since $\Phi$ is strictly decreasing, $\Phi(\sigma_0) \geq \Phi(1) > 0$ for all $\sigma_0 \in [0,1]$. Hiding is strictly profitable at every hiding rate, including the least favourable case $\sigma_0 = 1$. Hiding is therefore strictly dominant for every informed low-quality seller regardless of the strategies of others, so the unique equilibrium is $\sigma_0^* = 1$ with equilibrium belief $e^* = (1-\pi)\bar{\theta}/(1-\pi\bar{\theta})$.

\textit{Interpretation.} All informed low-quality sellers pool with uninformed sellers. The non-disclosure premium $\rho\cdot\beta_1\cdot e(1)$ is the maximum attainable across all regimes (Equation~\eqref{eq:premium}).

\medskip
\textbf{Case 3: Partial Pooling.} Suppose $\Delta(e(1)) \leq c_0 \leq \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, i.e., $\Phi(1) \leq 0 \leq \Phi(0)$.

$\Phi$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on $[0,1]$, with $\Phi(0) \geq 0$ and $\Phi(1) \leq 0$. By the intermediate value theorem, there exists $\sigma_0^* \in [0,1]$ with $\Phi(\sigma_0^*) = 0$; strict monotonicity of $\Phi$ implies uniqueness. When the boundary inequalities hold strictly, $\sigma_0^*$ lies in the open interval $(0,1)$; at the boundaries, $\sigma_0^* \in \{0,1\}$ and the regime collapses to Case 1 or Case 2.

At $\sigma_0^*$, informed low-quality sellers are exactly indifferent between hiding and disclosing; mixing at rate $\sigma_0^*$ is self-confirming through the belief function. This regime generates the closed-form hiding rate and is the empirically dominant regime in the estimation sample.

\medskip
\textbf{Uniqueness and exhaustiveness.} In Cases 1 and 2, uniqueness follows from strict dominance: $\Phi$ has constant sign on $[0,1]$ so no other $\sigma_0$ is consistent with optimality. In Case 3, uniqueness follows from the unique zero of $\Phi$. The three conditions $c_0 > \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, and $\Delta(e(1)) \leq c_0 \leq \Delta(\bar{\theta})$ partition $\mathbb{R}_{+}$ since $\Delta(\bar{\theta}) \geq \Delta(e(1))$ (as $e(0) = \bar{\theta} \geq e(1)$ and $\Delta$ is increasing). Equilibrium exists and is unique for all parameter values.
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Non-Disclosure Premium Characterisation \eqref{eq:premium}}

\begin{proof}
Under the benchmark $\alpha_k=\beta_k$, the equilibrium price of a listing equals its expected buyer valuation. For a low-quality seller who discloses ($d=1$, $\theta=0$), both buyer types value the property at $\beta_0$ (sensitive buyers: $v_1(d=1,\theta=0)=\beta_0+\beta_1\cdot 0=\beta_0$; insensitive buyers: $v_0=\beta_0$), so $p_1^*=\beta_0$. For a non-disclosing listing, sensitive buyers assign expected quality $e(\sigma_0^*)$ and value the property at $\beta_0+\beta_1 e(\sigma_0^*)$, while insensitive buyers value it at $\beta_0$. Weighting by buyer composition yields $p_0^*=\beta_0+\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$, so the non-disclosure premium is:
\begin{equation}
    p_0^*-p_1^* = \rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*). \label{eq:premium_derived}
\end{equation}
The three cases of~\eqref{eq:premium} follow by substituting the equilibrium $\sigma_0^*$ from each regime of Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium} into~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 1: Cost-Deterred Hiding ($\sigma_0^*=0$).}
Evaluating~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=0$:
\[
    e(0) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{(1-\pi)} = \bar{\theta}.
\]
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}: $p_0^*-p_1^* = \rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 2: Complete Adverse Selection ($\sigma_0^*=1$).}
With $\sigma_1^*=0$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose}, evaluating~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=1$:
\[
    e(1) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})+(1-\pi)} = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}.
\]
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}: $p_0^*-p_1^* = \rho\,\beta_1\,\dfrac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 3: Partial Pooling ($\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$).}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}, $\sigma_0^*$ solves the indifference condition $\Delta(e(\sigma_0^*))=c_0$, i.e., $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)=c_0$. But the left-hand side is $p_0^*-p_1^*$ by~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}, so $p_0^*-p_1^*=c_0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Ordering.}
Since $e$ is strictly decreasing by Lemma~\ref{lem:belief_monotone}, we have $e(1)<e(\sigma_0^*)<e(0)$ for all $\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$, hence $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1)<c_0<\rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}$, confirming the strict ordering stated after~\eqref{eq:premium}.
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:comparative_statics} (Comparative statics of the non-disclosure premium)}

\begin{proof}
From~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}, the non-disclosure premium is $\Delta\equiv p_0^*-p_1^*=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$, where the belief $e(\sigma_0)$ is given by~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}. The equilibrium hiding rate $\sigma_0^*\in[0,1]$ is determined by the regime characterised in Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}. We derive $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi$ and $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho$ in each of the three regimes, in turn.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 1: Cost-Deterred Hiding ($\sigma_0^*=0$).}
No informed low-quality seller hides, so the non-disclosing pool consists entirely of uninformed sellers. From~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=0$:
\begin{equation}
    e(0) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{(1-\pi)} = \bar{\theta},
\end{equation}
which is independent of $\pi$. The premium is therefore:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta = \rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}.
\end{equation}
Since $\bar{\theta}$ is a structural parameter, $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi=0$ and $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho=\beta_1\bar{\theta}>0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 2: Complete Adverse Selection ($\sigma_0^*=1$).}
All informed low-quality sellers hide. From~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=1$:
\begin{equation}
    e(1) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})+(1-\pi)} = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}},
\end{equation}
so $\Delta=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1)$. Differentiating with respect to $\pi$ by the quotient rule:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial e(1)}{\partial\pi}
        = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\pi\bar{\theta})-(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}\cdot(-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2}
        = \frac{\bar{\theta}(\bar{\theta}-1)}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2}
        = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2} < 0,
\end{equation}
hence $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi = \rho\,\beta_1\cdot\partial e(1)/\partial\pi < 0$.
Differentiating with respect to $\rho$ (with $e(1)$ determined solely by $\pi$ and $\bar\theta$):
$\partial\Delta/\partial\rho=\beta_1\,e(1)>0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 3: Partial Pooling ($\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$).}
The interior equilibrium satisfies $c_0=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$, so the premium is pinned at the hiding cost:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta = c_0.
\end{equation}
Changes in $\pi$ or $\rho$ induce compensating adjustments in $\sigma_0^*$ that restore the indifference condition, leaving the premium invariant: $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi=0$ and $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho=0$.

To verify: from~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}, $\partial e(\sigma_0)/\partial\pi = -\bar{\theta}(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0/[\pi(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0+(1-\pi)]^2 < 0$ for $\sigma_0>0$. Total differentiation of $c_0=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*(\pi),\pi)$ with respect to $\pi$ gives:
\begin{equation}
    0 = \rho\,\beta_1\left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial\sigma_0}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_0^*}{\mathrm{d}\pi} + \frac{\partial e}{\partial\pi}\right),
\end{equation}
so $\mathrm{d}\sigma_0^*/\mathrm{d}\pi = -(\partial e/\partial\pi)/(\partial e/\partial\sigma_0) < 0$: as $\pi$ rises, informed sellers populate the non-disclosing pool more heavily, so fewer low-quality sellers need to hide to sustain the same buyer belief, and $\sigma_0^*$ falls.

\medskip
\textbf{Summary.} The non-disclosure premium is \emph{weakly} decreasing in $\pi$ and \emph{weakly} increasing in $\rho$, with strict monotonicity only in the Complete Adverse Selection regime. In Case~1, the premium is determined by the population quality $\bar{\theta}$ of the uninformed pool and is insensitive to market fundamentals $\pi$ and $\rho$. In Case~3, the premium is pinned at the primitive $c_0$ by the indifference condition; it is the hiding rate $\sigma_0^*$, not the premium, that absorbs variation in $\pi$ and $\rho$.
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:rho} (Effect of $\rho$ on the non-disclosure premium)}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Part~(i).}
From~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}, the non-disclosure premium is $\Delta = \rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$. We differentiate with respect to $\rho$ in each regime, holding the regime-specific equilibrium $\sigma_0^*$ fixed where appropriate.

\medskip
\textit{Cost-Deterred Hiding ($\sigma_0^* = 0$).} Here $e(0) = \bar{\theta}$ by~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}, independent of $\rho$. Therefore $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho = \beta_1\bar{\theta} > 0$.

\textit{Complete Adverse Selection ($\sigma_0^* = 1$).} Here $e(1) = (1-\pi)\bar{\theta}/(1-\pi\bar{\theta})$, also independent of $\rho$. Therefore $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho = \beta_1\,e(1) > 0$.

\textit{Partial Pooling ($\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$).} The indifference condition $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*) = c_0$ pins $\Delta = c_0$. Changes in $\rho$ induce compensating adjustments in $\sigma_0^*$, as established in Proposition~\ref{prop:comparative_statics}(ii): $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial\rho > 0$. The premium satisfies $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho = 0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Part~(ii).}
From Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}, the \emph{Partial Pooling} regime requires $\Delta(e(1)) \leq c_0 \leq \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, i.e., $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1) \leq c_0 \leq \rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}$. Differentiating both bounds with respect to $\rho$: $\partial[\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1)]/\partial\rho = \beta_1\,e(1) > 0$ and $\partial[\rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}]/\partial\rho = \beta_1\,\bar{\theta} > 0$. Both boundaries increase with $\rho$. The width of the partial-pooling interval is $\rho\,\beta_1\,[\bar{\theta} - e(1)]$, which is increasing in $\rho$ since $\bar{\theta} > e(1)$ whenever $\pi > 0$. The regime is therefore weakly expanding in $\rho$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:pi} (Equilibrium effect of $\pi$: equilibrium moves toward full unraveling)}

\begin{proof}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}, the equilibrium belief under partial separation is:
\begin{equation}
    e(1) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1 - \pi\bar{\theta}}
\end{equation}

Taking the derivative:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial e(1)}{\partial \pi} = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\pi\bar{\theta}) - (1-\pi)\bar{\theta}(-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2} = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2} < 0
\end{equation}

As $\pi$ increases, $e(1)$ falls: the pool quality under partial separation deteriorates because fewer uninformed sellers are available to provide cover.

Since $\Delta(e)$ is increasing in $e$, $\Delta(e(1))$ falls. The condition for partial separation, $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, becomes harder to satisfy. For fixed $c_0$, an increase in $\pi$ can push the equilibrium from partial separation into the mixed or full unraveling region.
\end{proof}

When most sellers are informed ($\pi$ high), non-disclosure is suspicious. Buyers recognize that uninformed sellers are rare, so most non-disclosers must be strategic hiders. The favorable cover provided by uninformed sellers evaporates. Low-quality sellers find hiding less attractive, and the market approaches classical unraveling.

When most buyers care about quality ($\rho$ high), the stakes of disclosure are high. Low-quality sellers have much to lose from revealing their type. The non-disclosure premium is large, making hiding attractive even when pool quality is low. Partial separation is sustained, and by Corollary~\ref{cor:rho}(ii), the partial-pooling interval itself widens with $\rho$.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:c0} (Equilibrium effect of $c_0$: equilibrium moves toward full unraveling)}

\begin{proof}
An increase in $c_0$ shifts the function $\Phi(\sigma_0) = \Delta(e(\sigma_0)) - c_0$ downward. The boundaries become:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \Phi(0) &= \Delta(\bar{\theta}) - c_0 \quad \text{(decreases)} \\
        \Phi(1) &= \Delta(e(1)) - c_0 \quad \text{(decreases)}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The condition for full unraveling, $c_0 > \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, becomes easier to satisfy. The condition for partial separation, $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, becomes harder to satisfy.
\end{proof}

Higher hiding costs make concealment less attractive. Regulatory enforcement, administrative burden, or reputational concerns that raise $c_0$ push the market toward disclosure.

\subsection*{Proof of Lemma \ref{lem:threshold_regimes} (Equilibrium reduction in partial pooling)}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Pi(d=0)\equiv\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ where the equilibrium belief is $e^* = e(\sigma_0^*)$ from~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}. The revenue $\Pi(d=1,\theta)$ is independent of $\sigma_0^*$ since buyers observe $\theta$ directly under disclosure (Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose}).

\textit{Partial Pooling.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}, in the interior regime $\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$ is determined by the indifference condition
\begin{equation}
    \Pi(d=0;e^*) - c_0 \;=\; \Pi(d=1,\theta=0),
    \label{eq:pp_indifference}
\end{equation}
which states that an informed low-quality seller is indifferent between hiding (paying $c_0$ for the inflated pool revenue) and disclosing low quality. Substituting~\eqref{eq:pp_indifference} into the threshold definition~\eqref{eq:threshold},
\begin{equation*}
    \bar R^{\text{PP}} \;=\; \Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \Pi(d=0;e^*) + c_0 \;=\; \Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \Pi(d=1,\theta=0).
\end{equation*}
The cancellation is exact: the apparent $+c_0$ in the threshold formula is offset by the equilibrium movement of $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ (which in PP exceeds $\Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$ by exactly $c_0$). $\bar R^{\text{PP}}$ depends on $\rho$ and $X$ only.

\textit{Complete Adverse Selection.} The regime $\sigma_0^* = 1$ obtains because hiding strictly dominates disclose-low at the equilibrium pool composition $e^{\text{CAS}}=(1-\pi)\bar\theta/(1-\pi\bar\theta)$: $\Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}}) - c_0 > \Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$. The corner is locally stable in $c_0$ within the regime's defining inequality, so $\sigma_0^*$ and $e^*$ are locally constant in $c_0$, and the threshold retains its primitive form $\bar R^{\text{CAS}} = \Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}}) + c_0$ with $\partial \bar R^{\text{CAS}}/\partial c_0 = 1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:subsidy} (Subsidy comparative statics by regime)}

\noindent\textit{Throughout, we use the regime-conditional form of $\bar R$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold_regimes}. We assume $G$ is log-concave so that $\Pi(d,\theta;e) = \max_p \phi(p)\Gamma(p\mid d,\theta;e)$ admits a unique interior maximiser $p^*(d,\theta;e)$ and is differentiable in $e$. The optimal subsidy $S^* = \max\{\hat R(X) - \bar R, 0\}$ is piecewise linear in its arguments; we evaluate the derivatives on the active region $\hat R(X) > \bar R$. The relation $\partial S^*/\partial \bar R = -1$ then translates each derivative of $\bar R$ into the corresponding derivative of $S^*$.}

\subsubsection*{Partial Pooling}

\begin{proof}[Effect of $c_0$]
From Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold_regimes}, in PP, $\bar R^{\text{PP}} = \Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \Pi(d=1,\theta=0)$. Both terms are verified-disclosure revenues with $\theta$ observable; neither depends on the equilibrium hiding rate, the non-disclosing pool, or the cost of hiding. Hence $\partial \bar R^{\text{PP}}/\partial c_0 = 0$, and consequently $\partial S^*/\partial c_0 = 0$ when $\hat R(X) > \bar R^{\text{PP}}$.

Mechanism. The equilibrium indifference~\eqref{eq:pp_indifference} forces $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ to track $c_0$ one-for-one through the equilibrium response of $\sigma_0^*$, so the apparent $+c_0$ in the primitive threshold formula~\eqref{eq:threshold} is exactly cancelled by the equilibrium movement of $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$. The renovation incentive reduces to the verified-quality premium, which involves no hiding-related object.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Effect of $\pi$]
The verified-quality revenues $\Pi(d=1,\theta\in\{0,1\})$ are independent of the non-disclosing pool composition and hence of $\pi$. Therefore $\partial \bar R^{\text{PP}}/\partial \pi = 0$ and $\partial S^*/\partial \pi = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Effect of $\rho$]
Buyer valuations under disclosure satisfy $v_1(d=1,\theta) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \theta + \sum_n \beta_n X_n$ and $v_0 = \beta_0 + \sum_n \beta_n X_n$. The aggregate demand under disclosure is
\begin{equation*}
    \Gamma(p \mid d=1, \theta) = \rho\, G(v_1(d=1,\theta)-p) + (1-\rho)\, G(v_0 - p).
\end{equation*}
By the envelope theorem applied to $\Pi(d=1,\theta) = \max_p \phi(p)\Gamma(p\mid d=1,\theta)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \Pi(d=1,\theta)}{\partial \rho} = \phi(p^*)\, \big[G(v_1(d=1,\theta)-p^*) - G(v_0-p^*)\big].
\end{equation*}
Subtracting the $\theta=0$ from the $\theta=1$ case (noting $v_1(d=1,\theta=0) = v_0$, so the bracket is zero at $\theta=0$ and strictly positive at $\theta=1$ since $\beta_1>0$ and $G$ is strictly increasing):
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \bar R}{\partial \rho} = \frac{\partial \Pi(d=1,\theta=1)}{\partial \rho} - \frac{\partial \Pi(d=1,\theta=0)}{\partial \rho} > 0,
\end{equation*}
where the optimal price $p^*$ in each term is evaluated at its own argument. Consequently $\partial S^*/\partial \rho \leq 0$ in the active region.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Complete Adverse Selection}

\begin{proof}[Effect of $c_0$]
In CAS, $\sigma_0^* = 1$ is locally constant in $c_0$ within the regime's defining inequality, hence $e^{\text{CAS}}$ is independent of $c_0$ and so is $\Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}})$. Differentiating $\bar R^{\text{CAS}} = \Pi(d=1,\theta=1) - \Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}}) + c_0$ directly yields $\partial \bar R/\partial c_0 = 1$, hence $\partial S^*/\partial c_0 = -1$ when $\hat R(X) > \bar R$. The textbook substitutability between hiding-cost enforcement and renovation subsidies thus holds only at this corner.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Effect of $\pi$]
In CAS, $e^{\text{CAS}} = (1-\pi)\bar\theta/(1-\pi\bar\theta)$ with $\partial e^{\text{CAS}}/\partial \pi < 0$. By the envelope theorem,
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}})}{\partial \pi} = \frac{\partial \Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}})}{\partial e} \cdot \frac{\partial e^{\text{CAS}}}{\partial \pi},
\end{equation*}
with $\partial \Pi/\partial e = \phi(p^*)\rho\beta_1\, g(v_1(d=0) - p^*) > 0$ (because $v_1(d=0)=\beta_0+\beta_1 e+\sum_n\beta_n X_n$ is increasing in $e$ and $G$ is strictly increasing). Hence $\partial \Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}})/\partial \pi < 0$ and $\partial \bar R/\partial \pi = -\partial \Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}})/\partial \pi > 0$, giving $\partial S^*/\partial \pi \leq 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Effect of $\rho$]
The argument follows the PP case, with the additional step that $\Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}})$ depends on $\rho$ through the share of EPC-sensitive buyers in aggregate demand; $e^{\text{CAS}}$ itself is independent of $\rho$. Applying the envelope theorem to both $\Pi(d=1,\theta=1)$ and $\Pi(d=0;e^{\text{CAS}})$ and using $v_1(d=1,\theta=1) > v_1(d=0)$ yields $\partial \bar R^{\text{CAS}}/\partial \rho > 0$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Effect of renovation cost $\hat R(X)$}

\begin{proof}
In the active region $\hat R(X) > \bar R$, the unconstrained form $S^* = \hat R(X) - \bar R$ applies, giving $\partial S^*/\partial \hat R(X) = 1$. In the inactive region, $S^* = 0$ and the derivative is zero. The indicator-function statement in Proposition~\ref{prop:subsidy} captures both.
\end{proof}

\medskip

\noindent\textit{Summary of policy reading.} The substitutability $\partial S^*/\partial c_0 = -1$ obtains only in the Complete Adverse Selection corner, where the equilibrium hiding rate is at the boundary and $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ is locally constant in $c_0$. In the partial-pooling regime, which describes the bulk of French apartment and house markets, the equilibrium indifference identity forces $\Pi(d=0;e^*)$ to move one-for-one with $c_0$, so the apparent $+c_0$ in the threshold formula is cancelled exactly and $\partial S^*/\partial c_0 = 0$. Disclosure enforcement and renovation subsidies are therefore policy complements rather than substitutes outside the CAS corner: enforcement shifts the within-stock composition of hiders and disclosers along the indifference curve, while subsidies shift the stock itself toward verified high quality.
