\leftalignedtitle{Does Silence Pay? Mandatory Disclosure and Limited Attention in
  Housing Markets
}

\leftalignedauthor{
    Bastien Patras, \href{mailto:bastien.patras@sciencespo.fr}{bastien.patras@sciencespo.fr}$^\dagger$\\
    {\small $^\dagger$PhD Student, Sciences Po Department of Economics, Paris, France}\\
}
\vspace{1em}

\abs{
\textbf{Abstract}\\[0.5em]
Standard disclosure theory predicts that sellers of high-quality products always disclose, so buyers read silence as the lowest quality and unrated products should sell at or below disclosed poor ones. Yet after July 2021, when disclosed energy ratings became legally opposable in France, the price gap between unrated and disclosed inefficient listings widened by about 7\% (apartments) and 3.5\% (houses) on average across markets. We match listings from SeLoger, a major French housing platform, to the ADEME national registry of certified energy audits, which shows whether the seller holds a certificate, something no buyer can see. We use this information to build a Dye-like disclosure game with limited attention and extend it to renovation. Uninformed sellers shelter those who hide, and buyers differ in how sensitive they are to energy efficiency. Buyers also pay limited attention to silence and deduct only a small fraction of the energy cost the registry assigns to silent listings. Together, these components make silence profitable. Informed sellers with poor ratings may then stay silent rather than renovate, so we ask what subsidy it takes. Silence does not raise the subsidy a seller needs: she renovates once the subsidy covers her cost net of the rating premium. Our results show that, for a light renovation costing \euro{}250 per square metre, the state must cover at least \attBaseCostShareTwoFiftyApt{}\% of the cost on average in apartment markets that need support (\attBaseCostShareTwoFiftyHouse{}\% in house markets), because the rating premium repays only the rest. Needs vary widely across markets, so a uniform subsidy misfires in France: a grant set to reach the neediest market pays almost twice the median need, and one set at the median leaves half of the markets short. % [AUTHOR REWRITE]
}

\subsection{What the Model Adds}
\label{ss:policy_scope}

% [AUTHOR REWRITE] section opening
The model of Section~\ref{sec:model} treats $\theta$ as exogenous, but the policy question is what happens once quality becomes a choice. An informed seller who holds a low-quality certificate can hide it, disclose it, or renovate and disclose a verified high rating, and a subsidy is the instrument when renovation costs more than the market pays for it. We study that choice in the model of Section~\ref{ss:attention}, in which a buyer's attention to energy depends on whether the listing displays a rating, at the estimates of Section~\ref{ss:att_estimates}: $\hat\psi_\delta=\attPsiApt{}$ in apartments and \attPsiHouse{} in houses. The full-attention benchmark of Appendix~\ref{sec:equilibrium} is the case $\psi=\omega_1=1$, so every statement below reduces to its full-attention counterpart at that point.

Two caveats frame what the model adds. First, wherever the renovation threshold is the price gap $b$ between a verified efficient and a verified inefficient rating (Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}), that gap is the energy-efficiency premium $\hat\beta_1$ that Section~\ref{sec:identification} estimates directly, so the model does not produce a new number there. What it adds is the identification of which price gap governs renovation (the rating premium $\hat\beta_1$, rather than $\hat\Delta$ or $\hat b-\hat\Delta+c_0$), the conditions under which that reading fails, and the counterfactuals of Sections~\ref{ss:policy_ge} and~\ref{ss:att_policy}, which need a model to say what happens to hiding and to the population of eligible sellers. Second, the instruments in Table~\ref{tab:instruments} (Section~\ref{ss:att_policy}) act on different margins of the seller's problem, and only one of them moves the threshold outside Complete Adverse Selection.

The hiding cost is a single $c_0$ per market, held at the value that reproduces the observed hiding rate at the estimated attention, which averages \attCzeroFitApt{} log points in apartments and \attCzeroFitHouse{} in houses. Uninformed sellers have no strategy set, and the thresholds are computed holding beliefs fixed, except in Section~\ref{ss:policy_ge}, which lets the pool respond.

\subsection{The Renovation Problem}
\label{ss:renov_problem}

What does renovation gain a seller who could also hide? Answering requires one addition to the model: a renovation decision $r\in\{0,1\}$ that turns an informed low-quality seller into an informed high-quality seller with a new certificate.\footnote{Uninformed sellers have no strategy set: information status is a friction rather than a choice (Section~\ref{ss:model_setup}), so they cannot display, hide or renovate, since a renovation decision presupposes a diagnostic. They enter the analysis through the composition of the silent pool and, through $\pi$, the size of the population of potential renovators.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:att_prices}, a disclosed low rating sells for $p_{\rm L}$, a disclosed high rating for $p_{\rm L}+b$ and a silent listing for $p_{\rm L}+b\tilde e$, where $b=\rho\omega_1\beta_1$ and $\tilde e$ is the effective belief of Definition~\ref{def:effective_belief}. Measure payoffs relative to a disclosed low rating and write
\[
    h\;\equiv\;b\,\tilde e-c_0
\]
for the net gain from hiding. A subsidy $S\ge0$ lowers the renovation cost $\hat R$ that the seller faces to $\hat R-S$. Table~\ref{tab:strategies} lists the four strategies. We set $c_1=0$, so a renovated seller who hides bears the same cost as before.

\begin{lemma}[A renovated seller behaves like an informed high type]
\label{lem:renov_behaviour}
Strategy~4 is weakly dominated by Strategy~3 if and only if $b(\tilde e-1)\le c_0$, the condition under which an informed high-quality seller discloses. This holds in Cost-Deterred Hiding and in Partial Pooling (Corollary~\ref{cor:att_hq}) and in Complete Adverse Selection when $\sigma_1^*=0$. In Complete Adverse Selection with $\sigma_1^*>0$ a renovated seller also hides.
\end{lemma}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:attention}. $\Box$

\begin{definition}[Renovation Threshold]
\label{def:threshold}
The renovation threshold is the highest renovation cost, net of subsidy, at which an informed low-quality seller prefers to renovate to her best option without renovating:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{R}\;\equiv\;\max\{b,\,h\}\;-\;\max\{0,\,h\}\;=\;b-\min\big\{\max\{h,0\},\,b\big\}\;\in\;[0,\,b].
    \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\noindent Her payoff from renovating is $\max\{b,h\}-(\hat R-S)$ and her payoff from not renovating is $\max\{0,h\}$, so she renovates if and only if $\hat R-S\le\bar R$. When hiding is her best unrenovated option ($h\ge0$) and a renovated seller displays ($h\le b$), the threshold is $b-h=b-b\tilde e+c_0$, the form in which the problem first suggests itself: the market premium for verified quality, net of the gross price of silence, plus the hiding cost she no longer pays.

\begin{definition}[Minimum incentive-compatible subsidy]
\label{def:minimum_subsidy}
The minimum incentive-compatible subsidy is the smallest transfer that makes renovation weakly preferred for a seller with renovation cost $\hat R$, holding equilibrium beliefs fixed:
\begin{equation}
    S^*\;\equiv\;\max\!\left\{\hat{R}-\bar{R},\;0\right\}.
    \label{eq:subsidy}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Remark ($S^*$ is incentive-compatibility, not welfare optimum).}
The object $S^*$ is the minimum transfer that flips the marginal informed low-quality seller's decision from not renovating to renovating, given everyone else's behaviour. It is \emph{not} a Ramsey-optimal subsidy in the sense of \citet{ramsey_taxation_1927}. A welfare problem would maximise
\[
    \max_S \; \int \mathbf{1}\{\hat R \le \bar R + S\} \cdot \big[\Delta W(\hat R) - \mu S\big]\, dF_R(\hat R),
\]
where $\Delta W(\hat R)$ is the social gain per renovation (private surplus change, any energy-use externality, and the change in the transfer that buyers make to sellers who stay silent), $\mu\ge 1$ is the marginal cost of public funds and $F_R$ is the within-market distribution of renovation costs. With a degenerate $F_R$ the objective is a step in $S$, and $S^*=\hat R-\bar R$ is optimal only if the social gain per renovation covers its cost to the public purse, $\Delta W\ge\mu S^*$; otherwise the optimum is no subsidy. We treat $S^*$ as the operative positive object and leave the Ramsey characterisation to future work.

\subsection{The Renovation Threshold}
\label{ss:threshold}

\begin{proposition}[Renovation Threshold]
\label{prop:threshold}
Let $e(1)=(1-\pi)\bar\theta/(1-\pi\bar\theta)$ and take the equilibrium regime of Proposition~\ref{prop:att_equilibrium}. In every regime $\bar R\le b$, so the minimum subsidy satisfies $S^*\ge\max\{\hat R-b,0\}$. By regime:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Cost-Deterred Hiding and Partial Pooling.} $\bar R=b=\rho\omega_1\beta_1$. Within these regimes it does not depend on $c_0$, $\pi$, $\psi$ or $\zeta^{\rm def}$, and $\partial\bar R/\partial(\rho\omega_1)=\beta_1>0$.
    \item \textbf{Complete Adverse Selection} (every informed low-quality seller hides, so $h>0$).
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
        \item \emph{High-quality sellers disclose} ($\sigma_1^*=0$): $\bar R=b-h=b\,[1-\tilde e(1,0)]+c_0\in[0,b)$, with $\tilde e(1,0)=\psi e(1)+(1-\psi)e^{\rm def}$. Table~\ref{tab:threshold_cs} in Appendix~\ref{app:attention} gives the derivatives.
        \item \emph{High-quality sellers also hide} ($\sigma_1^*>0$): $\bar R=0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
At $\psi=\omega_1=1$ the proposition is the full-attention statement: $b=\rho\beta_1$, $\tilde e=e$, and only the case in which high-quality sellers disclose exists in Complete Adverse Selection.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:attention}. $\Box$

\medskip

The Cost-Deterred Hiding and Partial Pooling case is the central result of this section, and the mechanism is the one that Section~\ref{ss:renov_problem} makes visible. In Partial Pooling the marginal informed low-quality seller is exactly indifferent between hiding and disclosing a low rating, so $h=0$ and her best option without renovating is worth a disclosed low rating. Renovating gains her $b$ and nothing else. The hiding cost has cancelled, because the equilibrium hiding rate adjusts until the net gain from hiding is zero. In Cost-Deterred Hiding she does not hide and the same comparison applies. Raising the hiding cost therefore moves the hiding rate along the indifference condition and leaves the threshold where it was: tightening enforcement against concealment cannot buy renovation in these regimes; only a subsidy that improves the renovate-and-disclose payoff can.

Under Complete Adverse Selection every informed low-quality seller hides and hiding pays strictly, so renovating means giving up the rent $h$ and the threshold falls to $b-h$. Enforcement lowers $h$ and so raises the threshold one for one. The sign of $\partial\bar R/\partial(\rho\omega_1)$ in Complete Adverse Selection is the sign of $1-\tilde e$, which is negative whenever silence sells above a verified efficient rating, as it does in apartments.

Does the data place any market in Complete Adverse Selection? The bound $S^*\ge\max\{\hat R-b,0\}$ is the claim we rest on, and in our application the regime behind it is imposed, not estimated. Throughout, $\psi$ is $\hat\psi_\delta$, which Assumption~\ref{assum:no_pre_pricing} makes post-reform relative attention. Each market's hiding cost is calibrated so that its observed hiding rate is an equilibrium at that attention, and an interior hiding rate is an equilibrium only in Partial Pooling, so every market is in Partial Pooling by construction (\attScBaseNPpApt{} of \attNMarketsApt{} apartment markets and \attScBaseNPpHouse{} of \attNMarketsHouse{} house markets). The data do not confirm the placement: the observed premium lies inside its own Partial Pooling band in \attShareInBandApt{} percent of apartment and \attShareInBandHouse{} percent of house markets.

Reading the hiding cost instead off each market's premium, $c_0=\hat\Delta_m$, places \attRawNCasApt{} apartment and \attRawNCasHouse{} house markets in Complete Adverse Selection, with mean threshold \attRawRbarRatioApt{} and \attRawRbarRatioHouse{} of $b$ (Appendix~\ref{app:attention}); we do not take that reading, because it returns negative costs in \attRawNNegApt{} apartment and \attRawNNegHouse{} house markets. What holds under both readings is $\bar R\le b$, so whatever the hiding cost, the subsidy required is at least the excess of $\hat R$ over the rating premium $\hat b$.

The threshold itself needs no assumption about the default. The level of $\hat\psi_\delta$ and the hiding response in the counterfactuals below need the zero-default assumption. Because $\bar R=\hat b$ in the calibrated regime, the minimum subsidy does not depend on the observed hiding rate or on $\hat\Delta$, which is an implication for how subsidy needs vary across markets; testing it needs programme take-up by commune, which we do not have.

\subsection{Subsidies and Disclosure in Equilibrium}
\label{ss:policy_ge}

Does the response of the silent pool change the subsidy? The threshold holds beliefs fixed. A subsidy that induces renovation also removes disclosed low ratings from the market, and the silent pool responds. The next proposition lets it. Let $F$ be the share of informed low-quality sellers who renovate and $e(1;F)=(1-\pi)\bar\theta/\big(1-\pi+\pi(1-\bar\theta)(1-F)\big)$ the pool belief when every one of the remaining informed low-quality sellers hides.

\begin{proposition}[Subsidies and Disclosure]
\label{prop:subsidy_ge}
Take a market in Partial Pooling with $\psi>0$, and write $e^*$ for its pool belief~\eqref{eq:att_pp} and $\sigma_0^*$ for its hiding rate. The cost distribution $F_R$ is continuous, except where stated.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \emph{Phase 1.} For $F\le1-\sigma_0^*$ the mass of silent informed low-quality sellers, $(1-\pi)(\bar\theta-e^*)/e^*$ per listing, does not depend on $F$. The premium stays at $c_0$, the threshold stays at $b$, and the hiding rate among informed low-quality sellers who have not renovated rises to $\sigma_0^*/(1-F)$.
    \item \emph{Phase 2.} For $F>1-\sigma_0^*$ every remaining informed low-quality seller hides, the market is in Complete Adverse Selection, and $\bar R(F)=b-\psi b\,[e(1;F)-e^*]$, which falls as $F$ rises.
    \item \emph{Equilibrium.} The renovating share $F^*$ solves $F=F_R(\bar R(F)+S)$ and is unique. If instead all sellers share one cost $\hat R$ (a point mass, where equilibrium involves mixing at the atom), every informed low-quality seller renovates in a pure-strategy equilibrium if and only if $S\ge\hat R-b+\psi b\,(\bar\theta-e^*)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:attention}. $\Box$

\medskip

Inside Partial Pooling the indifference of the marginal hider pins down the belief $e^*$, and with it the number of silent informed sellers. Renovators therefore come out of the stock of sellers who would have disclosed a low rating. A subsidy buys renovation but not disclosure until that stock is exhausted: the premium does not move, and the share of the remaining informed low-quality sellers who hide rises. 

The stock is large. Disclosed poor ratings are \attGeDisclosedShareApt{} percent of informed low-quality apartment listings and \attGeDisclosedShareHouse{} percent of house listings, so up to that share can renovate at the partial-equilibrium subsidy $S^*$ without changing the number of hiders or the premium. Beyond it the remaining sellers hide and the threshold falls, so the subsidy needed for full take-up exceeds $S^*$ by $\psi b(\bar\theta-e^*)$, which is at most the width of the Partial Pooling band (Corollary~\ref{cor:att_band}). At the estimates this correction averages \attGeCorrPctBApt{} percent of $b$ in apartments (\euro{}\attGeCorrEurApt{} per m\textsuperscript{2}) and \attGeCorrPctBHouse{} percent in houses (\euro{}\attGeCorrEurHouse{}), so the partial-equilibrium subsidy is the general-equilibrium one to within about one percent of the rating premium. 

Uniqueness follows from Phase~2, where renovation decisions are strategic substitutes through the silent pool: each renovation removes a hider, raises the pool belief and with it the gain from hiding for those left, and so lowers the threshold for the next renovator. In markets where a subsidy reaches more informed low-quality sellers, the registry hiding rate among those who remain should rise while the non-disclosure premium stays flat, and this prediction does not rely on any calibrated quantity. Testing it requires programme take-up by commune and registry data after the subsidy.

\subsection{Renovation Subsidies in French Housing Markets: Magnitudes and Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:policy_empirical}
% ============================================================

% [AUTHOR REWRITE] magnitudes
In Partial Pooling and Cost-Deterred Hiding the threshold equals the rating premium $b$. Because $\hat\beta_1$ measures the quality premium in log points against a local price per m\textsuperscript{2}, the nominal threshold $\bar R_m=\hat b_m\times P_m$ scales with the local price level, and high-price urban markets have the largest verified-quality premia in euros even when the proportional premium is similar across locations. Across the \attNMarketsApt{} estimable apartment markets, $\bar R_m$ averages \euro{}\attScBaseRbarApt{}/m\textsuperscript{2} (median \euro{}\attBaseRbarMedApt{}, range \euro{}\attBaseRbarMinApt{} to \euro{}\attBaseRbarMaxApt{}); across the \attNMarketsHouse{} house markets the mean is \euro{}\attScBaseRbarHouse{} and the median \euro{}\attBaseRbarMedHouse{} (Figure~\ref{fig:policy_rbar_dist}).\footnote{These figures use the rating premium itself, $\bar R=\hat b$. The exact-pricing solver of Appendix~\ref{sec:equilibrium}, which takes the threshold from the full-attention equilibrium, gives means of \euro{}\attRbarCoreEurTwoFiftyApt{} and \euro{}\attRbarCoreEurTwoFiftyHouse{} per m\textsuperscript{2}. The difference reflects linear versus exact pricing; attention cannot explain it, since it is of the same size in the two segments (Appendix~\ref{app:attention}).}

The minimum subsidy is $S^*_m=\max\{\hat R-\bar R_m,0\}$, evaluated at two renovation-cost benchmarks: \euro{}250/m\textsuperscript{2} (a light, ``geste par geste'' intervention such as roof or wall insulation) and \euro{}500/m\textsuperscript{2} (a medium, two-measure upgrade), drawing on MaPrimeR\'enov' programme documentation.\footnote{MaPrimeR\'enov' distinguishes ``geste par geste'' interventions (insulation of walls or roof, costing roughly \euro{}100--400/m\textsuperscript{2} of treated area) from comprehensive ``r\'enovation d'ampleur'' operations (\euro{}700--1{,}200/m\textsuperscript{2} or more); the \euro{}500/m\textsuperscript{2} benchmark corresponds broadly to a two-measure upgrade.} The benchmarks are illustrative. The light benchmark corresponds to a partial upgrade and not to the full move from an E--G to an A--D rating that the premium $b$ prices, and the medium benchmark only approaches it; a comprehensive renovation at the cost range of the footnote would exceed the threshold in every market. A single $\hat R$ per market predicts that no informed low-quality seller exists where $\hat R<\hat b$, yet low ratings are disclosed in every market, so effective renovation costs exceed the benchmark in the \attBaseNoSubsidyTwoFiftyApt{} percent of apartment and \attBaseNoSubsidyTwoFiftyHouse{} percent of house markets where it falls below the premium. At $\hat R=250$, \attScBaseShareTwoFiftyApt{} percent of apartment markets require a positive subsidy; conditional on requiring one, the median $S^*_m$ is \euro{}\attBaseSMedTwoFiftyApt{}/m\textsuperscript{2}, or about \euro{}\attBaseUnitTwoFiftyApt{} per unit at the median apartment area of \attBaseAreaMedApt{}~m\textsuperscript{2}. For houses, \attScBaseShareTwoFiftyHouse{} percent of markets require support, with a conditional median of \euro{}\attBaseSMedTwoFiftyHouse{}/m\textsuperscript{2} (about \euro{}\attBaseUnitTwoFiftyHouse{} per unit at \attBaseAreaMedHouse{}~m\textsuperscript{2}). At $\hat R=500$ the shares are \attScBaseShareFiveHundredApt{} and \attScBaseShareFiveHundredHouse{} percent and the conditional medians \euro{}\attBaseSMedFiveHundredApt{} and \euro{}\attBaseSMedFiveHundredHouse{}/m\textsuperscript{2}. The cost-sharing ratio $S^*_m/\hat R$ is the regulator's decision variable. Among markets requiring support its mean is \attBaseCostShareTwoFiftyApt{} percent for apartments and \attBaseCostShareTwoFiftyHouse{} percent for houses at the light benchmark, and \attBaseCostShareFiveHundredApt{} and \attBaseCostShareFiveHundredHouse{} percent at the medium one. A fixed rate adjusts to the depth of the intervention and to local conditions, which a fixed euro amount does not.

These thresholds use the estimation markets, selected on a positive quality premium; the mean premium on all markets that pass the volume filter is lower (Table~\ref{tab:attention}, notes), so selection biases $\bar R_m$ upward and the subsidies downward.

No flat subsidy fits every market. Covering every apartment market at the light benchmark takes \euro{}\attBaseSMaxTwoFiftyApt{} per m\textsuperscript{2}, against a conditional median of \euro{}\attBaseSMedTwoFiftyApt{}, and for houses \euro{}\attBaseSMaxTwoFiftyHouse{} against \euro{}\attBaseSMedTwoFiftyHouse{}; at the medium benchmark the figures are \euro{}\attBaseSMaxFiveHundredApt{} against \euro{}\attBaseSMedFiveHundredApt{} and \euro{}\attBaseSMaxFiveHundredHouse{} against \euro{}\attBaseSMedFiveHundredHouse{}. A uniform national subsidy is therefore poorly targeted because the cost-sharing requirement varies across markets, although $S^*$ is an incentive-compatibility object and not a welfare optimum (the remark in Section~\ref{ss:renov_problem}). Figure~\ref{fig:policy_map} maps $S^*_m/\hat R$ at the light benchmark, with bubble size encoding the local threshold $\bar R_m$. A flat per-m\textsuperscript{2} grant set at the cross-market mean would over-pay markets where the private incentive already suffices and under-pay those where the gap is largest. The dispersion is systematic. The cost-sharing ratio is low where $\bar R_m$ is high, and $\bar R_m$ is high where local prices are high and the energy-efficiency premium $\hat\beta_{1,m}$ is large. The premium reflects the capitalised value of verified quality to sensitive buyers and does not measure how many buyers are sensitive (Appendix~\ref{app:rho_identification} shows the two are not identified apart in price data alone). A programme would differentiate the subsidy along these axes and co-target audit-infrastructure investment on markets where the threshold is large but verification is scarce, since $\pi$ does not change the threshold but scales the population it applies to (Section~\ref{ss:att_policy}).

At the light benchmark the bill is \euro{}\attScBaseCostTwoFiftyApt{} billion per year for apartments and \euro{}\attScBaseCostTwoFiftyHouse{} billion for houses, \euro{}\attScCostBaseTotTwoFifty{} billion in all, which, for scale, is \attScCostBaseTotTwoFiftyLo{} to \attScCostBaseTotTwoFiftyHi{} percent of MaPrimeR\'enov's annual budget of roughly \euro{}\attBudgetLo{}--\attBudgetHi{} billion. % [CHECK] cite the budget figure (Cour des comptes or ANAH) before submission.
At the medium benchmark it is \euro{}\attScCostBaseTotFiveHundred{} billion per year, \attScCostBaseTotFiveHundredLo{} to \attScCostBaseTotFiveHundredHi{} percent of the budget. The comparison gives scale only: the bill covers listings on one platform in the estimation markets, counted per listing, whereas the programme reaches all owners, so it is not a share of the programme and does not show the programme to be cheap. The bill covers only the sellers who are in the model's renovation problem, and an extension that lets uninformed sellers acquire a certificate and then renovate would enlarge the eligible population. The regime result makes each $S^*_m$ the smallest subsidy the model allows, so the bill is understated. It also counts a dwelling each time it is listed and ignores the extensive-margin participation decision, which overstates it, while coverage of one platform in the estimation markets understates it. By the remark in Section~\ref{ss:renov_problem} they are incentive-compatibility objects and not optimal transfers: with a marginal cost of public funds $\mu>1$, an externality or a non-degenerate cost distribution, the operative subsidy could differ from $S^*_m$ in either direction.

\subsection{Other Instruments and Counterfactuals}
\label{ss:att_policy}

% [AUTHOR REWRITE] section motivation
The regulator has other instruments besides the subsidy, and they act on different margins of the seller's problem. Table~\ref{tab:instruments} lists them: enforcement against hiding, the share of informed sellers, attention to silent listings and full attention to every listing. Does each instrument move the renovation threshold $\bar R$, and with it the subsidy a seller needs? Does it change who hides? And would a simple measure such as making silence salient reduce what the state has to spend on subsidies? The next proposition answers the first two for each instrument, with the hiding cost held at its calibrated value, and the closing subsection returns to the third.

\begin{proposition}[Instruments]
\label{prop:instruments}
Take a market in Partial Pooling at the observed hiding rate, with beliefs held fixed in the renovation decision, and assume $e^{\rm def}\ge\bar\theta$. In apartments this follows from $\hat\Delta>\hat b$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:att_incentives}(iii)); in houses it uses the zero-default assumption, under which $e^{\rm def}=\eta>1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \emph{Raising the share of informed sellers $\pi$ to one,} with the off-path belief after silence equal to zero, leaves $\bar R=b$ and the minimum subsidy unchanged. It raises the population who can renovate from the informed to every low-quality dwelling, and removes hiding whenever $c_0\ge b(1-\psi)e^{\rm def}$, which holds in any market that starts in Partial Pooling, since with no uninformed sellers a silent listing is priced at the default.
    \item \emph{Making silence as salient as a rating} (raising $\omega_0$ to $\omega_1$, so $\psi\to1$, with $\rho$ and $\omega_1$ fixed). What a displayed rating is worth, $b$, does not change; only the price of silent listings falls, by $\lambda=(1-\hat\psi)\,b\,(e^{\rm def}-e^*)$. The threshold stays at $b$ and the minimum subsidy $S^*$ is unchanged, and with beliefs held fixed so is the share of informed low-quality sellers who renovate at any subsidy. The hiding rate falls weakly, to zero if $c_0\ge b\bar\theta$. In the equilibrium of Proposition~\ref{prop:subsidy_ge} the subsidy needed for full take-up falls by $\hat\psi b(\bar\theta-e^*)-(b\bar\theta-c_0)^+$, which is $\hat\psi b(\bar\theta-e^*)$ when the market moves to Cost-Deterred Hiding.
    \item \emph{Full attention} (every EPC-sensitive buyer attends to energy on every listing, rated or not: $\omega_0=\omega_1=1$, so also $\psi\to1$). The rating premium rises to $\rho\beta_1=b/\omega_1$. The data identify only $\hat\rho=\rho\omega_1$, so $\omega_1\in[\hat\rho,1]$ and the change in the threshold satisfies
    \[
        0\;\le\;\Delta\bar R\;\le\;\max\{\beta_1^{\rm anchor}-\hat b,\,0\}.
    \]
    The lower bound is the maintained reading $\omega_1=1$ and the upper bound corresponds to $\rho=1$. Where $\hat\rho\ge1$ the threshold is unchanged. Outside Complete Adverse Selection $\Delta\bar R=b(1/\omega_1-1)$; in a market that moves into it the threshold is $b(1/\omega_1)[1-e(1)]+c_0$, which is at least $b$.
    \item \emph{Enforcement.} A rise in $c_0$ leaves $\bar R$ unchanged outside Complete Adverse Selection and moves the market toward Cost-Deterred Hiding.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:attention}. $\Box$

\medskip

How do the two attention scenarios differ? Making silence as salient as a rating acts only on silent listings: the price of a displayed rating is unchanged and the price of silence falls, so the sellers who hid have no reason to. Renovation pays the same $b$ as before, so the threshold does not move. Full attention also acts on displayed ratings: a displayed good rating is worth $\rho\beta_1$ instead of $\rho\omega_1\beta_1$. The data identify only the product $\hat\rho=\rho\omega_1$, so they cannot say how much of a low $\hat\rho$ is few EPC-sensitive buyers (then nothing changes) and how much is inattention to displayed ratings (then the premium rises a great deal). The result is a bracket between the maintained reading and an upper bound set by the energy-cost anchor. Table~\ref{tab:att_scenarios} reports the scenarios for both segments.

\paragraph{Salience and hiding.}
A mandatory ``rating not provided'' field in advertisements, or a platform default that treats a missing rating as a rating, would raise attention to silent listings. At a fixed hiding rate the policy lowers the non-disclosure premium by $\hat\lambda_m$, by~\eqref{eq:att_lambda}: \attSalienceApt{} log points in apartments and \attSalienceHouse{} in houses. With the equilibrium response every market moves to Cost-Deterred Hiding. The mean hiding rate falls from \attSigmaObsApt{} to \attSigmaFullApt{} percent in apartments and from \attSigmaObsHouse{} to \attSigmaFullHouse{} percent in houses, and, over the \attSampleYears{} years of listings in the sample, \attCfRevealedApt{} hidden unfavourable ratings of informed apartment sellers become visible, with no additional renovation (Table~\ref{tab:att_scenarios}). The threshold does not move, and neither does the subsidy bill. The apartment response is a knife-edge, because a single hiding cost makes the Partial Pooling band only \attBandWidthApt{} log points wide (Corollary~\ref{cor:att_band}), so we read it as a direction, not an elasticity.

\paragraph{The share of informed sellers.}
Raising $\pi$ to one leaves the per-seller subsidy where it was and changes only the population: the annual bill at the light benchmark rises from \euro{}\attScBaseCostTwoFiftyApt{} to \euro{}\attScPiOneCostTwoFiftyApt{} billion in apartments and from \euro{}\attScBaseCostTwoFiftyHouse{} to \euro{}\attScPiOneCostTwoFiftyHouse{} billion in houses, and hiding disappears.

\paragraph{Full attention.}
The full-attention result bounds how far the threshold could move if every EPC-sensitive buyer attended to energy on every listing. At the upper bound the apartment threshold rises from \euro{}\attScBaseRbarApt{} to \euro{}\attScHiRbarApt{} per m\textsuperscript{2} on average and the annual bill at the light benchmark falls from \euro{}\attScBaseCostTwoFiftyApt{} to \euro{}\attScHiCostTwoFiftyApt{} billion, and at the medium benchmark from \euro{}\attScBaseCostFiveHundredApt{} to \euro{}\attScHiCostFiveHundredApt{} billion (Figure~\ref{fig:policy_bound} in Appendix~\ref{app:attention}). A higher rating premium also raises the gain from hiding, so \attScHiNCasApt{} of \attNMarketsApt{} apartment markets move into Complete Adverse Selection and the mean hiding rate rises to \attScHiSigmaApt{} percent. The lower bound is zero. These are bounds because the data do not separate the share of EPC-sensitive buyers from the attention they pay to displayed ratings, and the upper bound rests on the energy-cost anchor and its ten-year horizon. In houses the same exercise moves \attScHiNCasHouse{} of \attNMarketsHouse{} markets into Complete Adverse Selection at once, with a mean hiding rate of \attScHiSigmaHouse{} percent. With one hiding cost per market every informed seller faces the same comparison, so the rise in the rating premium by a factor of about $1/\hat\rho$ pushes the gain from hiding past that cost for all of them together. With costs that differ across sellers only those whose cost lies below the new gain would start hiding. We regard the jump as a feature of the single cost and report the bound for apartments only.

\paragraph{What the exercises do not show.}
Three limits qualify the results. The first is that $\bar R=b$ requires the market to be outside Complete Adverse Selection, which holds at the calibrated hiding cost by construction (Section~\ref{ss:threshold}). The second is that the zero effect of salience on renovation is an implication of one hiding cost per market. With costs that vary across sellers, an informed seller who strictly prefers hiding would lose her rent by renovating and disclosing, so making hiding less attractive could change her decision; we do not model that margin, and Section~\ref{ss:att_saving} bounds what it could change. The third is that the benchmark renovation costs are illustrative. Salience changes who hides and, to first order, not who renovates, whereas a subsidy changes who renovates and not who hides until the stock of disclosed poor ratings is exhausted (Proposition~\ref{prop:subsidy_ge}).

\subsection{What Salience Could Save}
\label{ss:att_saving}

% [AUTHOR REWRITE] bound on the saving from salience
The result that salience leaves the subsidy unchanged follows from the single hiding cost, and it should not be read as a finding. With one cost per market a seller in Partial Pooling is indifferent between hiding and disclosing, so hiding earns her no rent and renovating destroys none. If hiding costs differ across sellers, those whose cost lies below the gain from hiding earn a strictly positive rent that renovation gives up. Their renovation threshold is below $b$ and they need a larger subsidy. Making silence salient lowers the gain from hiding and so shrinks that rent, and the saving to the state is the fall in these rents. We do not observe the distribution of hiding costs and we do not model it. The next proposition bounds the saving using only what we observe, the hiding rate and the price of silence.

\begin{proposition}[Upper Bound on the Saving from Salience]
\label{prop:att_saving}
Let hiding costs be distributed across informed sellers with any continuous distribution, independently of quality, and assume $e^{\rm def}\ge\bar\theta$ and that only low-quality sellers hide. Let $\hat\sigma_0$ be the observed hiding rate and $\lambda=(1-\hat\psi_\delta)\,b\,(e^{\rm def}-\hat e^*)$ the fall in the price of silence when $\psi\to1$. Let $S_i^*$ be the minimum subsidy of seller $i$, which depends on her own hiding cost. The average fall in $S_i^*$ per eligible seller, in log points of the price, is at most
\[
    \hat\sigma_0\;\min\{\lambda,\;b,\;100\hat R/P_m\},
\]
where $100\hat R/P_m$ is the renovation cost per m\textsuperscript{2} over the local price in log points. The saving is zero in Cost-Deterred Hiding, for any programme that reaches only the sellers who disclose today, and, in Partial Pooling, when all sellers share one hiding cost.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} See Appendix~\ref{app:attention}. $\Box$

\medskip

The cap at $b$ holds because a renovated seller can still hide at the same cost ($c_1=0$), so the rent that renovation destroys cannot exceed the rating premium. If the subsidy required her to display the new rating, the cap would be absent and the bound would be $\hat\sigma_0\min\{\lambda,100\hat R/P_m\}$. At the estimates the bound is \euro{}\attSavingUbTwoFiftyApt{} billion per year in apartments, or \euro{}\attSavingUbDTwoFiftyApt{} billion at the light benchmark and \euro{}\attSavingUbDFiveHundredApt{} billion at the medium one if the renovated seller must display. In houses it is \euro{}\attSavingUbTwoFiftyHouse{} billion, because $\hat\psi$ is close to one and the price of silence barely falls (\attSalienceHouse{} log points). Together the bound is \attSavingUbTotTwoFiftyLo{} to \attSavingUbTotTwoFiftyHi{} percent of MaPrimeR\'enov's annual budget, and the largest apartment market accounts for \attSavingTopShareApt{} percent of it. The bound is on the average of seller-specific subsidies. A single subsidy that must cover every seller in a market is set by the highest $S_i^*$, and for such a programme the bound does not apply: the fall is then at most $\min\{\lambda,b,100\hat R/P_m\}$, without the factor $\hat\sigma_0$.

How sensitive is the bound to its inputs? Using the observed wedge in place of the model's price of silence gives \euro{}\attSavingUbObsTwoFiftyApt{} billion for apartments. The hiding rate behind these figures is the maintained one, \attSigmaObsApt{} percent, which assigns every informed hider to low quality and so also counts hiders whose certificate is A to D; with the rate computed from E to G certificates alone, \attSigmaRegMeanApt{} percent, the bound is \euro{}\attSavingUbRegTwoFiftyApt{} billion.

The bound is not a point estimate, and the data do not pin the saving down further. Hiding rates by EPC letter identify more than one point of the cost distribution: the registry shows them rising from E to G in apartments, \attHideLetterEApt{}, \attHideLetterFApt{} and \attHideLetterGApt{} percent, as a gain from hiding that rises with consumption predicts; year-by-year hiding rates, once certificate dates are re-pulled, would exploit variation in the gain over time; and a direct measure of what concealment costs sellers would give the distribution itself. Even at the bound for seller-specific subsidies the saving is small against the budget, and the apartment figure rests mostly on one market.

\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

Four years after France mandated energy performance disclosure, silence is still rewarded. Properties listed without an EPC score trade at a premium over disclosed low-efficiency properties that the reform raised by 6.9 log points for apartments and 3.4 for houses (about 7\% and 3.5\%) on average across markets, the opposite of what standard unraveling theory predicts. Three departures from the classical framework resolve it.

The resolution requires three departures from the classical framework. First, not all sellers are informed. Only 51.2\% of apartment listings and 40.3\% of house listings match a post-reform certificate in the registry. When buyers observe a missing EPC score, they face genuine ambiguity. The non-disclosing seller may be concealing an unfavorable rating or may simply not know her property's energy performance. Uninformed sellers therefore provide strategic hiders with involuntary cover, preventing silence from being read as the worst possible signal. Second, not all buyers price energy performance into their offers. A smaller share of energy-sensitive buyers shrinks the reward to a verified good rating, and with it the renovation threshold. Third, apartment buyers attend to energy much less when a listing shows no rating than when it shows one, whereas house buyers attend almost as closely; in apartment markets this lets a silent listing sell above a verified efficient one, which no belief about silence can do. Uninformed sellers suffice to keep silence above a poor rating, because they keep it ambiguous; buyer heterogeneity scales the reward to a good rating and the gain from hiding; and limited attention is what lets the premium exceed what any belief can support. % [AUTHOR REWRITE]


These forces generate three equilibrium regimes. In the partial-pooling equilibrium, which our calibration imposes on every market, the fraction of informed low-quality sellers that hides adjusts endogenously until the marginal hider is exactly indifferent between concealment and disclosure. The indifference condition gives the paper's central theoretical result: the non-disclosure premium equals the concealment cost in partial pooling, invariant to the share of informed sellers and the share of energy-sensitive buyers. We calibrate the concealment cost to the premium and the registry-measured belief, which requires no separate estimate of buyer energy-sensitivity, and use the same estimates to test the full-attention implication that silence cannot sell above a verified efficient rating. The implication is not rejected for houses and is rejected for apartments, where the non-disclosure premium exceeds what the registry-measured belief and the energy-efficiency premium predict by a wide and precisely estimated margin. One attention parameter per segment, measuring how much buyers attend to energy on listings that show no rating and fitted to that fact, accounts for silent apartments selling above verified efficient ones; it does not explain which markets carry the largest premia. % [AUTHOR REWRITE]
Strategic hiding is itself uncommon. \attSigmaObsApt{}\% of informed apartment sellers with unfavorable ratings withhold them, versus \attSigmaObsHouse{}\% for houses, a rate that non-strategic silence among informed sellers may inflate (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} reads it from the registry). The premium persists with little strategic hiding because uninformed sellers keep silence ambiguous: the hiders' indifference sets its level at the hiding cost, and limited attention lifts it above a verified efficient rating in apartments. The informed hider merely breaks even, since the premium she earns equals what hiding costs her.

Although the renovation threshold mechanically incorporates the concealment cost, the seller's indifference condition causes that cost to cancel exactly in partial pooling. The renovation incentive reduces to the verified-quality premium, the price gap between high-quality and low-quality disclosed properties, and is invariant to both the concealment cost and the share of informed sellers. Tightening enforcement against non-disclosure therefore does not move the renovation threshold outside the corner in which every informed low-quality seller hides. The regulator has three instruments that act on distinct margins: renovation subsidies (closing the gap between the verified-quality premium and the renovation cost), enforcement against strategic hiding (which lowers hiding but moves the threshold only in Complete Adverse Selection), and audit-infrastructure investment (scaling how many sellers the other two instruments reach, not the per-seller incentive itself). Limited attention adds a further instrument: making silence as salient as a rating, which acts on buyers. With one hiding cost per market it lowers hiding without moving the renovation threshold, and with heterogeneous costs and seller-specific subsidies the saving is bounded; a higher weight on displayed ratings could raise the threshold, within a bound whose location the data do not pin down (Section~\ref{ss:att_policy}). % [AUTHOR REWRITE]
 A uniform national subsidy exploits none of this structure. The minimum cost-sharing requirement, a lower bound, varies widely across markets with local price levels and the verified-quality premium. The implied bill for the sellers in our listing sample who can renovate runs to \euro{}\attScCostBaseTotTwoFifty{} billion a year at the illustrative light renovation benchmark and \euro{}\attScCostBaseTotFiveHundred{} billion at the medium benchmark, between \attScCostBaseTotTwoFiftyLo{} and \attScCostBaseTotFiveHundredHi{} percent of the current annual budget of MaPrimeR\'enov'; this is a scale comparison, not a programme cost. Matching subsidies to need must condition on local price levels and the verified-quality premium, directing public expenditure to markets where the private renovation gap is largest, with audit investment scaling how many sellers any subsidy reaches. We leave heterogeneous hiding costs, certified consumption in place of rating bands, and take-up across communes to future work.

The broader lesson extends beyond energy labels. Disclosure mandates discipline strategic silence only when sellers are actually informed. When a substantial share genuinely does not know their product's quality, the mandate produces ambiguity rather than transparency. Where buyers do not attend to what a listing leaves out, silence is not read at all, and making absence visible is a complement to the other instruments. % [AUTHOR REWRITE]
 Punishing opacity more severely does little where sellers lack the information to disclose; producing that information does more. In housing energy markets, as in any market where non-disclosure mixes strategy with ignorance, audit-infrastructure investment is the instrument that expands the reach of every other tool in the regulator's toolkit.

\section{Data and Model Objects}

Our dataset includes residential property listings from French housing markets (Section~\ref{sec:data} gives the sample). For each listing $i$ in market $m$, we observe:

\begin{itemize}
\item $p$: listing price
\item $X$: vector of property characteristics (such as size, age, location, etc.)
\item $d \in \{0,1\}$: indicator of whether the Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) score is displayed in the listing
\item $\text{EPC}^{\text{listing}}$: the EPC score displayed on the listing (if $d_i = 1$)
\end{itemize}

We match these listings to data from ADEME (the French Environment and Energy Management Agency), which maintains an official registry of certified energy audits. The assessors who report these audits are third parties presumed to be honest and impartial. The match uses key characteristics registered in both databases, such as addresses, floor, item type, and area\footnote{Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the matching}. Comparing listings with the certified audits lets us assess the accuracy of the displayed EPC scores and observe in particular:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $K \in \{0,1\}$: whether a certificate for the dwelling appears in the registry at listing time
    \item $\theta \in \{0,1\}$: binary quality, with $\theta=1$ for grades A--D in the baseline cut (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} varies it); it is read from the certified grade for matched and disclosed listings, and informed non-disclosers are assigned $\theta=0$ in the baseline
\end{itemize}

The match sorts listings into four groups, reported in Table~\ref{tab:groups}. A seller with no post-July-2021 registry record ($K=0$) is classified as uninformed if the listing is silent and, under the no-falsification assumption, as a truthful discloser if it displays a score. The registry therefore gives $K$ for silent listings, the seller's information status as far as certified audits go, which buyers do not observe, and lets us study the behavior of informed and uninformed sellers separately.


\subsection*{Energy Costs from the Registry}
\label{app:energy_costs}

A dwelling of quality $\theta$ in market $m$ carries a capitalised energy cost $\zeta_{\theta,m}$, with $\zeta_{0,m}>\zeta_{1,m}>0$, measured in the units of the log price gaps, which is a first-order approximation. We take these costs from the construction of the anchor in Assumption~\ref{assum:energy_anchor}, $\zeta_{\theta,m}=\kappa\,p^E a(r,T)\,\bar E_{\theta,m}/P_m$, in which $\kappa$, $p^E$, $a(r,T)$, $\bar E_{\theta,m}$ and $P_m$ are the unit conversion, the energy price, the annuity factor, certified consumption and the price level, so that $\beta^{\rm anchor}_{1,m}=\zeta_{0,m}-\zeta_{1,m}$. Because the conversion factor $\kappa p^Ea(r,T)/P_m$ is common to both qualities, any energy cost can equivalently be expressed in kWh$_{\rm ep}$/m$^2$/yr. A silent dwelling of quality $\theta$ is assumed to cost the same to heat as a rated one.

Converting the consumption gap into euros requires three calibrated inputs: an energy price of about \euro0.21 per kWh (CRE/Eurostat, 2022--2024 average, blended across the national heating-vector mix), a discount rate of $3.75\%$ (the contemporary French mortgage rate), and a ten-year horizon. The horizon is shorter than full capitalisation of the energy stream would justify, and deliberately so. It matches the typical holding period, and the energy-efficiency-gap literature finds buyers behaving in revealed preference as though they discount durable energy attributes over exactly such truncated horizons \citep{allcott_is_2012}. The consumption averages pool disclosed and withheld matched listings, since restricting them to disclosed listings would condition on the seller's own hiding decision and reintroduce the selection the anchor exists to avoid. Assumption~\ref{assum:energy_anchor} and Equation~\eqref{eq:energy_anchor} in Appendix~\ref{app:rho_identification} give the construction in full.

The split of buyers into EPC-sensitive and EPC-insensitive types reflects how much of the ongoing energy cost a buyer will bear. Primary-residence buyers in northern France with long heating seasons, and investor-buyers who face the impending F/G rental ban under the \textit{loi Climat et R\'{e}silience}, have strong incentives to incorporate EPC scores into their bids. A vacation-home buyer who occupies the property a few weeks a year, or a buyer shopping in a mild climate, has much less riding on it and may rationally discount energy performance. This heterogeneity determines $\rho$, the share of the local buyer pool that is EPC-sensitive. Prices and the energy anchor identify only $\delta(\rho\omega_1)$, so the estimate of $\rho$ in Section~\ref{sec:identification} is the post-reform $\rho$ when $\omega_1=1$ and under Assumption~\ref{assum:no_pre_pricing}, and it is not clipped to $[0,1]$.

\subsection*{Independence of Information and Quality}
\label{app:independence}

Independence of information status and quality, $K\perp\theta$, is most plausible for transactions driven by exogenous life events such as succession following a death, court-ordered liquidation or divorce settlement, in which legal proceedings impose binding timing constraints that preclude any quality-contingent decision to obtain a certified EPC. In such cases the seller's information status is determined by administrative capacity and procedural deadlines rather than by the energy performance of the dwelling. Independence among forced sales does not by itself deliver it in the whole population, because sellers may select into audits by quality. Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} reports estimates that relax the treatment of high-quality hiding.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection*{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:belief_monotone} (Belief Monotonicity)}

\begin{proof}
Raising $\sigma_0$ adds low-quality hiders to the denominator of~\eqref{eq:belief_general} and leaves the numerator unchanged, so $e$ falls. Raising $\sigma_1$ adds the same term $\pi\bar\theta$ to numerator and denominator, and since $e<1$ the numerator effect dominates, so $e$ rises. The strict decrease in $\sigma_0$ is what makes the equilibrium of the full-attention benchmark (Appendix~\ref{sec:equilibrium}) unique: as more informed low-quality sellers hide, buyer beliefs worsen, which attenuates the benefit of hiding and restores incentive compatibility.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:premium} (Disclosure Incentives and Seller Sorting)}

\begin{proof}
At $\psi=\omega_1=1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:att_prices} gives the price of a silent listing relative to a listing that displays quality $\theta$ as $\rho\beta_1[e(\sigma_0^*)-\theta]$, with $\rho$ in place of $\rho\omega_1$. A seller who is informed ($K=1$) and of quality $\theta$ pays the hiding cost $c_0+\theta c_1$ if she stays silent, so she strictly prefers disclosure if and only if $\rho\beta_1[e(\sigma_0^*)-\theta]<c_0+\theta c_1$. With $c_0=c_1=0$ this is $\theta>e(\sigma_0^*)$. For $\theta=1$ it holds whenever $e(\sigma_0^*)<1$, since $\rho\beta_1>0$, and for $\theta=0$ it fails whenever $e(\sigma_0^*)>0$.
\end{proof}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Corollary \ref{cor:hq_disclose} (High-Quality Sellers Always Disclose)}

\begin{proof}
For $\theta=1$ the comparison in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:premium} reads $\rho\beta_1[e-1]<c_0+c_1$, whose left-hand side is negative whenever $e<1$. The belief $e(\sigma_0,\sigma_1)$ in~\eqref{eq:belief_general} is strictly below one for $\pi<1$ and $\bar\theta<1$, because the pool then contains uninformed low-quality sellers whatever $(\sigma_0,\sigma_1)$ is. So disclosure strictly dominates silence for an informed high-quality seller in any equilibrium, and $\sigma_1^*=0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:equilibrium} (Equilibrium Existence and Uniqueness)}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose}, $\sigma_1^* = 0$ in any equilibrium. The strategic problem reduces to characterising $\sigma_0^* \in [0,1]$, the hiding probability of informed low-quality sellers. The incentive to hide is
\[
    \Phi(\sigma_0) \;\equiv\; \Delta\!\left(e(\sigma_0)\right) - c_0
    \;=\; \frac{\rho\,\beta_1\,(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0 + (1-\pi)} - c_0,
\]
where $e(\sigma_0)$ is the equilibrium pool belief (Equation~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}). Since $e(\sigma_0)$ is strictly decreasing in $\sigma_0$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:belief_monotone}) and $\Delta$ is strictly increasing in $e$, $\Phi$ is \emph{strictly decreasing} in $\sigma_0$, with boundary values
\[
    \Phi(0) = \Delta(\bar{\theta}) - c_0, \qquad
    \Phi(1) = \Delta\!\left(\tfrac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}\right) - c_0.
\]

\medskip
\textbf{Case 1: Cost-Deterred Hiding.} Suppose $c_0 > \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, i.e., $\Phi(0) < 0$.

Since $\Phi$ is strictly decreasing, $\Phi(\sigma_0) \leq \Phi(0) < 0$ for all $\sigma_0 \in [0,1]$. Hiding is strictly unprofitable at every hiding rate, including the most favourable case $\sigma_0 = 0$. Disclosure strictly dominates for every informed low-quality seller, so the unique equilibrium is $\sigma_0^* = 0$ with equilibrium belief $e^* = \bar{\theta}$.

\textit{Interpretation.} All informed low-quality sellers disclose; the non-disclosing pool consists exclusively of uninformed sellers. Buyers correctly infer quality $\bar{\theta}$ from non-disclosure. Unlike the classical Grossman--Milgrom outcome, silence is uninformative rather than damning: non-disclosure carries no stigma because it reveals nothing beyond the seller's lack of a certified audit.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 2: Complete Adverse Selection.} Suppose $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, i.e., $\Phi(1) > 0$.

Since $\Phi$ is strictly decreasing, $\Phi(\sigma_0) \geq \Phi(1) > 0$ for all $\sigma_0 \in [0,1]$. Hiding is strictly profitable at every hiding rate, including the least favourable case $\sigma_0 = 1$. Hiding is therefore strictly dominant for every informed low-quality seller regardless of the strategies of others, so the unique equilibrium is $\sigma_0^* = 1$ with equilibrium belief $e^* = (1-\pi)\bar{\theta}/(1-\pi\bar{\theta})$.

\textit{Interpretation.} All informed low-quality sellers pool with uninformed sellers. The non-disclosure premium $\rho\cdot\beta_1\cdot e(1)$ is the minimum attainable across all regimes (Equation~\eqref{eq:premium}).

\medskip
\textbf{Case 3: Partial Pooling.} Suppose $\Delta(e(1)) \leq c_0 \leq \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, i.e., $\Phi(1) \leq 0 \leq \Phi(0)$.

$\Phi$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on $[0,1]$, with $\Phi(0) \geq 0$ and $\Phi(1) \leq 0$. By the intermediate value theorem, there exists $\sigma_0^* \in [0,1]$ with $\Phi(\sigma_0^*) = 0$; strict monotonicity of $\Phi$ implies uniqueness. When the boundary inequalities hold strictly, $\sigma_0^*$ lies in the open interval $(0,1)$; at the boundaries, $\sigma_0^* \in \{0,1\}$ and the regime collapses to Case 1 or Case 2.

At $\sigma_0^*$, informed low-quality sellers are exactly indifferent between hiding and disclosing; mixing at rate $\sigma_0^*$ is self-confirming through the belief function. This regime generates the closed-form hiding rate.

\medskip
\textbf{Uniqueness and exhaustiveness.} In Cases 1 and 2, uniqueness follows from strict dominance: $\Phi$ has constant sign on $[0,1]$ so no other $\sigma_0$ is consistent with optimality. In Case 3, uniqueness follows from the unique zero of $\Phi$. The three conditions $c_0 > \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, and $\Delta(e(1)) \leq c_0 \leq \Delta(\bar{\theta})$ partition $\mathbb{R}_{+}$ since $\Delta(\bar{\theta}) \geq \Delta(e(1))$ (as $e(0) = \bar{\theta} \geq e(1)$ and $\Delta$ is increasing). Equilibrium exists and is unique for all parameter values.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Non-Disclosure Premium Characterisation \eqref{eq:premium}}

\begin{proof}
In the full-attention case the equilibrium price of a listing equals its mean valuation~\eqref{eq:mean_valuation} at $\omega_0=\omega_1=1$. For a low-quality seller who discloses ($d=1$, $\theta=0$), sensitive buyers subtract $\zeta_0$ and insensitive buyers subtract $\zeta^{\rm ins}$, so $p_1^*=\bar V(X)-(1-\rho)\,\zeta^{\rm ins}-\rho\,\zeta_0$. For a non-disclosing listing, sensitive buyers subtract $\bar\zeta^{\rm sil}=e(\sigma_0^*)\,\zeta_1+(1-e(\sigma_0^*))\,\zeta_0$ and insensitive buyers again subtract $\zeta^{\rm ins}$, so $p_0^*=\bar V(X)-(1-\rho)\,\zeta^{\rm ins}-\rho\,\bar\zeta^{\rm sil}$. Subtracting, $p_0^*-p_1^*=\rho\,(\zeta_0-\bar\zeta^{\rm sil})=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$, so the non-disclosure premium is:
\begin{equation}
    p_0^*-p_1^* = \rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*). \label{eq:premium_derived}
\end{equation}
The three cases of~\eqref{eq:premium} follow by substituting the equilibrium $\sigma_0^*$ from each regime of Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium} into~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 1: Cost-Deterred Hiding ($\sigma_0^*=0$).}
Evaluating~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=0$:
\[
    e(0) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{(1-\pi)} = \bar{\theta}.
\]
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}: $p_0^*-p_1^* = \rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 2: Complete Adverse Selection ($\sigma_0^*=1$).}
With $\sigma_1^*=0$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:hq_disclose}, evaluating~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=1$:
\[
    e(1) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})+(1-\pi)} = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}.
\]
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}: $p_0^*-p_1^* = \rho\,\beta_1\,\dfrac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}}$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 3: Partial Pooling ($\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$).}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}, $\sigma_0^*$ solves the indifference condition $\Delta(e(\sigma_0^*))=c_0$, i.e., $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)=c_0$. But the left-hand side is $p_0^*-p_1^*$ by~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}, so $p_0^*-p_1^*=c_0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Ordering.}
Since $e$ is strictly decreasing by Lemma~\ref{lem:belief_monotone}, we have $e(1)<e(\sigma_0^*)<e(0)$ for all $\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$, hence $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1)<c_0<\rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}$, confirming the strict ordering stated after~\eqref{eq:premium}.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:comparative_statics} (Comparative statics of the non-disclosure premium)}

\begin{proof}
From~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}, the non-disclosure premium is $\Delta\equiv p_0^*-p_1^*=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$, where the belief $e(\sigma_0)$ is given by~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}. The equilibrium hiding rate $\sigma_0^*\in[0,1]$ is determined by the regime characterised in Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}. We derive $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi$ and $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho$ in each of the three regimes, in turn.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 1: Cost-Deterred Hiding ($\sigma_0^*=0$).}
No informed low-quality seller hides, so the non-disclosing pool consists entirely of uninformed sellers. From~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=0$:
\begin{equation}
    e(0) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{(1-\pi)} = \bar{\theta},
\end{equation}
which is independent of $\pi$. The premium is therefore:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta = \rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}.
\end{equation}
Since $\bar{\theta}$ is a structural parameter, $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi=0$ and $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho=\beta_1\bar{\theta}>0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 2: Complete Adverse Selection ($\sigma_0^*=1$).}
All informed low-quality sellers hide. From~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified} at $\sigma_0=1$:
\begin{equation}
    e(1) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{\pi(1-\bar{\theta})+(1-\pi)} = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1-\pi\bar{\theta}},
\end{equation}
so $\Delta=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1)$. Differentiating with respect to $\pi$ by the quotient rule:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial e(1)}{\partial\pi}
        = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\pi\bar{\theta})-(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}\cdot(-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2}
        = \frac{\bar{\theta}(\bar{\theta}-1)}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2}
        = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2} < 0,
\end{equation}
hence $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi = \rho\,\beta_1\cdot\partial e(1)/\partial\pi < 0$.
Differentiating with respect to $\rho$ (with $e(1)$ determined solely by $\pi$ and $\bar\theta$):
$\partial\Delta/\partial\rho=\beta_1\,e(1)>0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Case 3: Partial Pooling ($\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$).}
The interior equilibrium satisfies $c_0=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$, so the premium is pinned at the hiding cost:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta = c_0.
\end{equation}
Changes in $\pi$ or $\rho$ induce compensating adjustments in $\sigma_0^*$ that restore the indifference condition, leaving the premium invariant: $\partial\Delta/\partial\pi=0$ and $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho=0$.

To verify: from~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}, $\partial e(\sigma_0)/\partial\pi = -\bar{\theta}(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0/[\pi(1-\bar{\theta})\sigma_0+(1-\pi)]^2 < 0$ for $\sigma_0>0$. Total differentiation of $c_0=\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*(\pi),\pi)$ with respect to $\pi$ gives:
\begin{equation}
    0 = \rho\,\beta_1\left(\frac{\partial e}{\partial\sigma_0}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_0^*}{\mathrm{d}\pi} + \frac{\partial e}{\partial\pi}\right),
\end{equation}
so $\mathrm{d}\sigma_0^*/\mathrm{d}\pi = -(\partial e/\partial\pi)/(\partial e/\partial\sigma_0) < 0$: as $\pi$ rises, informed sellers populate the non-disclosing pool more heavily, so fewer low-quality sellers need to hide to sustain the same buyer belief, and $\sigma_0^*$ falls.

\medskip
\textbf{Summary.} The non-disclosure premium is \emph{weakly} decreasing in $\pi$, strictly only in the Complete Adverse Selection regime, and \emph{weakly} increasing in $\rho$, strictly in Cases~1 and~2 and flat in Case~3. In Case~1, the premium $\rho\beta_1\bar{\theta}$ is determined by the population quality $\bar{\theta}$ of the uninformed pool: it does not depend on $\pi$ and rises with $\rho$. In Case~3, the premium is pinned at the primitive $c_0$ by the indifference condition; it is the hiding rate $\sigma_0^*$, not the premium, that absorbs variation in $\pi$ and $\rho$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:rho} (Effect of $\rho$ on the non-disclosure premium)}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Part~(i).}
From~\eqref{eq:premium_derived}, the non-disclosure premium is $\Delta = \rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*)$. We differentiate with respect to $\rho$ in each regime, holding the regime-specific equilibrium $\sigma_0^*$ fixed where appropriate.

\medskip
\textit{Cost-Deterred Hiding ($\sigma_0^* = 0$).} Here $e(0) = \bar{\theta}$ by~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}, independent of $\rho$. Therefore $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho = \beta_1\bar{\theta} > 0$.

\textit{Complete Adverse Selection ($\sigma_0^* = 1$).} Here $e(1) = (1-\pi)\bar{\theta}/(1-\pi\bar{\theta})$, also independent of $\rho$. Therefore $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho = \beta_1\,e(1) > 0$.

\textit{Partial Pooling ($\sigma_0^*\in(0,1)$).} The indifference condition $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(\sigma_0^*) = c_0$ pins $\Delta = c_0$. Changes in $\rho$ induce compensating adjustments in $\sigma_0^*$, as established in Proposition~\ref{prop:comparative_statics}(ii): $\partial\sigma_0^*/\partial\rho > 0$. The premium satisfies $\partial\Delta/\partial\rho = 0$.

\medskip
\textbf{Part~(ii).}
From Proposition~\ref{prop:equilibrium}, the \emph{Partial Pooling} regime requires $\Delta(e(1)) \leq c_0 \leq \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, i.e., $\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1) \leq c_0 \leq \rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}$. Differentiating both bounds with respect to $\rho$: $\partial[\rho\,\beta_1\,e(1)]/\partial\rho = \beta_1\,e(1) > 0$ and $\partial[\rho\,\beta_1\,\bar{\theta}]/\partial\rho = \beta_1\,\bar{\theta} > 0$. Both boundaries increase with $\rho$. The width of the partial-pooling interval is $\rho\,\beta_1\,[\bar{\theta} - e(1)]$, which is increasing in $\rho$ since $\bar{\theta} > e(1)$ whenever $\pi > 0$. The band therefore shifts up and widens as $\rho$ rises.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:pi} (Equilibrium effect of $\pi$)}

\begin{proof}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:belief_simplified}, the equilibrium belief under Complete Adverse Selection is:
\begin{equation}
    e(1) = \frac{(1-\pi)\bar{\theta}}{1 - \pi\bar{\theta}}
\end{equation}

Taking the derivative:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial e(1)}{\partial \pi} = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\pi\bar{\theta}) - (1-\pi)\bar{\theta}(-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2} = \frac{-\bar{\theta}(1-\bar{\theta})}{(1-\pi\bar{\theta})^2} < 0
\end{equation}

As $\pi$ increases, $e(1)$ falls: the pool quality under Complete Adverse Selection deteriorates because fewer uninformed sellers are available to provide cover.

Since $\Delta(e)$ is increasing in $e$, $\Delta(e(1))$ falls, so the condition for Complete Adverse Selection, $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, becomes harder to satisfy: a market with $c_0<\Delta(e(1))$ at some $\pi$ is in Partial Pooling at a larger $\pi$. The upper boundary $\Delta(\bar\theta)=\rho\beta_1\bar\theta$ does not involve $\pi$, because $e(0)=\bar\theta$ when the pool contains only uninformed sellers; no change in $\pi$ therefore moves a market from Partial Pooling into Cost-Deterred Hiding. Within Partial Pooling, $e^*=c_0/(\rho\beta_1)$ is fixed and the hiding rate is $\sigma_0^*=\frac{1-\pi}{\pi(1-\bar\theta)}\big(\bar\theta/e^*-1\big)$, which falls with $\pi$ and tends to $0$ as $\pi\to1$.
\end{proof}

When most sellers are informed ($\pi$ high), uninformed sellers are rare and the cover they provide thins. In Complete Adverse Selection the pool belief falls with $\pi$, and with it the premium. In Partial Pooling the belief is pinned at $e^*=c_0/(\rho\beta_1)$ and does not move, so fewer informed low-quality sellers are needed to sustain it, and the hiding rate falls toward zero.

When most buyers care about quality ($\rho$ high), the stakes of disclosure are high. Low-quality sellers have much to lose from revealing their type. In Cost-Deterred Hiding and Complete Adverse Selection the premium rises with $\rho$; in Partial Pooling it stays at $c_0$, and by Corollary~\ref{cor:rho}(ii) the partial-pooling interval itself shifts up and widens with $\rho$.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:c0} (Equilibrium effect of $c_0$: equilibrium moves toward Cost-Deterred Hiding)}

\begin{proof}
An increase in $c_0$ shifts the function $\Phi(\sigma_0) = \Delta(e(\sigma_0)) - c_0$ downward. The boundaries become:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \Phi(0) &= \Delta(\bar{\theta}) - c_0 \quad \text{(decreases)} \\
        \Phi(1) &= \Delta(e(1)) - c_0 \quad \text{(decreases)}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The condition for Cost-Deterred Hiding, $c_0 > \Delta(\bar{\theta})$, becomes easier to satisfy. The condition for Complete Adverse Selection, $c_0 < \Delta(e(1))$, becomes harder to satisfy.
\end{proof}

Higher hiding costs make concealment less attractive. Regulatory enforcement, administrative burden, or legal exposure that raise $c_0$ push the market toward disclosure.
