## A. Short (165 words)

Standard disclosure theory predicts that sellers of high-quality products always disclose, so buyers read silence as the lowest quality. Yet after July 2021, when disclosed energy ratings became legally opposable in France, unrated listings gained about 7% (apartments) and 3.5% (houses) on disclosed inefficient ones. Matching listings from SeLoger, a major French housing platform, to the ADEME registry of certified energy audits shows whether each seller holds a certificate, which buyers cannot see. In a Dye-like disclosure game with limited attention, uninformed sellers who shelter hiders, buyers who differ in their sensitivity to energy efficiency, and limited attention to silence make silence profitable. Silence does not raise the subsidy a seller needs to renovate: the subsidy must cover her cost net of the rating premium. For a light renovation costing EUR 250 per square metre, the state must cover at least 53% of the cost on average in apartment markets that need support (56% in house markets). Needs vary widely, so a uniform subsidy misfires.

## B. Medium (206 words)

Standard disclosure theory predicts that sellers of high-quality products always disclose, so buyers read silence as the lowest quality and unrated products should sell at or below disclosed poor ones. Yet after July 2021, when disclosed energy ratings became legally opposable in France, the price gap between unrated and disclosed inefficient listings widened by about 7% (apartments) and 3.5% (houses) on average across markets. We match listings from SeLoger, a major French housing platform, to the ADEME national registry of certified energy audits, which shows whether the seller holds a certificate, something no buyer can see, and build a Dye-like disclosure game with limited attention that we extend to renovation. Uninformed sellers shelter those who hide, buyers differ in how sensitive they are to energy efficiency, and they also pay limited attention to silence. Together, these components make silence profitable. Silence does not raise the subsidy a seller needs: she renovates once the subsidy covers her cost net of the rating premium. For a light renovation costing EUR 250 per square metre, the state must cover at least 53% of the cost on average in apartment markets that need support (56% in house markets). Needs vary widely across markets, so a uniform subsidy misfires in France.

## Caveats common to both variants
- Neither states that the subsidy is a lower bound exact only in the regime the calibration imposes (the
  data fit that regime in 0% of apartment and 11% of house markets). The body states it (Section 5.3).
- "Limited attention to silence" is stated for both segments, as in the current abstract; the result is
  that full attention is rejected in apartments and not in houses (Section 4.5).
- Cost shares are lower bounds at an illustrative benchmark; selection of markets pushes them down.
