# Sentence-Level Conventions

## Register

**Flat declaratives for documented facts; hedged verbs for mechanisms.** The corpus is
sharply asymmetric about this, and the asymmetry is what makes it credible. Facts you
measured are stated without qualification. Interpretations are hedged, every time:
*appear to*, *plausibly*, *likely*, *arguably*, *suggest*, *may*.

> Fact: "about 75 percent of companies disclose in the first supplement."
> Mechanism: "these circumstances make it plausible that some companies did not fully reason
> through the cognitively demanding logic."

Never hedge a number. Never assert a mechanism.

**"We" throughout**, including in single-authored work.

**Present tense for what the paper does** ("we examine," "we document," "we estimate"); past
tense for events in the setting.

## Connectives that carry the argument

The corpus uses a small, repeated set as sentence and paragraph openers: *Accordingly*,
*Notably*, *Strikingly*, *Collectively*, *In this vein*, *For one … For another*, *First …
Second … Lastly*, *Yet*.

*Yet* is the workhorse for setting up tension — it opens the second sentence of the paper.

## Enumeration

Enumerate constantly, in prose rather than bullet lists. Findings, assumptions, institutional
features, and justifications all get *First, … Second, … Third, …*. Bulleted lists are rare in
the body text of these papers; the enumeration lives inside paragraphs.

## The key theoretical term gets quotation marks

The corpus writes "unravelling" in quotation marks on *every* use, throughout the paper. This
signals that the term names a specific formal result rather than a loose idea. Pick your
paper's central theoretical term and treat it the same way consistently.

## Citation clusters

Dense parenthetical clusters, almost always opened with *e.g.*:

> "(e.g., Dye, 1985; Jung and Kwon, 1988)"

Two to four references per cluster is normal. A claim about a literature always carries one.

## Footnotes carry the caveats

Substantive footnotes, often long — alternative explanations, institutional qualifications,
data quirks, ruled-out mechanisms. The corpus routinely runs footnotes of 100+ words. This is
how the main text stays clean while every referee objection still gets an answer somewhere.

Use footnotes for: why an alternative explanation fails, why a variable is measured this way,
what a data limitation prevents, and what a modelling omission would and would not change.

## Magnitudes always come with a scale

Never report a coefficient without something to compare it to. The corpus reports a
value-relevance result as "explaining a remarkable 58% of the cross-section of investors'
stock price valuations" — the scaling reference is what makes the number mean something.

In prose, *percent* is generally spelled out for headline figures ("about 75 percent"); the
symbol appears in parentheticals, tables, and precise figures. Be internally consistent.

## Nouns are fixed

Choose one noun for each actor and never vary it for stylistic relief. The corpus says
"companies" and "investors," thousands of times, never alternating to "firms" or
"shareholders." Synonym variation reads as imprecision in this literature — the reader
wonders whether a different set is meant.

## Outside corroboration is quoted at length

When a contemporary source, practitioner, or regulator independently reaches your
conclusion, quote it as a display block. The corpus block-quotes a trade publication from
1894 that anticipates its own finding. This is disproportionately persuasive and costs
little.

## Avoid

- Rhetorical questions
- "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," and similar reader-instruction adverbs (*Strikingly* and
  *Notably* are used, but attached to specific results, not as filler)
- Roadmap paragraphs
- Notation in the introduction or abstract
- Hedging a measured fact, or asserting an unmeasured mechanism
- Synonym variation on core nouns
- Displayed mathematics with no economic gloss
