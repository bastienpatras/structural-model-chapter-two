\section{Unraveling with Possibly Uninformed Sellers}
\label{sec:unraveling}

\subsection{The Unraveling Benchmark}

Asymmetric information about product quality can prevent trade from happening at the right
prices. If buyers cannot verify quality before purchasing, they bid no more than the value of
what they expect to receive on average. Sellers of genuinely good products are then underpaid,
and may withdraw from the market altogether, leaving buyers facing a pool of worse quality than
they had hoped for \citep{akerlof_lemons_1970}. Buyers pay for
uncertainty they would rather not bear, and the best sellers cannot capture the value of what
they own.

Disclosure appears to resolve this problem without any regulatory intervention. Suppose quality
is verifiable, so that a seller who makes a claim about it can be held to that claim. A seller
of the highest-quality product will disclose, because staying silent means being valued at the
average of the pool rather than at what the product is actually worth. Buyers, anticipating
this, understand that any seller who remains silent is not of the highest quality, and revise
downward what they will pay for silence. That revision gives the second-highest-quality seller
the same incentive, and disclosing becomes worthwhile for them too. The argument repeats, and each
round of disclosure worsens the expected quality of the remaining silent pool, which strengthens
the incentive for the best of those who remain to separate themselves. The process continues
until all sellers but possibly the very worst have disclosed. This is the unraveling result of
\citet{grossman_informational_1981} and \citet{milgrom_good_1981}. Even sellers with bad news
disclose, because they anticipate that silence will be read as worse news still. If it holds,
market forces alone deliver near-complete disclosure and a mandate is redundant.

The argument rests on four assumptions. First, buyers know
that sellers possess private information about quality; if a seller may simply not know, silence
no longer implies unwillingness to reveal. Second, disclosure is costless, or at least cheap
relative to the price consequences of silence; a large literature examines how disclosure costs
truncate the unraveling process and leave a residual pool of non-disclosers
\citep{verrecchia_1983, jovanovic_1982}. Third, disclosure is credible, so that a claim about
quality cannot be fabricated; without verifiability, disclosure degenerates into cheap talk and
the unraveling logic never starts \citep{crawford_sobel_1982, stocken_credibility_2000}. Fourth, buyers are homogeneous in how much they value the
disclosed attribute, so that the stakes of disclosure are the same as they would be with a single representative
buyer.

The first three assumptions have received sustained attention; the fourth has not, although
it sets the stakes of the entire mechanism. A seller discloses to avoid the revenue
lost when buyers price silence pessimistically, and that loss is proportional to the share of
buyers who care about the attribute. Whether unraveling describes any
particular market is therefore an empirical question, and answering it requires knowing which of
these assumptions fails there.

\subsection{Two Departures and How They Work Together}

Two of the four assumptions are implausible in markets where quality is certified by a
third party and buyers differ in their exposure to the certified attribute.

The first departure concerns the assumption that all sellers hold private information. When some sellers genuinely lack private
information, silence pools those who \emph{will not} disclose with those who \emph{cannot}.
\citet{dye_disclosure_1985} showed that this possibility breaks the buyer inference on which
unraveling depends, because a rational buyer confronted with silence must form a posterior over two
distinct populations, and how pessimistic that posterior should be depends on their relative
masses rather than on the act of silence itself. \citet{jung_kwon_1988} characterised the
resulting equilibria and showed that partial pooling, in which some informed sellers of poor
quality withhold while the uninformed are silent by necessity, is generically stable. Disclosure
stops somewhere in the interior instead of unraveling to the bottom.

The second departure concerns the assumption that buyers are homogeneous. If only a fraction of buyers price the disclosed
attribute, both the reward to disclosing it and the penalty for withholding it are diluted, because
part of the demand side is indifferent to what was withheld. \citet{fishman_mandatory_2003} show formally that mandated
disclosure can be ineffective, and even welfare-reducing, when buyers differ in their capacity to
use disclosed information. The composition of demand thus sets the size of the stakes, the reward to a good rating and the
gain from hiding a poor one, and so how they compare with any cost of hiding.

Buyer homogeneity has a second margin. A buyer who prices the attribute when it is displayed may not think about it when it is missing. If she does not, she prices a silent listing without forming a belief about its quality, as though energy were not part of the transaction \citep{hirshleifer_limited_2003, dellavigna_investor_2009}. The adverse inference from silence is then weaker than any belief could make it, and silence can sell above a verified efficient rating, which no belief about the silent pool can produce. Section~\ref{ss:model_motivation} takes up this margin.

The two departures work together: the first defeats unraveling and the second sets the stakes. A larger mass of sellers who cannot
disclose dilutes the adverse inference buyers draw from silence, which raises the expected quality
of the silent pool and makes withholding more attractive to informed sellers of poor quality.
Those sellers then join the pool, and while their entry worsens its true average quality, buyers
cannot separate them from the involuntarily silent. Meanwhile, a demand side in which many buyers
do not price the attribute scales down both the reward to a good rating and the gain from hiding a
poor one, so whether hiding pays depends on how large those stakes are relative to its cost. Put together,
the consequence goes beyond persistence of non-disclosure. Silence can command a
\emph{premium} over the worst disclosed quality, since the silent pool contains a substantial
mass of sellers whose silence carries no adverse information at all. The sellers who cannot
disclose do not intend to shelter anyone, but their involuntary presence makes silence ambiguous,
and that ambiguity makes concealment privately rational whenever the stakes exceed its cost.

Because the two departures operate on different primitives, they map out a space of possible
market outcomes rather than a single prediction. Figure~\ref{fig:equilibrium_outcomes} organises
that space along two dimensions, the share of sellers who hold verifiable information and whether
buyers price the attribute at all. Full unraveling requires every seller to be informed and some
buyers to price the attribute, and if no buyer prices it, disclosure is irrelevant. Where some
sellers are uninformed and some buyers price the attribute, silence survives in equilibrium and can
be rewarded rather than punished, with the share of buyers who price it setting the stakes against
the cost of hiding. Within that cell a third primitive matters, the attention buyers pay to what a silent listing leaves out.

The taxonomy implies that the informational content of silence is a market-level object rather than a
universal one, so two markets facing the same disclosure mandate can sit in different cells and sustain
opposite price patterns, and that the two primitives are separately movable by policy. Investment in
certification infrastructure raises the share of sellers holding verifiable information, while the
share of buyers who price the attribute responds to the legal weight the certificate carries and
to the cost of the attribute itself. Which cell a given market occupies, however, cannot be
settled by theory. It requires measuring the composition of the silent pool and the attention
buyers pay to silence, which is what the data described in the next section allow us to do.
